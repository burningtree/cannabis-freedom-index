Under the French mandate, growing cannabis and making and trading hashish were prohibited in
[Lebanon](/country/lb/) and Syria, as in every mandated territory. The ban is usually dated to 1926.
It did not stop the Bekaa valley from becoming one of the main sources of hashish in the region.

## A new crop for an old market

Hashish was not an old Lebanese export. Thomas Russell, the head of Egypt's narcotics bureau, told the
League of Nations that when he arrived in Egypt in 1902 all of its hashish came from Greece. Around
[1907](/history/lebanon-hashish-growing-begins-1907/), he wrote, some people began growing it as an
experiment in the Lebanon district of Syria. When Greece curbed its crop in the 1920s, Lebanese growing
"increased enormously", and nearly all of it went to Egypt, smuggled across Palestine.

## A ban the rulers did not enforce

The historian Haggai Ram shows how little the ban meant in practice. France claimed that its measures
went further than the 1925 Geneva convention, which required only control. But its colonial budget
was shrinking, its police were thin on the border, and corruption reached most of the administration.
Many French officials and Lebanese politicians owned the land the cannabis grew on.

In 1929 the French admitted that for some districts hemp was an income that "could not easily be
replaced in a single season". In the Baalbek district, a Lebanese minister reported, large landowners
refused to destroy their crops. Russell put it bluntly: peasants grew the crop on the orders of
notables who sat in the local governments.

## Geneva shames Paris

In the 1930s Russell took his complaint to the League and urged France to make possessing hashish an
offence in Syria, since growing, making and carrying it already were. France responded with a new
decree, a narcotics squad in the Sûreté Générale and more field destruction. Egypt noticed fewer
Syrian arrivals, though Turkish hashish soon replaced them. By 1939 Russell called the planting by
Lebanon's governing classes "a national revolt against authority". Ram quotes a later judgement that
most enforcement campaigns were theatre, meant to appease Egypt.

## After independence

The pattern outlived the mandate. Independent Lebanon banned growing again in 1946, and a British
envoy called the hashish trade of the late 1940s "an open scandal". Plans to wean Bekaa farmers off the
crop came and went. In [2020](/history/lebanon-medical-cultivation-2020/) Lebanon became the first Arab
country to allow growing cannabis, for medicine and industry.
