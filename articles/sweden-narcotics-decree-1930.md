Sweden made unauthorised cannabis possession punishable by a fine in 1930.
It also prohibited importing, exporting, making, possessing or offering hashish
for sale. Cannabis was joining a system of narcotics controls shaped by
international drug agreements and earlier restrictions on dangerous substances.

## Cannabis sellers had faced controls before

Sweden had already extended its poison-law penalties to
[unauthorised cannabis sales and supply in 1876](/history/sweden-indian-hemp-sales-control-1876/).
The drug appeared as Indian hemp in rules that also dealt with arsenic and
other poisons. People supplying cannabis therefore faced legal restrictions
long before the later narcotics laws.

The government then developed a separate system for narcotics. After Sweden
joined the Hague drug convention, rules issued in 1923 controlled drugs
including opium, morphine and cocaine. Different substances were subject to
different restrictions, with fines for breaches. The next international
agreement widened the range of drugs governments were expected to control.

## The Geneva agreement brought cannabis into the list

The [1925 Geneva Convention](/history/geneva-opium-convention-1925/) added
cannabis and coca leaves to international controls. Sweden followed with
its 1930 narcotics decree, bringing those substances and further opium
derivatives under the special rules.

Olle Hoflund's history traces this sequence from international agreement to
domestic regulation. The change placed cannabis alongside drugs already
subject to controls on their trade, extending Sweden's approach beyond its
earlier poison-sales rules.

## Holding cannabis became an offence

The new possession rule meant someone holding narcotics without legal authority
could be fined. For cannabis users, that brought the law directly to possession,
alongside the restrictions already affecting suppliers.

Hashish, the resin obtained from cannabis, faced an especially broad
prohibition. The rules covered its movement across the border, manufacture,
possession and offering it for sale. A person could therefore come within those
prohibitions at several stages, from bringing resin into Sweden to holding it
or offering it to another buyer.

## Prison followed three years later

Sweden [tightened its narcotics rules again in 1933](/history/sweden-aggravated-drug-offences-prison-1933/),
introducing imprisonment for offences committed in aggravating circumstances.
The change added the prospect of custody to a system that had used fines.

Those successive decisions connected the control of suppliers to punishment
of possession. They became part of the earlier legal system that Sweden
later replaced with its
[1968 narcotics penal law](/history/sweden-narcotics-penal-act-1968/), as
[Swedish cannabis policy](/country/se/) moved toward heavier penalties.
