In 1868 Egypt forbade growing, using and importing cannabis. It was one of the first
attempts by a modern state to ban the drug outright, and the start of a campaign Egypt
would eventually take to the League of Nations.

## The measure

Egypt was then still under the nominal rule of the Ottoman sultan. The decree was the
first of a series: a tax on imports in [1874](/history/egypt-import-tax-1874/), an order
to destroy all hashish in [1877](/history/egypt-destruction-order-1877/), a new law in
[1879](/history/egypt-import-ban-1879/) and a criminal offence of cultivation in
[1884](/history/egypt-cultivation-ban-1884/).

## Who it was aimed at

Hashish in Egypt was the habit of the poor, in the towns and in the countryside. In
parts of the Arab world it had long been looked down on as the vice of Sufis and of the
lower orders. The historian Liat Kozma reads the Egyptian campaign as a mixture of
public health and religious concern, anxiety about the country's image abroad, and a
centralising state's wish to civilise its lower classes.

## Why it failed

Hashish was cheap, easily grown, and easily smuggled in from Greece. The rules exempted
foreigners. The bans had to be reissued in 1891 and 1894, which is a measure of how
little they had achieved, and use among the poor carried on.

## Where it led

Unable to stop the supply, Egypt pressed its neighbours to restrict growing, and in
[1924](/history/geneva-egypt-proposal-1924/) its delegate asked the world to do the
same. The [Geneva convention](/history/geneva-opium-convention-1925/) that followed put
cannabis under international control for the first time.
