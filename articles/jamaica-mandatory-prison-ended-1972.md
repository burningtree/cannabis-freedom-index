On 11 August 1972 Jamaica's parliament abolished mandatory prison sentences for growing, selling and
possessing ganja. Four days later about 200 prisoners were released. The change rested in part on a
study of Jamaican ganja smokers that a government minister had handed to the island's main newspaper.

## Thirty years of compulsory prison

Since [1941](/history/jamaica-mandatory-prison-1941/) a conviction for ganja had meant prison, whatever
the judge thought. In [1964](/history/jamaica-mandatory-minimum-1964/) the minimum for a first possession
offence rose to eighteen months. Rubin and Comitas concluded that six decades of control had failed: growing
and smoking had increased, and the methods used to enforce the law had damaged the administration of
justice. No government had ever collected systematic information on who used ganja or with what effects.

## The study goes public

That information came from a field study of chronic smokers funded by the US National Institute of
Mental Health and led by Vera Rubin and Lambros Comitas. On 28 March 1972 its final report was given to Kenneth McNeill, health
minister in the new People's National Party government of Michael Manley. McNeill, a doctor, had argued in
1964 that there was no evidence linking ganja to crime. From 3 June 1972 the Daily Gleaner printed the full
report in 50 daily instalments. McNeill admitted he had given it to the press, writing that the people
should have access to it and draw their own conclusions.

## The debate

The government bill went further than ganja: it ended mandatory sentences for rape, robbery with violence,
burglary and housebreaking as well. In the August debate McNeill called the study one of the most
epoch-making pieces of research of the century. It had found no significant link between ganja and crime,
and no evidence that ganja led smokers to other drugs. He also told the House that ganja would stay on the
list of dangerous drugs. The opposition, which had brought in the 1964 law, declined to vote.

| Offence | First conviction after 1972 |
|---|---|
| Possession | A fine of up to $1,000, or up to three years, or both |
| Cultivating, selling or dealing | A fine of up to $2,000, or up to seven years, or both |

Maximum penalties stayed high, and for dealing they rose, but courts could now choose. On 15 August the
Governor-General pardoned some 200 prisoners serving mandatory sentences, most of them for ganja.

## What it did not change

Possession remained a crime in [Jamaica](/country/jm/) for more than four decades. Within two years the
government was alarmed by large-scale export of ganja and proposed
[heavy trafficking penalties](/history/jamaica-trafficking-bill-1974/). Small amounts were not
decriminalised until [2015](/history/jamaica-ganja-reform-2015/).
