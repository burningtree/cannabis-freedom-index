Eswatini still prosecutes cannabis under a law of 1922. The Opium and Habit-forming Drugs Act began as a proclamation for the Swaziland protectorate, and it is the first dated restriction in the country's record.

## What the proclamation did

A United Nations survey of 1955, compiled with the Swaziland government, describes the law as it then stood. It applied to the cannabis plant, known locally as dagga or intsangu, and to cannabis and its resin. Growing the plant or producing the drug needed a permit authorised by the Resident Commissioner; none had been issued in recent years. There was no legal use outside medicine.

After an amendment of 1952, any narcotics offence carried a fine of up to £500 or up to three years in prison, or both. Police of the rank of superintendent could authorise searches of premises and vehicles, forfeited drugs were burned, and the Resident Commissioner could pay informers out of public funds.

## How it was enforced

The law made no distinction between smoker and dealer, but the courts did, judging by quantity. In recent years, the survey says, smokers had received fines of ten shillings to £20, and traffickers up to 24 months with hard labour. Vehicles were confiscated: one car and nine bicycles in Mbabane district in five years.

In the five years to 1949, 1,065 people were prosecuted for cannabis, about 4% of all prosecutions. The survey describes a crop grown in the misty western highlands and on the Lubombo range, hidden among maize and sorghum, and sold mainly across the border in South Africa. The "red dagga" of the mist belt was of high quality and much in demand. The grower, it notes, was usually not a smoker.

## Why the index moved

The [1922 law](/history/swaziland-habit-forming-drugs-act-1922/) is the only scored step in [Eswatini's](/country/sz/) history, from the unregulated state to prohibition. Nothing since has changed the score: medical cannabis bills were tabled in 2020 and 2024, and the 1922 Act remains the law the courts apply. The country's present total is 11.
