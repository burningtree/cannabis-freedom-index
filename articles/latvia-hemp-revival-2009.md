In 2009 Latvian farmers sowed fibre hemp again — 114 hectares of it around Krāslava, in the east.[@1] Hemp had once stood beside flax among the country's main crops; in the nineteenth century the British fleet used ropes of Riga hemp.[@1]

## A crop with a police escort

The revival came with a warning. A textile-industry leader told growers they could end up at a police station, proving for half a year that their plants held nothing harmful.[@1] The plants held 0.2–0.3% THC — fibre, not drug — but the burden of proof felt reversed.[@1]

That fear was not abstract. Krāslava farmers joked that if their hemp were good for getting high, addicts would long since have gathered round the fields.[@1] The joke carried the real point: everyone could see the crop, and the law still treated every cannabis plant as suspect first.

## What grew from it

Farmers organised: growers from Aizkraukle, Limbaži and Krāslava met to plan processing, a mower that could harvest 500 hectares a season was bought, and the Krāslava flax factory weighed a first-processing line.[@1] The same region's hemp food — roasted seed ground into butter for rye bread — gave the fibre a culinary twin, traced in [hemp-seed butter](/culture/traditions/latvian-hemp-seed-butter/).[@1]

The revival is the base layer under [Latvia's](/country/lv/) hemp rules today: outdoor industrial and garden hemp under Article 6(2), EU-listed seed, a below-0.3% standard.[@1]
