In February 1940 Mexico began treating drug dependence as an illness and supplying
addicts through doctors. Five months later, under pressure from the United States, it
stopped.

## The man behind it

Leopoldo Salazar Viniegra was a psychiatrist and public servant who took on Mexico's
conventional wisdom about drugs with a confidence that drew comparisons to Pasteur. He
had two targets.

The first was opiates. He proposed a state monopoly that would provide people dependent
on morphine with cheap, pure doses through medical channels, taking them out of the
hands of traffickers.

The second was marijuana. In
[October 1938](/history/mexico-salazar-marijuana-myth-1938/) he told the Academy of
Medicine that the drug caused neither madness nor violence, and that the belief was a
myth.

## The regulation

The [regulation of February 1940](/history/mexico-brief-legalisation-1940/), issued
under President Lázaro Cárdenas, put the first idea into law. Doctors could prescribe
controlled doses to dependent patients, and the state ran the supply.

It is sometimes described as a moment when Mexico legalised drugs, cannabis included.
It was not. The programme principally supplied morphine. Salazar's marijuana proposals
never went beyond research and publicity, though that alone caused at least as much
controversy at home.

## The suspension

The plan was rescinded within months, under pressure from the United States. The
regulation was suspended in July 1940.

## Why it is remembered

For decades the episode was a footnote. It has become a symbol since, for two reasons
the historian Isaac Campos sets out: resentment of the American intervention, and the
results of the punitive policies that followed. The regulation closely resembled the
maintenance programmes reformers argue for today.

The index lists the suspension for context. Neither the regulation nor its withdrawal
changed what the law said about cannabis, so [Mexico's](/country/mx/) score did not
move in 1940.
