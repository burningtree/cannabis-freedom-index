In October 1800, General Jacques-François “Abdallah” Menou issued a prohibition of hashish in French-occupied Egypt. The order reached beyond soldiers to the establishments supplying the drug and to customs. Its authorship is clearer than its exact day: David Guba’s archival research gives 8 October, while an early printed reproduction carries a different Republican-calendar date.

## The order in print

Silvestre de Sacy reproduced three articles of the measure in his 1806 Chrestomathie arabe. The first prohibits a strong drink made with hashish and what the text calls smoking hemp seed throughout Egypt. It justifies the restriction with claims about loss of reason and violent delirium.

That wording belongs to the historical document. It does not identify a chemically tested preparation or establish a modern botanical explanation of what soldiers and residents consumed. The text is evidence of the authorities’ categories and fears.

The second article prohibits preparation of the drink. Cafés and public or private houses distributing it were to be walled up, and their proprietors arrested and detained for three months. The third orders arriving bales of hashish confiscated at customs and publicly burned.

## Menou’s decision

Guba identifies Menou as the commander responsible. Napoleon had left Egypt in 1799. According to Guba, complaints about intoxicated soldiers mistreating Egyptians helped prompt the measure. His interpretation also connects it with Menou’s effort to align colonial government with the religious and moral expectations of Egyptian Sunni elites.

The resulting prohibition was therefore broader than a rule for a barracks. The printed order names establishments and imports across the occupied territory. Its claims about violence remain official justifications, not demonstrated facts about all consumers.

## Why the day needs a note

Guba dates the order to 8 October 1800 and cites an archival order of 16 Vendémiaire, Year IX. De Sacy’s reproduction instead introduces it as 17 Vendémiaire. These are different dates in the Republican calendar.

The database retains Guba’s 8 October date, but the discrepancy is now explicit. An early printed reproduction is valuable evidence of the operative wording; it does not remove the need to compare the surviving manuscript and other printings before treating the day as settled.

## A temporary occupation

The measure belongs to the history of French occupation and Egyptian cannabis control. It does not establish an uninterrupted prohibition extending into the later nineteenth century.

Earlier [medieval campaigns](/history/egypt-hashish-suppression-1378/) and Egypt’s later [1868 prohibition](/history/egypt-first-ban-1868/) remain separate events, with different authorities and evidential problems. Calling the 1800 order Napoleon’s first worldwide cannabis ban would erase those distinctions.
