The first penal law against cannabis in the modern sense was issued by an army of
occupation, to protect its own soldiers.

## An army without wine

Napoleon's troops landed in Egypt in 1798. Alcohol was hard to come by, and the soldiers
took to what the country offered instead: hashish, smoked or taken in drinks.

In October 1800 the French command banned both. Smoking hashish and drinking preparations
made from it were prohibited, with three months in prison. The fear, by one account, was
that cannabis would cost the army its fighting spirit.

## Enforcement

The order went beyond the barracks. Cafes that sold the drinks were shut and boarded
up, their owners were jailed, and imported hashish was burned.

It made little difference. The troops mostly ignored the ban, and when the French left
in 1801 they took supplies of hashish home with them.

## What followed in France

The episode introduced hashish to France as a subject. In
[1809](/history/france-sacy-assassins-lecture-1809/) a scholar in Paris linked it to the
medieval Assassins, fixing an association with the Orient and madness. A generation
later French doctors were prescribing it:
[Moreau's book](/history/france-moreau-du-hachisch-1845/) of 1845 proposed hashish as a
way to study mental illness.

## What followed in Egypt

Egypt's own rulers issued bans from [1868](/history/egypt-first-ban-1868/), with no more
success.

The index lists the decree for context and gives it no score change. It applied under an
occupation that lasted three years, and it was not obeyed even by the men who issued it.
[Egypt's](/country/eg/) scored history begins with the
[1879 ban](/history/egypt-import-ban-1879/).
