Montenegro's cannabis ban predates Montenegro's drug law by eighty years — it arrived from
Belgrade. On 1 January 1930 the Criminal Code of the Kingdom of Yugoslavia took effect
kingdom-wide, including in Montenegro, punishing the "serving" of narcotic drugs with up to six
months in prison under its crimes against public health.[@1]

## Geneva first, code second

The timing was international. Yugoslavia had ratified the Geneva opium convention on
4 September 1929, binding itself to the world's drug-control regime; the criminal code had
passed on 27 January 1929 and waited a year to bite.[@1] For the kingdom's successor states, the
1930 commencement is the recorded first ban — the base layer under [Montenegro's](/country/me/)
scores as much as under its neighbours'.[@1]

## A ban the country barely needed

The code criminalised supply into a culture that mostly grew hemp for other reasons. Interwar
Yugoslavia devoted about one-third of its industrial-plant land to hemp, making the kingdom the
world's third-largest grower after the Soviet Union and Italy — fibre and woody mass for peasant
households, low in THC beside Indian hemp.[@2] Where drugs were enjoyed rather than grown, the
police had long looked away: Belgrade's salon circles ran through the 1920s with ministers among
the clientele, and when the dealer Ja'far Ismailović stood trial in 1934 the court ruled that
enjoyment itself was not punishable, suspending his sentence.[@2]

Six months for serving, nothing for smoking: that was the Yugoslav settlement of 1930, and in
Montenegro it stood until later Yugoslav and then Montenegrin laws replaced it.
