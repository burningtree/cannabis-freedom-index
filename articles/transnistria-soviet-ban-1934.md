On 27 October 1934, the Soviet Union banned the unauthorised cultivation of Indian hemp and opium poppy across its territory. Issued jointly by the Central Executive Committee and the Council of People's Commissars, the decree restricted drug-bearing crops exclusively to state-approved medical and scientific purposes under strict commissariat quotas.[@1] Unlicensed cultivation became a criminal offence subject to mandatory crop confiscation and penal sanctions under republican criminal codes, taking effect from the 1935 planting season.[@1]

## An autonomous republic on the Dniester

In 1934, the territory east of the Dniester existed as the Moldavian Autonomous Soviet Socialist Republic (Moldavian ASSR) within Soviet Ukraine.[@1] Governed from its capital at Tiraspol, the autonomous republic operated under the criminal legislation of the Ukrainian SSR, which transposed the all-union decree by enacting criminal penalties for unauthorised sowing.[@1,3]

The prohibition targeted crops intended for narcotic use while leaving traditional industrial hemp intact.[@1,2] The agrarian communities along the Dniester plain had long grown fibre hemp for cordage, cloth and seed oil, and the central authorities sought to control drug plants without disrupting collective farm textile output.[@1,2]

## Encouraging fibre while outlawing drugs

The October prohibition followed a major all-union [hemp development decree](/history/soviet-hemp-production-incentives-1934/) issued in March 1934, which offered economic incentives to farmers who expanded industrial hemp cultivation.[@2] That earlier measure provided tax relief, increased grain allowances at fixed state prices and preferential supply of manufactured goods for collective farms fulfilling state hemp delivery contracts.[@2]

Soviet policy thus bifurcated the plant into two legal categories: promoted agricultural bast fibre and prohibited Indian hemp.[@1,2] Because the decree did not define chemical thresholds such as THC percentages, administrative authorities distinguished crops largely by declared variety and official procurement quotas.[@1,2]

## The foundation of the modern prohibition

The 1934 decree established the legal architecture of cannabis prohibition in the region for the remainder of the Soviet period.[@1] When the Moldavian SSR was created in 1940 by combining Transnistria with annexed Bessarabia, the unified republic preserved identical sowing prohibitions, later codified in the [1961 criminal code](/history/transnistria-soviet-criminal-code-1961/).[@1]

Following its de facto separation from Moldova in the early 1990s, the self-proclaimed Transnistrian authorities retained those Soviet prohibitions in their initial continuity statutes, eventually incorporating them into the territory's [2002 narcotics law](/history/transnistria-narcotics-law-2002/) and standalone criminal code.[@1] The [Transnistria profile](/country/xt/) traces how this initial 1934 sowing ban formed the basis of today's criminal cultivation offences.[@1]
