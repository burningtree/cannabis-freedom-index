For three decades after independence Indonesia went on applying the [Dutch narcotics decree of 1927](/history/indonesia-colonial-ban-1927/). In 1976, fifteen years after the UN Single Convention on Narcotic Drugs, it replaced the decree with Law No. 9 of 1976 on Narcotics. The account here follows the Transnational Institute's study of cannabis in Indonesia.

## From months to years

The colonial decree had punished cannabis offences with fines or with prison terms counted in months. The 1976 law counts in years.

| Offence | 1927 decree | 1976 law |
|---|---|---|
| Personal use | fine of up to 3,000 guilders or three months | up to two years in prison |
| Cultivation, production, possession, small-scale distribution | fine of up to 1,000 guilders or six months (cultivation) | up to six years and a fine of 10 million rupiah |
| Dealing and trafficking | fines | twenty years or life, and a fine of up to 30 million rupiah |

## No schedules

The law does not sort substances into classes. It describes the cannabis plant simply as a type of narcotic, and it prohibits cultivation and possession. One opening remains: narcotics may be used, restrictively, "for medical and research purposes only".

## What came next

The 1976 law was the first of three. Law 22 of [1997](/history/indonesia-narcotics-law-1997/) introduced schedules, put cannabis in Schedule I, limited it to research and brought in the death penalty for production and trafficking. Law 35 of 2009 kept that structure. The study notes that between 2009 and 2012 some 37,923 people were imprisoned in Indonesia for using cannabis.

## Why the index moved

The scores for possession, cultivation, sharing and consumption each fall from 1 to 0: every one of these acts now carried a prison term of years, two for use, six for growing or holding, and up to life for supply. Enforcement falls from 2 to 1. Access stays at 1, because the law still allowed restricted medical and research use; that last point disappeared in 1997. The total moves from 12 to 3.
