In 1793 the East India Company found a way to profit from cannabis without controlling it. Anyone retailing ganja in Bengal — which included today's Bangladesh — had to buy a licence from the local colonial officer first.[@1] Once licensed, the seller could buy as much as he wanted from the growers and sell it wherever he liked; the Company cared about the income, not about how much was grown or smoked.[@1]

## Taxing a trade too big to resist

The temptation was the scale of the commerce. Markets for the drug stretched across India and through its population, with large regions such as the Ganja Mahal dedicated to supplying them — and the British could not resist siphoning taxes from so extensive a trade.[@1] Between 1793 and the 1850s Company officers were content with a steady income and did not intervene too directly.[@1] The system they devised was tax-farming in its simplest form: pay for permission, then trade freely.[@1]

## A licence to buy

The mechanics put the checkpoint at the retailer's first step. Before approaching the peasant producers, the retailer turned up at the colonial officer's office and paid for a licence granting permission to proceed and buy stock.[@1] Licensed, he headed to his supplier, bought as much as he wanted, and sold it where he wanted.[@1] The government's concern began and ended with guaranteeing that the licences were paid for.[@1]

## From income to control

The free-trade licence lasted more than half a century, but mid-century the Company saw more money in counting the trade than in merely licensing it: from 1854 the wholesaler was taxed in his own warehouse, with the district collector assessing his holdings and watching how much retailers took.[@1] Act II of 1876 completed the net — cultivation licences so collectors knew every growing peasant, a dedicated establishment at Nowgong touring the fields to estimate the February crop, storage licences stating exact amounts, export permits, and purchases weighed and checked.[@1] Growing concentrated around Naogaon in today's northern Bangladesh, and the crop passed through government warehouses to licensed shops — the system the [1876 tightening](/history/india-bengal-ganja-licences-1876/) describes.[@1] That licensed trade survived the Company, the Raj and partition, until independent Bangladesh banned cultivation in [1987](/history/bangladesh-cultivation-ban-1987/) and sales in [1989](/history/bangladesh-ban-1989/). [Bangladesh](/country/bd/) still lives with the geography the Company mapped for revenue.
