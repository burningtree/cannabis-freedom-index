President George Weah signed Liberia's new drug law on 12 July 2023, setting four months to one year for first-time cannabis use and making cannabis trafficking non-bailable. It also required the deportation of non-Liberians convicted under the law after they served their sentence or sanction.

## Bail had divided the Senate

The West Africa Drug Policy Network worked on the amendment with Representative Thomas Goshua and the drug agency. During the [Senate debate in July 2022](/history/liberia-drug-law-bail-debate-2022/), a committee challenged the proposed denial of bail and recommended a bond worth twice the seized drugs' value.

Another senator argued that wealthy traffickers could afford bail and evade prosecution. The network urged a constitutional compromise protecting people who use drugs. Weah's office presented the eventual signing as part of a campaign against drug trafficking and harm to young people.

## Cannabis use carried its own prison range

The signed law listed cannabis, resin, extracts and tinctures in Schedule I. Unauthorised first-time use was a first-degree misdemeanor carrying four months to one year. On a second apprehension it became a second-degree felony, with one to five years or more under the law's sentencing references.

Cash bail was required for use cases. The express option for rehabilitation, education, aftercare or community service appeared in the separate paragraph dealing with drugs in Schedules II–IV.

The [2014 law](/history/liberia-controlled-drugs-act-2014/) had already made first-time Schedule I use a misdemeanor and provided a general treatment alternative. The new text added an explicit minimum prison term and raised the grade for repeat use.

## Holding cannabis was a separate felony

Personal possession and cultivation were second-degree felonies, while possession or cultivation for trafficking were first-degree felonies. The latter were non-bailable; the former required cash worth twice the bond.

The possession and cultivation penalty clauses specified ten to twenty years or sentencing under the Penal Law. Those general rules ordinarily capped second-degree felonies at five years. The overlapping language made the distinction between offence grades particularly consequential for someone holding cannabis for themselves.

## Consequences reached institutions and foreign residents

The law required drug clearance for public-institution admission, enrolment or employment and directed private institutions to introduce such measures. Failure to comply could justify refusal.

For non-Liberians, the earlier law's possible deportation proceedings became mandatory immediate deportation after serving a drug sentence or sanction. These rules extended the consequences of a cannabis conviction into education, work and the ability to remain in [Liberia](/country/lr/).
