President George Weah signed Liberia's new drug law on 12 July 2023,[@4] setting four months to one year for first-time cannabis use and making cannabis trafficking non-bailable.[@1] It also required the deportation of non-Liberians convicted under the law after they served their sentence or sanction.[@1]

## Bail had divided the Senate

The West Africa Drug Policy Network worked on the amendment with Representative Thomas Goshua and the drug agency.[@3] During the [Senate debate in July 2022](/history/liberia-drug-law-bail-debate-2022/), a committee challenged the proposed denial of bail and recommended a bond worth twice the seized drugs' value.[@3]

Another senator argued that wealthy traffickers could afford bail and evade prosecution. The network urged a constitutional compromise protecting people who use drugs.[@3] Weah's office presented the eventual signing as part of a campaign against drug trafficking and harm to young people.[@4]

## Cannabis use carried its own prison range

The signed law listed cannabis, resin, extracts and tinctures in Schedule I.[@1] Unauthorised first-time use was a first-degree misdemeanor carrying four months to one year. On a second apprehension it became a second-degree felony, with one to five years or more under the law's sentencing references.[@1]

Cash bail was required for use cases. The express option for rehabilitation, education, aftercare or community service appeared in the separate paragraph dealing with drugs in Schedules II–IV.[@1]

The [2014 law](/history/liberia-controlled-drugs-act-2014/) had already made first-time Schedule I use a misdemeanor and provided a general treatment alternative.[@2] The new text added an explicit minimum prison term and raised the grade for repeat use.[@1]

## Holding cannabis was a separate felony

Personal possession and cultivation were second-degree felonies, while possession or cultivation for trafficking were first-degree felonies. The latter were non-bailable; the former required cash worth twice the bond.[@1]

The possession and cultivation penalty clauses specified ten to twenty years or sentencing under the Penal Law. Those general rules ordinarily capped second-degree felonies at five years.[@1] The overlapping language made the distinction between offence grades particularly consequential for someone holding cannabis for themselves.

## Consequences reached institutions and foreign residents

The law required drug clearance for public-institution admission, enrolment or employment and directed private institutions to introduce such measures. Failure to comply could justify refusal.[@1]

For non-Liberians, the earlier law's possible deportation proceedings became mandatory immediate deportation after serving a drug sentence or sanction.[@1] These rules extended the consequences of a cannabis conviction into education, work and the ability to remain in [Liberia](/country/lr/). Enforcement followed: between September and October 2025 the drug agency arrested 178 suspects across all 15 counties.[@5]
