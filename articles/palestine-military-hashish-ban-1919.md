In 1919 the British military government in Palestine warned the public that henceforth it would be an offence to cultivate, export, introduce, buy, sell, offer for sale or be in possession of hashish.[@1] It is the first reference to an anti-hashish regime in Palestine — issued before the Mandate itself, when the smuggling routes feeding Egypt had just shifted from Greece to the Levant.[@1]

## A law copied from London

The warning became statute with the Dangerous Drugs Ordinance of 1925, modelled on Britain's metropolitan Dangerous Drugs Acts of 1920 and 1925, which had established controls over opiates and cannabis respectively.[@1] The ordinance categorically outlawed manufacturing, importing, exporting and possessing opiates, coca leaves, cocaine and Indian hemp.[@1] Amendments followed in 1928, 1932 and 1936, each incorporating the League's Opium Advisory Committee recommendations — above all higher penalties for traffickers — with the 1936 text also making transit through Palestine an explicit offence and tightening limits on hashish smokers.[@1]

## A fight the police did not want

The historian's verdict on all this lawmaking is blunt: the British had no chance against the hashish trade, for lack of resources and because drug smuggling ranked below more pressing exigencies.[@1] Palestine lacked the infrastructure, the money and the will to stop the flow; the fight against smuggling lost out to battling illegal immigration and to spiralling Arab-Jewish tensions.[@1] Policing hashish was, in the record's own impression, an annoyance and a burden, fulfilled tepidly by demoralised, underpaid servicemen facing hostile societies — while the CID chased assassinations, arms, robbery, bombs and sabotage instead.[@1]

## A corridor, not a market

What the laws confronted was mostly transit: hashish grown in Lebanon and Syria moving through Palestine to Egypt, whose own ban was already decades old.[@1] The new borders meant little to the traffickers, whose contempt for them put the colonial state's legitimacy itself in question.[@1] Bedouin couriers sewed parcels into camel saddles or glued them under the camel's hump — a ploy used so often that officers openly mocked it — risking death and prison for a few pounds' commission while Beirut notables rubbed their hands over each consignment safely run into Egypt.[@1] The [1925 ordinance](/history/palestine-mandate-ordinance-1925/) and its successors stayed on the books, but the trade flowed around them all the way to [1948 and beyond](/country/ps/).[@1]
