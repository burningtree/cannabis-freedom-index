Until 14 October 1969, anyone could legally bring marijuana or LSD into Iceland. That day the Ministry of Justice and Ecclesiastical Affairs issued a regulation banning their manufacture and sale, and the newspaper Tíminn cheered on its front page: "MARIHUANA OG LSD LOKS BANNAÐ HÉR!" — marijuana and LSD finally banned here.[@1]

## The opium law nobody enforced

Icelandic drug control dated back to 1923, when an international convention the country had joined produced what were called the opium laws.[@2] They sat with the Justice Ministry and were largely unenforced.[@2] In spring 1968 parliament amended the old rules on the opium trade and manufacture, but the law still made no provision against the common intoxicants — and so, as Tíminn noted, anyone caught with a quantity of these drugs could have expected to walk free.[@1,2] Meanwhile talk and writing about drug smuggling into the country filled the press, drug use among the young was heating up, and the ministry, in the paper's words, snapped to attention.[@1,2]

## Making and selling, banned

The regulation was announced in the Legal Gazette, Lögbirtingablaði, and it named its targets in both languages: LSD, in Icelandic lyselgí, and marijuana, in Icelandic kannabis.[@1] What it banned was manufacture (tilbúningur) and trade (verzlun) in these common pleasure-drugs, which many believed were already consumed considerably in the country.[@1] The paper framed the change as closing a loophole: importing the drugs had been entirely legal until that day.[@1]

## From regulation to the 1974 law

The 1969 regulation was only the start of a crackdown. In 1973 parliament created a specialised drug police unit in Reykjavík under its own drug court — seven officers at first — showing how frightening the authorities found the new problem.[@2] Then the [1974 narcotics law](/history/iceland-narcotics-act-1974/), No. 65, replaced the opium laws outright and raised the maximum for major violations from six to ten years in prison.[@2] Its preamble said the use of drugs was becoming a serious social problem, borne out by scientific revelations of their harmfulness — language that carried the 1969 ban into statute and still shapes how [Iceland](/country/is/) punishes cannabis today.[@2]
