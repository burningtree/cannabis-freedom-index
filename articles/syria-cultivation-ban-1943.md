In 1943, under President Shukri al-Quwatli, Syria banned growing cannabis.[@3] The ban changed little on the ground: in the Jabal al-Druze, the Druze mountains of the south, growers kept producing hashish beyond the officials' reach.[@3] It took a military ruler's forced administration of the mountain after 1949 to make the ban bite there.[@2]

## Geneva's paper prohibition

Syria entered prohibition through the mandate system, not through its own parliament.[@1] After the Geneva convention of 1925 invited states to control cannabis, France ratified it in Syria and Lebanon's name, and outright prohibition followed from the combination of the convention and mandate obligations.[@1] Two Genevan bodies watched enforcement at once: the Opium Advisory Committee and the Permanent Mandates Commission.[@1]

The French administration enforced theatrically.[@1] The hashish trade's patrons were often its own upper-class clients, and the crop assured peasant livelihoods, so the rules were applied just enough to satisfy Geneva without alienating allies.[@1] From January 1930 Egypt's police chief Russell Pasha singled out Syrian and Lebanese production as a priority target, and Egyptian pressure kept Paris at least appearing to act.[@1]

## Five tons at Hama

In October 1934 a new, harsher penal regime for cannabis production came into force in Syria.[@1] On that very day the gendarmerie found five tons of cannabis at al-Jumaqliyyah near Hama, on a farm of the powerful Barazi clan, whose members included education minister Husni al-Barazi.[@1] Citizens of Hama petitioned Geneva over the affair, and the Mandates Commission asked for more information in June 1935, pressing the French administration.[@1] The clan head and a brother caught supervising hashish production received only minimal fines, and the French toughened drug policy in the following years.[@1]

## The mountains held out

The 1943 ban restated the prohibition for an independent Syria, but the Jabal stayed outside it.[@3,2] Hashish production funded the Atrash clan's autonomy, and officials could do nothing to eradicate it until forced non-Druze administration was imposed, which Adib al-Shishakli did after taking power in 1949.[@2] American reports then found the ban "in the main consistently enforced" in the Jabal, and smuggling to Jordan sharply curtailed, with Shishakli's brother Salah directing border control.[@2]

## What came next

The 1943 ban fixed Syria's posture for half a century: growing forbidden everywhere, enforced where the state could reach.[@3,2] The drug law of the United Arab Republic years replaced the mandate-era rules in 1960 and stayed in force for thirty-three years.[Syria's 1960 law](/history/syria-law-182-1960/) Law 2 of 1993 then put growers under the same article as smugglers, with death as the penalty, and it still anchors [Syria's](/country/sy/) drug control today.[Syria's 1993 law](/history/syria-death-penalty-1993/)
