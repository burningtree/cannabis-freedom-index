Mainland Tanzania's 2015 drug law made unlawful cultivation of prohibited plants punishable by at least thirty years in prison.[@1] It also provided a separate penalty tier for small quantities held for personal consumption, where a fine, imprisonment or both were possible.[@1]

## The new law replaced an older penalty system

The [1995 drug law](/history/tanzania-illicit-traffic-act-1995/), which operated from 1996, had already prohibited unauthorised cannabis growing, possession and supply.[@2] Its original principal offences allowed a large fine, life imprisonment or both, while small personal-use amounts had their own penalty.[@2]

The replacement created the Drug Control and Enforcement Authority and reorganised the criminal rules.[@1] For growers, its prohibited-plant offence carried an express minimum of thirty years.[@1]

The same part of the law addressed allowing land to be used for unlawful cultivation and other dealings with prohibited plants.[@1] Its reach therefore extended beyond the person planting the crop.[@1]

## Personal consumption had to be proved

The small-quantity rule covered drugs proved to be intended for personal consumption.[@1] The holder bore the burden of showing that purpose instead of sale or distribution.[@1]

For the residual group of drugs outside the specially named category, the law provided a fine of at least 500,000 shillings, a three-year prison term or both.[@1] That gave the court a choice, while leaving possession unlawful.[@1]

The statute left the definition of a small quantity to regulations.[@1] The [2016 regulations](/history/tanzania-small-quantity-regulations-2016/) subsequently set no more than fifty grams for cannabis and five grams for resin or oil.[@3]

## Trafficking faced another scale of punishment

The original trafficking offence carried life imprisonment.[@1] The [2017 amendment](/history/tanzania-trafficking-thresholds-2017/) later separated lower-quantity cannabis cases from larger consignments, setting a thirty-year tier at fifty kilograms or less.[@4]

That amendment also expanded the trafficking definition and revised the conditions under which courts could choose treatment for certain drug-use convictions.[@4]

## The cultivation minimum reached the appeal courts

In [March 2022](https://tracker.cannabisfreedom.fyi/report/tanzania-cultivation-sentence-upheld-2022/), the Court of Appeal upheld a thirty-year cultivation sentence against a man linked to a field containing 535 cannabis plants.[@5] It dropped his separate possession conviction because the plants proved cultivation rather than that additional charge.[@5]

The surviving sentence showed how the statutory minimum operated in [Tanzania](/country/tz/): success on one count could still leave a grower facing decades in prison.[@5]
