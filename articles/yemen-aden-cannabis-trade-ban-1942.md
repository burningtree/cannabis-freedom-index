Aden banned cannabis in 1942. Ordinance No. 9 of that year, in force from 1 March and later amended in 1943, 1944 and 1945, gave the colony a full dangerous-drugs code — Chapter 41 of the 1955 revised laws — and Indian hemp runs through it from the definitions to the schedules.[@2]

## Ganja, bhang, or otherwise

"Indian hemp" means the dried flowering or fruiting top of the pistillate plant known as cannabis sativa L., from which the resin has not been extracted, "by whatever name (ganja, bhang or otherwise) such tops are called".[@2] The schedule then lists hemp three ways: the resin from cannabis sativa (charas or otherwise), any product with that resin as its base, and any hemp extract or tincture.[@2]

## Growing, trading, holding

Section 4 bans cultivating the opium poppy or Indian hemp without the Governor's written permission for scientific purposes, and section 5 bans manufacturing, buying, selling or possessing any dangerous drug without a licence — anything so handled may be seized.[@2] Imports and exports move only on Financial Secretary certificates matched to the receiving country's papers, and transhipment in territorial waters needs written authorisation with the consignee government's certificate attached.[@2]

## A year, fifteen hundred shillings, eight cases

Conviction without a specific penalty draws up to a year in prison, a fine of up to fifteen hundred shillings, or both.[@2] The ban was not paper: the colony's 1952 report to the United Nations records eight hemp cases that year, with 1.75 kilograms seized and fines imposed, distinguishing the cannabis ban from licensed trade in other drugs and the registered-user opium supply.[@1] The [country page](/country/ye/) continues the story on both sides of unification.
