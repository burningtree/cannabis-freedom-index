In July 1992, Taiwan stopped fighting drugs as a war and started treating them as a public-health problem. The revision renamed the martial-law-era Suppression of Opium and Narcotics statute the Narcotics Elimination Act and rewrote its purpose: no longer preventing communist poisoning, but protecting the health of body and mind.[@1,2]

## Treatment exits widen

The harshest edges of the old code softened. Addicts who came forward before discovery could enter treatment at designated centres instead of facing charges, and even non-addict users who surrendered earned reduced or remitted punishment.[@1] The change built on a smaller 1973 fix, which had reopened voluntary treatment after the original one-month self-report window lapsed.[@1,3] Use itself stayed punishable — one to three years for cannabis smoke — and possession six months to two years, but the path around those penalties grew wider.[@1]

## Fewer mandatory deaths

Several death sentences became a choice between death and life imprisonment: growing poppies to make opium, corrupt officials' drug offences, and even a third use offence after two cured treatments.[@1] Supplying opium, heroin or cannabis still drew death or life, and holding cannabis to sell kept its seven-year floor, but the code no longer demanded death wherever it once had.[@1,2] Fines, untouched since the 1950s, were redenominated in New Taiwan dollars at modern levels.[@1]

## Treatment wired into the sentence

The revision also wired treatment into the sentence itself. Courts could send a suspect to a treatment centre for up to a month of observation before ordering compulsory treatment, and every day of observation or treatment counted against any prison term ultimately imposed.[@1] Informants who gave up their source could still earn reductions, and seized drugs were destroyed as before.[@1]

## A bridge, not an end

The 1992 act kept the old structure of separate articles for supply, possession, use and officials, only softening and reframing it. Six years later the whole statute was replaced by the Narcotics Hazard Prevention Act, which sorted drugs into scheduled categories and put cannabis in the second.[@1] The 1992 revision is the hinge between the two eras: the moment the law's stated enemy changed from the communists to addiction itself. The [Taiwan profile](/country/tw/) follows the story through the 1998 rewrite to today.
