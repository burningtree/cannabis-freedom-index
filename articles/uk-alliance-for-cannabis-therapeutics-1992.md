Britain's path to medical cannabis did not begin with doctors or politicians. It began
with a woman who had multiple sclerosis and could not buy the one thing that helped.

## The gap

Cannabis-based medicines were
[withdrawn](/history/uk-cannabis-medicines-withdrawn-1973/) in the United Kingdom in
1973. A synthetic cannabinoid, nabilone, was
[licensed](/history/uk-nabilone-licensed-1982/) in 1982 for the side effects of cancer
treatment. For everyone else there was nothing lawful.

## Clare Hodges

Hodges had lived with MS for nine years. Orthodox medicine had given her limited relief
and many side effects. After reading about cannabis in a medical journal she tried
smoking it.

Getting it was the first difficulty. "As a middle-class mother of two young children,"
she recalled, "I had a bit of a problem obtaining cannabis." When she did, "the physical
relief was almost immediate … I was comfortable with my body for the first time in
years."

She tried nabilone when it became available, hoping for a legal alternative. It gave
her side effects without the benefit, and she went back to the plant.

## The Alliance

In 1992 her neurologist put her in touch with other patients doing the same. They
formed the Alliance for Cannabis Therapeutics, named after an American group, to
campaign for research and for access through the health service.

They were clear about what they were not.

> We are not campaigning for the general legalization of cannabis.

Seriously ill people, Hodges said, should get their medicine from a doctor and not have
to supply it themselves. That separation from the legalisation campaigns of the 1960s
and 1970s, which had failed, was the Alliance's strength. So was her respectability.

## What it achieved

The Alliance and the MS Society gave evidence to a
[House of Lords committee](/history/uk-lords-therapeutic-cannabis-inquiry-1997/) in the
late 1990s, putting patients' experience before parliament. The two groups differed:
the Alliance wanted access to herbal cannabis while trials ran, and the Society would
not press for it. Clinical trials followed, and with them the cannabis-based spray
Sativex.

The plant itself stayed illegal for patients until
[2018](/history/uk-medical-cannabis-2018/), after the cases of two children with severe
epilepsy. By then the MS Society had
[changed its mind](/history/uk-ms-society-herbal-cannabis-2017/) about herbal cannabis
too.
