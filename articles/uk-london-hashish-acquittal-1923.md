A London prosecution in 1923 exposed a simple problem for police: the brown
slabs they had seized were hashish, not opium, and selling cannabis was still
legal. Neither defendant was convicted, but the failed case helped turn
cannabis into a national press and policy issue.

## An arranged purchase

On 8 August detectives approached Thomas Garza in Old Compton Street and
asked whether he had opium to sell. Garza took them to a room occupied by
Idris Abdullah. The officers were shown a brown substance, arrested both men
for offering opium and recovered additional slabs from Abdullah's trunk.

The police described Garza as an Italian subject and Abdullah as Sudanese.
Both worked as coffee makers in West End restaurants. Newspapers quickly
emphasised their occupations, origins and the supposedly exotic drug trade.

## The substance was legal

Government analysis identified the material as hashish made from Indian hemp.
That destroyed the premise of the arrest: cannabis was not then among the
drugs restricted in Britain. Nevertheless, police continued with an opium
charge.

Abdullah spoke little English, making it impossible to establish that he had
understood Garza's statements about opium. His charge was withdrawn. Garza
went before the County of London Sessions on 21 August and argued that he had
acted only as an interpreter. The jury acquitted him.

## A failed case becomes a drug scare

Police representatives still told the Home Office that the episode proved a
London traffic in hashish. Newspapers described a growing foreign drug menace,
associated hashish with madness and predicted that Parliament would close the
legal gap. Home Office officials were more sceptical and found no evidence of
widespread British use.

Historian James H. Mills found this to be the only identifiable court case
behind the Pharmaceutical Society's later reference to cases involving
cannabis. In August 1924 the Society resolved to classify cannabis and its
resins as poisons. Those [sales controls took effect in
1925](/history/uk-cannabis-poison-schedule-1925/), before the broader
[1928 restrictions](/history/uk-ban-1928/).

The index records the prosecution as context. It moved no score for the
[United Kingdom](/country/gb/): the acquittal confirmed that selling hashish
was not yet prohibited and produced no immediate change in law.
