Germany's imperial ordinance on the trade in medicines took effect on **1 May 1890**. It explicitly reserved Indian-hemp herb and two named cannabis preparations for sale through pharmacies, while providing exceptions for wholesale and specified institutional dealings.

The ordinance was signed on **27 January**. The database now uses its express commencement date rather than the signature date.

## Two schedules with different functions

Section 1 governs preparations listed in **Schedule A** when offered or sold as remedies. Its coverage includes extracts and tinctures, mixtures, pills, ointments and other dosage forms, with numerous specified exceptions. The provision applies whether or not a preparation actually contains medicinally effective substances.

Section 2 separately addresses the drugs and chemical preparations in **Schedule B**. These may be offered or sold only in pharmacies, subject to the ordinance's exceptions. The cannabis-related entries include **Herba Cannabis indicae**, **Cannabinon** and **Cannabinum tannicum**.

The herb entry is translated in the schedule as the herb of Indian hemp. The latter two entries identify historical cannabis preparations; their names should not be read as proof that their composition matched a modern standardized cannabinoid medicine.

## Wholesale and institutional exceptions

Section 3 expressly exempts wholesale trade from the preceding provisions. It also exempts sales of Schedule B articles to pharmacies and to state institutions serving research or teaching purposes, provided those institutions are not also treatment establishments.

Those exceptions qualify any shorthand statement that the ordinance prohibited every sale outside a pharmacy. Its central distinction concerned permitted channels of trade and the way products were marketed.

For Schedule A, the phrase “as remedies” is especially significant. The rule is not simply a list of plants that nobody outside a pharmacy could handle. For Schedule B, the named substance classification operates through a different provision.

## Replacement and commencement

Section 4 states that the ordinance enters into force on **1 May 1890**. On the same date it repeals the medicines ordinance of **4 January 1875**, together with separate ordinances concerning artificial mineral waters and honey preparations.

## What the ordinance does not establish

The pharmacy-only rule does not itself establish that every covered sale required a doctor's prescription. Prescription and repeat-dispensing requirements must be verified in the separate provisions governing those matters. Nor does this text create a general prohibition on possessing, using or cultivating cannabis.
