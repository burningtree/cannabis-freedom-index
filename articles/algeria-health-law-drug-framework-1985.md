Algeria's 1985 health law kept prison available for illicit drug use while providing routes through treatment that could prevent prosecution or punishment.[@1,2] It also narrowed the capital clause from the repealed 1975 ordinance to specified trafficking and facilitation offences.[@1,2]

## Drug use stays within criminal law

A person using substances or plants classified as narcotics faced two months to one year in prison, a 500–5,000-dinar fine, or either penalty alone.[@1] The law therefore allowed a financial penalty without prison as well as imprisonment.[@1] Personal use remained a criminal offence at that range.[@1]

Trafficking and facilitating another person's use remained separate offences.[@1,2] The capital clause referred to those specified offences where their character threatened the Algerian people's moral health.[@1,2] The ordinary use offence was outside that list, unlike the broad wording of the [1975 ordinance](/history/algeria-drug-ordinance-1975/).[@1,2]

## Following treatment could prevent a prosecution

The law said prosecution would not be pursued against people who followed the prescribed detoxification treatment through to completion.[@1] It also covered users who had undergone detoxification or medical supervision after the acts alleged against them.[@1] Seized substances and plants still had to be confiscated.[@1]

If a person had already been charged and needed treatment, an investigating judge or juvenile judge could order a detoxification course with medical supervision and rehabilitation measures.[@1] The trial court could continue those requirements and could withhold the ordinary use penalty when the treatment provisions applied.[@1]

## Doctors remained connected to the court

Treatment could take place in a specialised establishment or outside it under medical supervision.[@1] The treating doctor had to inform the judicial authority periodically about progress and results.[@1] Evading an ordered course exposed the person to the use penalties again.[@1]

The treatment provisions consequently changed a user's possible outcome while leaving the offence itself in place.[@1] The person's response to prescribed care and the court's decisions mattered to whether punishment followed.[@1]

## Replacement legislation arrives in 2004

The [2004 drug law](/history/algeria-law-04-18-2004/) expressly repealed these drug provisions, including the capital clause.[@3] It set a new use and personal-possession range of two months to two years, with a fine or either punishment alone, and retained treatment-linked non-prosecution.[@3]

A [2007 implementing decree](/history/algeria-treatment-diversion-procedure-2007/) later detailed the medical reports and certificates supporting those decisions.[@4] Treatment remained part of the legal history of [Algeria's drug controls](/country/dz/) alongside severe supply penalties.[@3,4]
