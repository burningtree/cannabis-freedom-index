For eleven years Jamaican law banned growing and importing ganja while leaving holding and smoking it legal. In 1924 the net widened: a new Dangerous Drugs Law made cultivation, possession, sale and smoking all criminal, with up to six months in prison and/or a £100 fine on first conviction, and up to two years and/or £250 on the second.[@1] The law stayed in force until 1941.[@1]

## Completing the 1913 work

The [1913 Ganja Law](/history/jamaica-ganja-law-1913/) had slipped cannabis into an opium bill, driven by planter fears of crime among Indian labourers and by church campaigns — but it named only growing and importing as offences.[@1] The 1924 revision closed the gap, criminalising the user and the seller alongside the grower.[@1] It fit the colony's governing habit: a plantocracy-dominated legislature dealing with social problems through repressive control, with London approving colonial laws and rejecting the most blatantly punitive measures.[@1]

## Order, propaganda and depression

What the new offences meant in practice depended on the anxieties of the day. After Washington's 1937 anti-marihuana campaign painted the drug as debilitating and horrifying, public concern flared in Jamaica too, sharpened by the rise of Rastafari among poor black Jamaicans.[@1] Then the Depression hit the sugar industry, and 1938 brought massive demonstrations amid economic distress.[@1] The combination — a determination to maintain order, leftover North American publicity and Rastafari-linked antagonism — produced the 1941 revision: a first conviction now meant a mandatory maximum of one year in prison with no option of a fine, and a second up to two years.[@1] Rastafari itself, with its belief in ganja as a holy plant and repatriation to Ethiopia, became an object of fear and ridicule among respectable Jamaicans despite its profession of peace and freedom.[@1]

## The ratchet turns

The 1924 law thus sits between two harsher eras: the 1913 ordinance that started with growers and importers, and the post-1938 crackdown that kept raising the price of getting caught.[@1] Each revision kept the same logic its authors stated plainly — an unstable, excitable population to be held down by harsh laws — long after the medical debate that might have checked it had been lost.[@1] [Jamaica](/country/jm/) would keep tightening for decades before the question of reform returned.
