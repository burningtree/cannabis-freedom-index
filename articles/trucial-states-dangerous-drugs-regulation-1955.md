In 1955 Britain banned Indian hemp across the Trucial States, the sheikhdoms
that would become the United Arab Emirates.[@1] The regulation carried up to
three years in prison, but the rulers never enforced it — and hashish stayed
on open sale for another decade and a half.[@1]

## Dhows full of opium

By the 1950s the drugs trade in the Trucial States was expanding fast.[@1]
Gulf dhows carried opium from India and Pakistan alongside arms, slaves and
migrants, and elderly buyers in the sheikhdoms dosed themselves with opium
and hashish smuggled from Iran.[@1] Britain, which handled the sheikhdoms'
foreign affairs, answered with a special Dangerous Drugs Regulation filed in
London as PRO F0371/114755.[@1]

## Ten articles and two hospitals

The regulation ran to ten articles.[@1] It prohibited selling or supplying
any dangerous drug except through two named hospitals — al-Maktum Hospital
in Dubai and the American Mission Hospital in Sharjah — or under a licence
from the British Political Agent.[@1] Imports and exports needed the Agent's
permission, prescriptions required real treatment, and even helping someone
commit a drugs offence abroad counted as an offence at home.[@1] The banned
list named raw opium and its derivatives, coca leaves and their preparations,
and Indian hemp with its preparations.[@1] Anyone convicted faced up to three
years in prison, a fine of 5,000 Indian rupees, or both, plus forfeiture of
everything connected with the crime.[@1]

## The rulers left it on paper

The regulation was never applied by the rulers of the Trucial States, and
London never asked them to pass matching ordinances.[@1] They preferred to
deal with drugs under Islamic law, and the Political Agent was content: both
sides considered the British regulation and Islamic law together adequate.[@1]
In practice the trade went on. Through the 1960s opium, hashish and qat sold
openly, above all in Dubai and Sharjah, with opium and hashish the two drugs
people actually used.[@1] The one punishment the record preserves is personal
rather than legal: the Ruler of Sharjah had a dealer punished for bringing
opium and hashish from Iran to sell to elderly users who believed the drugs
boosted their virility.[@1]

## Abu Dhabi and Dubai wrote their own laws

The paper ban lasted until oil wealth gave the biggest emirates states of
their own to run. In 1970 Abu Dhabi issued the first locally made Dangerous
Drugs Act, punishable by up to five years in prison, through the
[Abu Dhabi drugs act of 1970](/history/abu-dhabi-dangerous-drugs-act-1970/).[@1]
Dubai followed with its own law the next year, and the remaining emirates
mostly adopted Dubai's text, through the
[Dubai drugs law of 1971](/history/dubai-dangerous-drugs-law-1971/).[@1]
That shared prohibition became the foundation the federation built on after
December 1971; the [United Arab Emirates profile](/country/ae/) follows the
later federal laws.[@1]
