In December 1971 West Germany replaced its Opium Law of 1929 with a Narcotics Law, the Betäubungsmittelgesetz. The law of 22 December 1971 was republished in a tidied version on 10 January 1972.[@1] It turned a statute about the medical supply of opiates into a criminal law aimed at a new kind of offender, and it more than tripled the maximum sentence.[@1]

## A paper law

Until the mid-1960s drug policy was a small corner of German politics. The Opium Law mainly regulated how the population was supplied with opium, morphine and other medicines, and it was seldom used against anyone: in the early 1960s between 100 and 150 people a year were convicted under it in the whole Federal Republic, two or three a week.

## The "hashish wave"

That changed abruptly at the end of the decade. The German encyclopedia's account of the law names three pressures.[@1]

- **Abroad:** the international conventions, the Single Convention of 1961 and the Psychotropics Convention of 1971, and the "youth drug problem" as it was perceived in the United States.[@1]
- **The press:** from the late 1960s, newspapers gave the impression of a vast "hashish and drug wave" about to roll over the country. The government's own 1970 bill spoke of a marked rise in consuming Indian hemp and its resin hashish, a likely gateway effect drawing young people into the world of narcotics, and a legislature that named protecting youth as its purpose.[@3]
- **Politics:** public opinion tied the drug question to the student protest movement, the extra-parliamentary opposition that had formed during the grand coalition of 1966 to 1969.[@1]

## What the law did

The new law sorted offenders into types and gave each type its level of sanction. The maximum sentence rose from three years to ten. It rose again, to fifteen, on 1 January 1982.[@1]

The law also let the federal government add substances to its schedules by regulation. That power, an executive body creating offences that carry long prison terms, has been contested by constitutional and criminal lawyers ever since.[@1] The permit-and-schedule design itself continued the Geneva system: cannabis and its resin had been under the Opiumgesetz's control since the 1925 agreement, with hashish under its absolute ban, and the new law kept that continuity.[@3]

## The long consequence

The 1971 law, in its 1981 version, governed cannabis in Germany for half a century. The Constitutional Court softened it in [1994](/history/germany-constitutional-court-1994/) by requiring prosecutors to drop cases involving small amounts,[@3] and cannabis was taken out of it altogether by the [Cannabis Act of 2024](/history/germany-cannabis-act-2024/).[@2]

## Why the index moved

The index moved [Germany](/country/de/) from 24 to 15 with the [1971 law](/history/germany-narcotics-law-1971/). Possession, cultivation, enforcement and consumption each fall by one point and access goes to 0: the ceiling rose to ten years under a law now written for prosecution, and pharmacy sales of cannabis had ended.
