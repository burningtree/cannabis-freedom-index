On 1 June 1964 the Netherlands Antilles put a new Opium Ordinance into force, replacing a drug law from July 1929 that had served for thirty-five years.[@1] Where the old ordinance is known mostly by its number, the replacement survives as a full text — and it banned growing cannabis outright.[@1]

## Replacing the 1929 ordinance

The 1960 ordinance, signed that 28 March, states plainly that it replaces the ordinance of 30 July 1929 on opium and other narcotics, published as P.B. 1940 no. 138.[@1] Its own commencement came only in 1964, fixed by a national decree of 29 April that year.[@1] The Antillean legislature kept amending it afterwards — in 1967, 1997, 1999 and 2004, with some amendments lapsing before they could ever apply — but the core stood.[@1]

## What the ordinance said

Hemp got its own dictionary entry: flowering or fruit-bearing tops or parts of any plant of the cannabis genus, seeds and leaves excepted unless they came with the tops, from which the resin had not been extracted, by whatever name.[@1] The resin drawn from hemp was listed alongside it.[@1] Then came the short sentence that mattered most for growers: the cultivation of plants of the cannabis genus is prohibited.[@1]

Extract and tincture of hemp faced their own bans, and the ordinance reached beyond growers and dealers to the places they used: owners, tenants and users of vehicles, vessels, aircraft, buildings, yards and enclosed grounds where scheduled substances were found faced detention or fines unless they showed they had taken every necessary step against the unauthorised presence.[@1]

## Penalties and successors

The penalty ladder ran steep: the gravest trafficking offences drew life imprisonment or up to twenty-four years plus fines in the millions of guilders, with twelve-year bands below them, minimum fractions for repeat offenders, and twenty-year terms where death resulted.[@1] Buying, renting, transporting or hiding objects obtained through drug crime carried up to twelve years of its own.[@1]

The ordinance carried Antillean drug control into the modern era and, through the consolidated text, into [Curaçao's own 2024 drug law](/history/curacao-opium-ordinance-2024/), which replaced the inheritance that July.[@1] For Curaçao the 1964 commencement — thirty-five years after the first ordinance — remains the hinge between the colonial drug regime and today's [scores](/country/cw/).[@1]
