From 14 June 1955, people breaking Iraq's drug law faced a fine of 100–1,000 dinars, six months to five years in prison, or both.[@1] Unauthorised cannabis possession came within that law, which covered Indian hemp, its resin and preparations made from them.[@2] The amendment supplied one general penalty for violations of the law.[@1]

## Cannabis holders needed lawful authority

The [1938 drug law](/history/iraq-dangerous-drugs-law-1938/) had put cannabis within a system of professional licences, prescriptions and controlled trade.[@2] Doctors, dentists, veterinarians, licensed pharmacists and other authorised people could hold drugs through the permitted channels.[@2] Other holders needed to have obtained them from a licensed pharmacist on an authorised doctor's prescription, or under the named pharmacy laws.[@2]

Manufacture required a licence, imports and exports needed permits, and pharmacists and dealers had to keep records.[@2] Those requirements explain the reach of the 1955 penalty: it applied to violations of an existing control system.[@1,2]

## The court could choose a fine, imprisonment or both

Law 64 of 1955 replaced the drug law's penalty clause.[@1] Its prison option had both a minimum and a maximum, while the fine had its own monetary range.[@1] The text expressly allowed the two punishments to be combined.[@1]

The amendment also required confiscation of materials and articles mentioned in the law when seized in the offender's actual use.[@1] It began on publication in the Government Gazette; the reproduced publication notice gives 14 June 1955.[@1]

## A new law kept the same general penalty range

The [1965 replacement law](/history/iraq-narcotics-law-1965/) retained the general choice of a 100–1,000-dinar fine, six months to five years, or both.[@3] It brought the earlier drug and cultivation controls together and allowed supervised cannabis crops grown exclusively for fibre and seeds.[@3]

Its explanation pointed to shortcomings in the older laws and the need to match the Single Convention on Narcotic Drugs.[@3] Iraq was replacing a control system that had developed through separate laws and amendments.[@3]

## Personal-use and supply penalties then separated

In [1967](/history/iraq-drug-penalties-1967/), personal possession or growing brought one to five years in prison **and** a fine.[@4] Supply, including giving drugs away, carried a term of hard labour and a higher fine.[@4] The earlier choice between fine and prison gave way to distinct penalties within [Iraq's continuing drug controls](/country/iq/).[@1,4]
