In January 1843 a London medical journal gave its front pages to a drug almost unknown in Western
medicine. The *Provincial Medical Journal* reprinted, revised by its author, the memoir in which
W. B. O'Shaughnessy — professor of chemistry at the Medical College in Calcutta, then on leave in
London — described four years of trials of Indian hemp on animals, on himself, and on patients with
tetanus, hydrophobia and cholera.[@1]

## A drachm every half hour

O'Shaughnessy's directions were bold and exact. For tetanus, a drachm of the tincture every half
hour until the spasms ceased or narcotism set in; for hydrophobia, ten to twenty grains of resin to
be chewed; for cholera, thirty drops of tincture every half hour to check vomiting and purging.[@1]
"Of all powerful narcotics," he wrote, "it is the safest to use with boldness and decision."[@1] He
had left a large supply of gunjah with Mr. Squire, a chemist of Oxford Street, who undertook to put
the extract at the disposal of any hospital physician or surgeon willing to test it — so that the
remedy might be "extensively and exactly tested without favor or prejudice."[@1]

## The February letter

The reprint provoked comment, and O'Shaughnessy answered in the journal on 8 February 1843. He
disclaimed any wish to present hemp as a specific, but stood by its "extraordinary anti-convulsive
power": six horses with traumatic tetanus, four recovered.[@1] He pointed to cases lately published
in the same journal by a Mr. Ley, who entertained "no doubt" of the anti-convulsive power, and
conceded a difficulty raised by Dr. Pereira's London trials — the Indian resin sent from Calcutta
sometimes did nothing, probably through age and adulteration, so that larger doses were needed in
Britain than in India.[@1]

## What followed

A provincial journal had already reported hemp resin in convulsive disorders in 1842, and
O'Shaughnessy spoke before a crowded meeting of the Royal Medico-Botanical Society while in
London.[@2] Over the following decades British doctors tried cannabis for period pains, in labour,
for migraine and in asylums — the beginning of a medical career for the drug in Britain that ran
from these 1843 pages to the specialist prescriptions of [today](/country/gb/).[@2]
