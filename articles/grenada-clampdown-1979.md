Grenada's Rastafarians helped make the 1979 revolution and were then jailed by it. They joined the New Jewel Movement from the start, served in its army and built its schools and roads — and within two years many were detained without trial for growing the herb.[@1]

## From Gairy's shears to the revolution

Under Prime Minister Eric Gairy, soldiers sheared off Rastafarians' locks and called them "dutty" (dirty). Maurice Bishop's movement promised to end that discrimination, which is one reason Rastas backed it: under the new government they entered the army, the administration and leadership posts, and brethren from nearby islands sought refuge in Grenada.[@1]

How many took part in the 13 March 1979 overthrow is disputed — Horace Campbell puts it above four hundred Rastafarians in the People's Liberation Army, while a participant interviewed later estimated about one hundred — but that they were involved from the beginning is not. They joined workers' education projects and the Centre for Popular Education, and some saw Bishop as a saviour, an attitude that lasted at least a year.[@1][@2]

## Big growers versus small growers

The People's Revolutionary Government took a nuanced view of ganja: sympathetic to local use with an eye to reform, but determined to rein in large-scale production for international distribution. The big producers clear-cut forests and ruined small peasants' farms, and criminal networks used nearby islands to transship Colombian narcotics — some dealers even grew dreadlocks and aped Rasta behaviour.[@1]

The police chief asked Rastafarians to help limit large-scale growing. In May [1980](/history/grenada-clampdown-1979/) the big growers led a demonstration against the restrictions, met by a counter-demonstration for self-sufficiency in food production. The growers warned that ending large-scale production would lead to outlawing smoking itself — an appeal to Rasta feeling that served their own commercial interest.[@1]

Then, on 19 June 1980, a bomb exploded at a Labour Day celebration, killing three children. Those behind it were tied to large-scale marijuana production for international distribution.[@1]

## The falling-out

In 1981 the relationship frayed openly. Rastas conspicuously grew ganja and some were jailed, including middle-class members of prominent families; many ended up in the Hopevale detention camp, described as Grenada's gulag.[@1] The younger interviewees explain the dilemma: the Rastas wanted legalisation, but the government feared flouting international drug rules, so it moved to ban ganja and arrests began — without due process or hearings, and some visible Rastas picked up arms over the issue, bringing a wider crackdown.[@1]

The split was already being exploited from outside. In October 1979 the Torchlight newspaper ran a story, illustrated by a Jamaican Rasta artist, claiming Grenadian Rastafarians would march against school exclusions and ganja arrests; Campbell shows two young Rastas were manoeuvred by Trinidadian and Barbadian anti-communist elites into denouncing the government. The plot — an anti-government cabal with US involvement covering an assassination plan and an invasion — was soon exposed, and Grenadian Rastas counter-demonstrated to reaffirm their support for the revolution.[@1][@2]

The bitterness lasted. Even supporters of the revolution count Hopevale among its saddest chapters, and the feeling that Bishop had reneged on his respect for the brethren survived the revolution itself.[@1] When Grenada finally [decriminalised cannabis in 2026](/history/grenada-decriminalisation-2026/), it closed a circle that began with the growers who had helped put the revolution in power.
