Nepal banned cannabis in the 1970s. For twenty years after that, at one festival, the
state itself handed it out.

## The arrangement

Mahashivaratri, the great night of Shiva, brings holy men from across Nepal and India
to the Pashupatinath temple in Kathmandu. Smoking cannabis is part of their devotion.

Until 1995 the trust that runs the temple distributed marijuana to the sadhus at the
festival. It did not have to buy it. As the trust's director put it in 2018: "The
sadhus were given marijuana confiscated by the Home Ministry." What the police seized
during the year, the temple gave away on one night.

## The end of it

The practice stopped on the instruction of King Birendra.

Use at the festival went on being tolerated, partly because of its religious meaning.
Visitors came to smoke as well as to worship.

## Tightening

In February 2018 the trust and the police announced a change. Visitors caught smoking
or holding cannabis at the temple would be
[arrested](/history/nepal-shivaratri-arrests-2018/). The sadhus, about 2,000 of them
that year, stayed exempt, but were told not to sell or offer marijuana to anyone else.
A police officer said the festival was drawing young people into addiction.

In the five months before, police at the local station had detained about 300 people
for cannabis and handed nearly 200 young smokers back to their parents with a warning.

## What it shows

Nepal closed its licensed cannabis shops in [1973](/history/nepal-shops-closed-1973/)
under foreign pressure and made use a crime in
[1976](/history/nepal-narcotics-act-1976/). The Pashupatinath arrangement shows how
partial that change was in practice: a government enforcing a ban and supplying a
ritual with the proceeds.
