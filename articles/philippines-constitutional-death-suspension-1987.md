The Philippines' new Constitution stopped courts imposing death sentences in February 1987 and reduced existing death sentences to reclusion perpetua, a prison penalty under the penal code.[@1,2] The protection extended to cannabis offences, which had been covered by drug laws allowing death for trafficking.[@4] It left Congress a route to restore capital punishment for heinous crimes.[@1]

## A constitutional protection reached drug defendants

The Constitution took effect when voters ratified it on 2 February.[@1,2] The Supreme Court later confirmed that date in De Leon v. Esguerra, a dispute over local officials' tenure.[@2] Its effect began with ratification, although the presidential proclamation announcing the result followed later.[@2]

The Bill of Rights prohibited cruel, degrading or inhuman punishment alongside excessive fines.[@1] Its death-penalty clause had two practical consequences: courts could no longer impose death under existing laws, and sentences already imposed had to be reduced.[@1]

## Cannabis offences still carried long prison terms

The drug law had made selling, delivering or giving away prohibited drugs punishable by life imprisonment to death.[@4] Cannabis defendants therefore came within the wider constitutional protection against execution.[@1,4]

The [1982 amendment](/history/philippines-penalties-raised-1982/) separately prescribed six years and one day to twelve years, plus a fine, for possessing or using marijuana.[@3] Growing cannabis carried fourteen years and one day to life imprisonment.[@3] Those custodial penalties explain why people could still face severe punishment after the constitutional change.[@1,3]

The Constitution also required laws to address physical or psychological punishment of prisoners and subhuman detention conditions.[@1] Its protections reached the treatment of people in custody as well as the sentence a court could impose.[@1]

## Congress retained power to bring death sentences back

The constitutional exception allowed Congress to provide for death when compelling reasons involving heinous crimes justified it.[@1] The [1993 death-penalty law](/history/philippines-death-penalty-1993/) expressly invoked that clause.[@5]

For specified drug offences, its highest quantity band included at least 750 grams of marijuana or fifty grams of marijuana resin or oil.[@5] It also introduced lower penalties for smaller quantities, which the Supreme Court later interpreted in [People v. Simon](/history/philippines-simon-quantity-ruling-1994/).[@4,5]

## A later statute removed death again

In [2006](/history/philippines-death-penalty-abolished-2006/), Congress again prohibited death sentences and replaced them with the appropriate prison penalty.[@6] The two changes sit at different points in [the Philippines' cannabis history](/country/ph/): the 1987 Constitution ended the earlier capital regime, and the 2006 Act removed the death provisions Congress had subsequently restored.[@1,5,6]
