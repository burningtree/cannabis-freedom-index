The Drugs and Drug Trafficking Act, No. 140 of 1992, was assented to on 2 July 1992 and published in the Government Gazette on 15 July, in the last years of white minority rule. It repealed the Abuse of Dependence-producing Substances Act of [1971](/history/south-africa-mandatory-minimums-1971/) and its amendments, and with them the mandatory minimum prison sentences of a law its own government had called the toughest in the Western world.

## How dagga is classed

The Act sorts drugs into three groups: dependence-producing substances, dangerous ones and undesirable ones. Cannabis is in the last. The schedule lists "Cannabis (dagga), the whole plant or any portion or product thereof", with one exception, the pharmaceutical dronabinol.

## The offences

| Section | Conduct | Penalty for an undesirable substance |
|---|---|---|
| 4 | Using or possessing | a fine the court thinks fit, up to 15 years in prison, or both |
| 5 | Dealing | up to 25 years in prison, with or without a fine |

The only people outside section 4 are patients who obtained the substance from a doctor, dentist or pharmacist, and those professionals themselves.

What matters about these penalties is what they lack. There is a ceiling and no floor. Under the 1971 Act a first conviction for possession meant at least two years, and for dealing at least five. From 1992 a magistrate could fine.

## The presumptions

The Act made the prosecutor's work easier in other ways. A person was presumed to be dealing, until they proved otherwise, if:

- they were found with more than 115 grams of dagga;
- they were found with a dangerous substance on school grounds or within 100 metres of them;
- dagga plants were found on cultivated land of which they were the owner, occupier or manager;
- they were conveying a drug, or in charge of the vehicle in which it was found.

## The same law, two eras

The Act was written under one constitution and applied under another. It remained the law on dagga through the first twenty-four years of democracy, until the Constitutional Court ruled in [2018](/history/south-africa-constitutional-court-2018/) that sections 4 and 5 could not be applied to an adult's private use, possession and cultivation.

## Why the index moved

The index moved [South Africa](/country/za/) from 7 to 11 with the [1992 Act](/history/south-africa-drugs-act-1992/). Only enforcement changed, from 0 to 2: with the mandatory minimums gone, a fine became possible for possession. Possession, cultivation, sharing and consumption stay at 1, since each was still a crime with a long maximum sentence.
