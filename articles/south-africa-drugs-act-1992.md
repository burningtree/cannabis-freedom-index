The Drugs and Drug Trafficking Act, No. 140 of 1992, was assented to on 2 July 1992 and published in the Government Gazette on 15 July, in the last years of white minority rule. It repealed the Abuse of Dependence-producing Substances Act of [1971](/history/south-africa-mandatory-minimums-1971/) and its amendments.[@1] That law's mandatory minimum prison terms had already been [abolished in 1986](/history/south-africa-sentencing-discretion-1986/).[@2]

## How dagga is classed

The Act sorts drugs into three groups: dependence-producing substances, dangerous ones and undesirable ones. Cannabis is in the last. The schedule lists "Cannabis (dagga), the whole plant or any portion or product thereof", with one exception, the pharmaceutical dronabinol.

## The offences

| Section | Conduct | Penalty for an undesirable substance |
|---|---|---|
| 4 | Using or possessing | a fine the court thinks fit, up to 15 years in prison, or both |
| 5 | Dealing | up to 25 years in prison, with or without a fine |

The only people outside section 4 are patients who obtained the substance from a doctor, dentist or pharmacist, and those professionals themselves.

What matters about these penalties is what they lack. There is a ceiling and no floor. Under the 1971 Act a first conviction for possession had meant at least two years, until a 1986 amendment did away with the minimums and made fines possible.[@2] The 1992 Act kept that freedom: a magistrate could still fine.[@1]

## The presumptions

The Act made the prosecutor's work easier in other ways. A person was presumed to be dealing, until they proved otherwise, if:

- they were found with more than 115 grams of dagga;
- they were found with a dangerous substance on school grounds or within 100 metres of them;
- dagga plants were found on cultivated land of which they were the owner, occupier or manager;
- they were conveying a drug, or in charge of the vehicle in which it was found.

The 115-gram rule did not last. In [November 1995](/history/south-africa-bhulwana-presumption-1995/) the new Constitutional Court struck it down: the state could not explain the figure, unchanged since it was set as four ounces in [1954](/history/south-africa-dealing-presumption-1954/), and a regular user might hold that much.[@3]

## The same law, two eras

The Act was written under one constitution and applied under another. It remained the law on dagga through the first twenty-four years of democracy, until the Constitutional Court ruled in [2018](/history/south-africa-constitutional-court-2018/) that sections 4 and 5 could not be applied to an adult's private use, possession and cultivation.

## Why the index did not move

The index scores for [South Africa](/country/za/) stay where the [1986 amendment](/history/south-africa-sentencing-discretion-1986/) left them. That change, not the 1992 Act, raised enforcement from 0 to 2 by ending mandatory prison. Possession, cultivation, sharing and consumption stay at 1, since each was still a crime with a long maximum sentence.[@1,2]
