Uruguay's pharmacies began selling cannabis in 2017. It was the second time the state
had given them that job. The first was eighty years earlier.

## The 1937 law

Law 9.692, of 11 September 1937, dealt with hashish and other narcotics in three moves.

- **Import** became a monopoly of the state.
- **Sale** was allowed only in pharmacies, on a doctor's prescription.
- **Possession** by anyone not authorised became an offence, with one to two years in
  prison.

It was a prohibition in the form of a public service. The drug was not declared
forbidden; it was declared the state's, to be handed out through a medical channel.

## A habit of monopolies

The approach was typical of the country. In 1931 Uruguay had created a state monopoly
on distilling spirits, which lasted until 1996. Controlling a risky product by owning
its supply was an established way of doing things.

## The long middle

In [1974](/history/uruguay-personal-use-1974/) possession of a minimal quantity for
personal use stopped being punished, while growing and selling stayed serious crimes.
For almost forty years a Uruguayan could lawfully hold cannabis that could not lawfully
be obtained.

## The return

When the government of José Mujica set out to close that gap, it reached for the same
instrument. The [2013 law](/history/uruguay-legalisation-2013/) put the whole chain
under state control, with production by contracted growers and sale through
pharmacies, which [began in 2017](/history/uruguay-pharmacy-sales-2017/). This time no
prescription was needed.

In the index the 1937 law is [Uruguay's](/country/uy/) first step down from an
unregulated state. What makes it worth a second look is that the idea in it, the state
as the only legitimate dealer, survived the prohibition it was written to enforce.
