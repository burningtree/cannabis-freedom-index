California outlawed cannabis in 1913, more than two decades before the federal Marihuana Tax Act, and the other states followed until most had restricted it by 1933.[@1] The 1913 law passed with no public debate, as an obscure amendment drafted by the State Board of Pharmacy in the middle of one of the country's earliest and most aggressive anti-drug campaigns.[@2,3]

## A pharmacy board at war with drugs

California pioneered American drug enforcement. San Francisco passed the country's first known anti-narcotics measure, against opium dens, in 1875, and by 1907 the pharmacy board had pushed sales of opium, morphine and cocaine onto a prescription-only footing, seven years before Congress passed the Harrison Act. It backed the rules with undercover agents, informers and raids on pharmacists and Chinese dens.[@2,3]

Cannabis itself was barely an issue. It was known as hashish or Indian hemp, an exotic vice of foreigners and a handful of bohemians, and the Mexican word "marihuana" was scarcely heard before 1910.[@2,3] An [1889 Missouri statute](/history/us-missouri-hasheesh-den-1889/) had fined keepers of houses used for smoking hasheesh, but that single-state den-keeping offence left the national timeline untouched, which still starts with the 1913 bans.[@1]

## "Hindoos," loco-weed and a letter

In July 1911 a board member, Henry Finger, wrote to the federal drug official Hamilton Wright that Punjabi Sikh immigrants, called "Hindoos," had created a demand for cannabis indica and were drawing white Californians into the habit, and asked whether the Hague conference could help curb it. Wright answered that California's legislature should act on Indian hemp.[@2,3]

Meanwhile "marihuana" drifted north with migrants and soldiers in Mexico's revolutionary disorders of 1910–1920. Pharmacy journals noticed it as a relative of Indian hemp tied to delinquents and soldiers, so the board added "loco-weed" to its 1913 bill alongside hemp.[@2,3]

## A misworded ban from 10 August 1913

The legislature passed the bill unanimously, over the opposition of the state's druggists.[@2,3] It took effect on 10 August 1913, but instead of adding cannabis to the Poison Act section restricting sales, it amended the section banning possession of opium paraphernalia: holding "extracts, tinctures, or other narcotic preparations of hemp, or loco-weed" became unlawful, except for corn remedies of negligible potency.[@2,3]

On paper the wording banned even medical use, though no evidence shows it was ever used against medicine. It looks like a drafting blunder, moved from a section that allowed possession on prescription to one that allowed none, perhaps because field-grown cannabis seemed a user problem rather than a pharmacy one.[@2,3] A 1915 amendment put flowering tops, leaves, extracts and tinctures under prescription-only sale, while the 1913 possession ban stayed on the books until 1937.[@2,3]

## What came next

Massachusetts had acted in 1911, and by 1933 most states restricted cannabis, many through the Uniform State Narcotic Drug Act.[@1] [The 1937 Tax Act](/history/us-marihuana-tax-act-1937/) then taxed every transaction so heavily that legal supply, including prescriptions, became impractical. The country's current patchwork is on [its profile](/country/us/).
