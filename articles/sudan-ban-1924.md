Sudan’s Hashish and Opium Ordinance appeared in the government gazette on 25 August 1924. It prohibited unauthorised cultivation, manufacture, sale, possession, transport and smoking, with up to one year in prison, a fine, or both. The same law preserved recognised cannabis medicines and permitted small scientific quantities.[@1]

## The new ordinance replaced an earlier hashish law

The governor-general in council made the ordinance, signed by Governor-General Lee Stack and council secretary R. V. Bardsley. Its repeal clause expressly removed the Hashish Ordinance 1907. Cannabis control therefore already existed when the replacement appeared in the gazette.[@1]

Earlier [contraband controls from 1901](/history/sudan-cannabis-contraband-imports-1901/) had restricted cannabis imports while allowing qualified medical practitioners, licensed poison sellers and people specially permitted by the governor-general to receive them. The revised collection marks the extension to local manufacture in 1906.[@2]

The 1924 law used “hashish” broadly. Its definition included cannabis indica or cannabis sativa, preparations and mixtures made from hashish, and intoxicating drugs prepared from it. Its prohibitions reached movement into, out of and within Sudan, including goods passing through on their way elsewhere.[@1]

## Medicines and scientific work had exceptions

Tincture and extract of cannabis indica were expressly named in the medical exception, alongside officially recognised pharmacopoeial preparations. Licensed doctors, dentists, pharmacists and veterinary surgeons could import, transport, possess and sell those preparations under the conditions of the public-health ordinance.[@1]

A person could also possess and take those preparations as medicine in accordance with the public-health rules. The exception connected the permitted product with the conditions governing its handling and use.[@1]

For scientific purposes, the governor-general could issue a written permit allowing cultivation, manufacture, possession, import or export of small quantities. The permit named the authorised person and could impose conditions.[@1]

## Cafés and transport could be caught in a case

The law also prohibited smoking equipment and allowed confiscation of drugs, pipes, packaging and property used for concealment or transport. A court could order the seized property destroyed or otherwise disposed of.[@1]

Owners or managers of cafés and similar public places could be punished for committing, assisting or permitting an offence there. A court could withdraw a licence or close the premises for up to a year. A repeat conviction under that premises rule could also bring expulsion for a person who was not a native of Sudan.[@1]

The ordinance belonged to the history of the whole territory, including what is now [South Sudan](/country/ss/). The [Sudan profile](/country/sd/) and the separate [southern history entry](/history/south-sudan-ban-1924/) sit within that shared past: broad restrictions operated alongside medical exceptions and scientific permits.[@1]
