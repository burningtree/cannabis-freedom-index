Addiction specialist Jenishbek Nazaraliev proposed legalising cannabis in Kyrgyzstan in January 2014, beginning with an experiment around Lake Issyk-Kul. His organisation dated the initial proposal to 4 January and argued that regulation could reduce corruption, improve oversight and bring tax revenue. He wanted legal medical and recreational use.[@1]

## A doctor challenged the prohibition policy

Nazaraliev was a former presidential candidate who had opened Bishkek’s first private addiction clinic in 1993. Speaking to RFE/RL’s Kyrgyz Service, he argued that keeping cannabis underground left the market in the hands of the black economy.[@2]

His proposal focused on production and sales as well as consumption. The campaign’s own statement said legal regulation could give the authorities greater control over growers, sellers and users. It presented the Issyk-Kul experiment as a way to change how an existing cannabis economy was governed.[@1]

## Harvesting already supported local livelihoods

RFE/RL reporters spoke with villagers in the Tiup and Ak-Sui districts around the lake. They described people rubbing cannabis plants between their hands, scraping off the resin and packaging it for local dealers, who supplied larger buyers.[@2]

Villagers discussed paying police to overlook harvesting. Local police told the reporters that eliminating a trade tied to people’s survival would be difficult. These accounts gave the proposal a practical setting: cannabis cultivation and gathering continued despite prohibition.[@2]

## The drug-control service opposed legalisation

The state drug-control service argued that defeating traffickers was essential to social stability. It said legalisation would fail to control networks extending beyond Kyrgyzstan, along routes carrying drugs towards Russia and the European Union.[@2]

Nazaraliev’s organisation instead argued that prohibition rewarded criminals and corrupt officials. The disagreement concerned who could control the market and how, with the doctor seeking a regulated experiment and the drug-control service defending enforcement.[@1,2]

## Later reforms changed penalties for small offences

The [2019 misdemeanour code](/history/kyrgyzstan-new-codes-2019/) retained offences for small possession and cultivation and provided fines and probation-style restrictions. A finding under that code did not create a criminal record, but it could still place duties on the person through probation supervision.[@3]

The [replacement offences code from 2021](/history/kyrgyzstan-small-drug-offences-2021/) used administrative penalties for small possession and small unauthorised growing. Those changes concerned punishment for personal conduct; Nazaraliev’s proposal had also sought a lawful production and sales system. The [country profile](/country/kg/) explains the penalties that followed.[@1,4]
