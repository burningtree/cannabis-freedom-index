For half a century Switzerland punished the trade and left the user alone. The first Narcotics
Act of 1924 banned opium and cocaine; in 1951 the ban stretched to cannabis — but consuming it
stayed legal.[@1]

## 1 August 1975: use becomes punishable

A revision of the Narcotics Act changed that on 1 August 1975: using drugs became punishable for
the first time, though only as a minor offence, and courts could waive the penalty in light
cases.[@1][@3] The same revision doubled down on enforcement — more arrests, police registration
of users and sellers, no needle programmes, onerous methadone rules — and rejected harm
reduction outright.[@2]

## The crackdown that grew the crisis

It did not work. Zurich's injecting population grew from under 4,000 at the time of the revision
to an estimated 10,000 in 1985, 20,000 in 1988 and 30,000 in 1992, with HIV spreading through
shared needles until, by 1989, half of all new Swiss HIV infections were linked to injecting.[@2]
The response migrated from the statute book to the park: in 1987 Zurich fenced the problem into
the Platzspitz tolerance zone, health workers followed, and the failure of punishment as policy
became impossible to ignore.[@2]

## The long unwind

The unwind took decades. From 1991 the Federal Council pursued the four-pillar strategy —
prevention, therapy, harm reduction, repression — and in 1992 it approved prescribed heroin for
the most dependent.[@1] Consumption stayed punishable, but its price fell: a CHF 100 fixed fine
from 2013, up to 10 grams unpunishable from 2017, and from 2021 scientific trials selling
regulated cannabis to enrolled adults.[@1] The 1975 revision still sits in the law's ancestry —
as the warning [Switzerland](/country/ch/) learned from.
