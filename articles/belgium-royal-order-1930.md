Belgium's Royal Order of 31 December 1930 formed a regulatory layer beneath
the [1921 drug law](/history/belgium-drug-law-1921/). A government text circulated
by the United Nations in 1952 shows how that system controlled cannabis
alongside other narcotics.

The edition matters. The UN document reproduces the order with amendments
through 20 September 1951, rather than its untouched original wording. The
rules described here follow that consolidated text; its later additions should
not all be attributed to the day the order was signed in 1930.

## Cannabis products and a specific exception

Article 1 expressly covered Indian hemp, its resin, preparations based on that
resin, and hemp extract and tincture. Hashish, esrar, chira and djamba appeared
as names of resin-based preparations. The list therefore reached several forms
of cannabis, rather than identifying only the plant.

The same article excluded preparations containing Indian-hemp extract or
tincture for external use. That specific exception is part of the historical
rule. It does not establish a general exemption for cannabis products or
demonstrate how widely such preparations were actually available.

## Permission governed domestic dealings

Article 11 required prior ministerial authorisation to manufacture, possess,
sell, offer, deliver or acquire narcotics, whether payment was involved or not.
An authorisation was personal, valid for one year and subject to withdrawal.
It identified the place where the permitted operations could occur.

Pharmacists with public dispensaries and certain medical practitioners received
permission within professional limits. Acquisition or possession under a medical
prescription was excluded from Article 11's general authorisation requirement.
These provisions placed medical distribution within a controlled channel.

## Prescriptions, records and inspection

Article 19 required pharmacists dispensing narcotics to receive an original
prescription written, dated and signed by an eligible practitioner. Other
provisions governed written orders, registers and the retention of documents.

The system also involved physical controls. Article 12 required stocks held
for sale or supply to be secured in a locked cabinet or reserved room, with
arrangements for inspection. Article 25 gave inspectors access to specified
premises and required checks of authorisations and registers. The text describes
their powers, rather than supplying evidence of inspection frequency.

## The order and the law worked together

Article 2 connected the Narcotics Service's duties to the 1921 Act and the
orders made under it. Article 28 directed that violations of this order be
punished under that Act, without prejudice to Penal Code penalties.

For [Belgium's history](/country/be/), the distinction is essential: the statute
provided a legal framework, while regulations identified substances and governed
their handling. This government communication establishes explicit cannabis
coverage in the reproduced edition. It does not, by itself, establish Belgium's
earliest cannabis restriction or the original scope of the 1921 law.
