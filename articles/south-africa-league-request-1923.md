Cannabis reached the agenda of the League of Nations because South Africa put it there.
The request came one year after the country banned dagga at home.

## A law in need of company

In November 1922 the Union government
[classified dagga](/history/south-africa-national-ban-1922/) as a habit-forming drug,
alongside cocaine, morphine and opium. The ban had been pushed by doctors and farmers
in the Cape and passed in a year of "black peril" politics, when newspapers blamed
dagga for crime.

It had critics inside the government. Native affairs officials warned that the habit
was deeply rooted, and police were quietly
[told to go easy](/history/south-africa-enforcement-compromise-1922/) in remote African
areas.

## The petition

In 1923 the office of the prime minister, Jan Smuts, petitioned the League's dangerous
drugs committee to add cannabis to the international list of controlled substances.
The South Africans wrote that from their point of view cannabis was "the most important
of all the habit-forming drugs", and that it was missing from the convention.

The League's advisory committee responded by asking governments for information on the
production, use and trade of the drug. Its circular went out in November 1924.

## What it started

That same month the Second Opium Conference opened in Geneva. Cannabis was not on its
agenda either, but the question was now in the air, and on the second day
[Egypt's delegate](/history/geneva-egypt-proposal-1924/) demanded that hashish be
included. South Africa was among the few countries that supported him.

The [convention of 1925](/history/geneva-opium-convention-1925/) was the result. From
that year South Africa began recording dagga arrests and seizures alongside those for
other controlled drugs.

Egypt is usually credited with bringing cannabis into international law. South Africa
asked first. The index lists the request for context; it moved no score for
[South Africa](/country/za/).
