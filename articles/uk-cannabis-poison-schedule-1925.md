Cannabis first came under controls for Britain's general public through poison
law, not dangerous-drugs law. The measure regulated retail sales and labels;
it did not yet create a general prohibition on possession or use.

## A pharmaceutical decision

The Pharmaceutical Society announced in August 1924 that it had resolved to
add cannabis and its resins to the Poisons Schedule. Its published explanation
referred generally to court cases involving hashish and other cannabis
preparations. James H. Mills found no fuller evidentiary case behind the
decision and notes that senior Home Office officials and a Cambridge
pharmacologist had not thought cannabis belonged in the dangerous-drugs law.

Under the procedure then in force, the Privy Council approved additions
recommended by the Society. The cannabis order took effect on 3 April 1925.

## What a legal sale required

Only a chemist could sell a product containing cannabis. The purchaser had to
be known to the seller or accompanied by someone known to the seller. The
chemist recorded the customer and transaction in a poisons register.

Packaging also changed. A container had to identify the drug and state its
proportion to the other ingredients, while displaying the word "POISON" in
red or against a red background. Cannabis medicines and other products did not
disappear, but ordinary retail access became conditional and documented.

This framework differed from the Dangerous Drugs Acts. It supervised the
seller, purchaser and presentation of a legal product instead of placing
cannabis within the criminal controls already applied to opium and cocaine.

## Parliament debates corn plasters

The practical controversy concerned products such as corn plasters, some of
which included cannabis. MPs questioned whether manufacturers and small shops
had received enough time to relabel existing stock. The government declined to
postpone the order, saying the Pharmaceutical Society's resolution had been
made the previous August and interested businesses had received adequate
notice. Mills found no comparable parliamentary debate over the case for
classifying cannabis itself as a poison.

This was Britain's first general-public cannabis control, following the
narrower [Army Council order of 1916](/history/uk-army-council-cannabis-order-1916/).
It was distinct from the [1928 regulations](/history/uk-ban-1928/) that confined
lawful possession and sale to medical and licensed channels.

The index records the 1925 order as context. It moved no score for the
[United Kingdom](/country/gb/) because cannabis remained legally available
through chemists and possession and use were not yet generally prohibited.
