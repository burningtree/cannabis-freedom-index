On 29 August 2013 Deputy Attorney General James M. Cole issued guidance for federal cannabis enforcement following the [legalisation votes in Colorado and Washington](/history/us-colorado-washington-2012/).

The Justice Department deferred challenges to those states’ laws while expecting effective regulation. Federal cannabis offences remained in force.

## Eight priorities

| Federal concern | Scope identified by the memo |
|---|---|
| Minors | Distribution to minors |
| Criminal organisations | Revenue reaching gangs, cartels and other criminal enterprises |
| Diversion | Cannabis moving from authorising states into other states |
| Other offences | State-authorised activity concealing other drugs or illegal conduct |
| Violence | Violence and firearms in cultivation and distribution |
| Public health | Impaired driving and other adverse health consequences |
| Public lands | Cultivation and its associated dangers on public lands |
| Federal property | Possession or use on federal property |

## Regulation and discretion

The memo expected states to fund and enforce their regulatory systems. An operation’s size or commercial character alone should not determine whether it attracted federal attention; prosecutors were to assess cases individually.

Page 4 expressly preserved prosecution authority, created no enforceable rights and supplied no legal defence under federal law. Even an absence of the listed factors did not bar action where an important federal interest justified it.

Boullosa and Wallace treat the 2013 decision as an opening for state experimentation. Their broad political account is best read alongside the memo’s express limits.

## Withdrawal

The guidance was rescinded in January 2018. Colorado’s US Attorney Bob Troyer said his office would continue focusing on the greatest community safety threats under general prosecution principles. That local statement does not establish a permanent nationwide promise against federal enforcement.
