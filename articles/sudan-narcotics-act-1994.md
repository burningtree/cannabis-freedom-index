Sudan's published narcotics statute separates personal consumption from supply and trafficking.
That distinction matters: describing the country solely through capital trafficking penalties
conceals both the personal-use prison term and the law's limited treatment provision.

The Act's heading gives 9 August 1994. Article 2 repeals the Hashish and Opium Act 1924, and
article 3 defines hashish to include cannabis plants and their parts. The UNODC-hosted Arabic
text is a later consolidation: article 18 refers to a 2009 medicines law. Its catalogue attachment
is labelled 1996, which should not be mistaken for a separate enactment date or a guarantee that
this is the unamended 1994 original.

## Personal use and cultivation

Article 20(1) provides up to five years in prison and a fine set by the court for use and for
possession, purchase, import or production intended for personal consumption. It expressly
applies the same penalty to growing plants to extract drugs for one's own use.

Article 20(2) bars prosecution of a dependent user who voluntarily seeks treatment or requests
it before trial. The text does not extend this protection to every consumer or establish how
readily treatment can be obtained. Nor does a maximum of five years tell us the usual sentence.

The index therefore distinguishes personal cultivation from commercial production while
recognising that both remain crimes. Its consumption score reflects an express use offence.
These are corrections to the historical reconstruction following recovery of the legal text;
they do not represent a newly discovered reform in 2026. The precise amendment history of the
available consolidation remains open.

## Gifts and trafficking

Article 16 covers supplying drugs or facilitating access whether payment is involved or not.
Its ordinary penalty is at least ten years and a fine. Preparing or managing a home or other
place for drug consumption or dealing also falls within this provision. Gifts between adults
are not exempt.

Article 15 prescribes life imprisonment and a fine for the listed activities committed with
trafficking intent. Specified aggravations bring the death penalty. Articles 16 and 17 contain
additional capital provisions for aggravated supply and certain international organised-crime
connections. These provisions should not be presented as the ordinary penalty for personal use.

A Supreme Court judgment illustrates the distinction. In a final order dated 16 November 2018,
the court upheld life imprisonment for possession of 657.3 grams of hashish, treating the
quantity as evidence of trafficking intent. It reduced the fine to 10,000 pounds. The judgment,
published in the judiciary's 2019 collection, does not set a universal weight threshold or show
how small personal-use cases are usually handled.

## Licensing and foreign nationals

Articles 12 and 14 permit licensed medical and scientific activities, including scientific
possession of controlled plants and seeds by recognised research institutions. Those general
powers do not demonstrate an operating cannabis patient programme or available medicines.

Under article 22(2), deportation is compulsory for foreign nationals convicted under articles
15 or 16 and discretionary for personal-use offences under article 20(1). Current enforcement,
particularly during the conflict, remains a separate evidence gap.
