New York’s 1860 poison-sales law explicitly included cannabis and its preparations. It regulated transactions through records and labels, with important exceptions and geographical limits. The contemporary legal account therefore supports a history of cannabis regulation before the better-known twentieth-century possession bans.

## Cannabis in a statutory schedule

Francis Tillou’s paper on druggists and poison sales reproduces provisions of chapter 442, passed on 16 April 1860. Section 3 named cannabis at the end of a longer list that also included opium and numerous other substances.

The record concerns that statute as reproduced by a lawyer addressing the Medico-Legal Society in January 1870. The version consulted was printed in the society’s revised collection of papers in 1874. The original 1860 session-law volume has not been independently inspected here.

## Records and labels

The law required a book recording the recipient’s name and residence, together with a witness’s name and residence. A written order or prescription from an authorized physician supplied an exception. The record book was to remain open to inspection.

A separate provision required identifying information and a red-ink poison label when a transaction took place without such a physician’s order. The distinction matters: a description suggesting that every package invariably required the same label would overlook the exception in the reproduced text.

## Limits on the law’s reach

The scheduled provisions excluded wholesale quantities of one pound or more. The statute also limited its application to incorporated cities and villages with populations of at least one thousand. Calling it a uniform rule for every community in New York would therefore overstate its geographical reach.

Violations were misdemeanours punishable by a fine not exceeding fifty dollars. The transaction controls did not amount to a general prohibition on possessing cannabis, nor did they make all sales dependent on a medical prescription.

## A changing regulatory framework

The [1862 amendment](/history/us-new-york-poison-amendment-1862/) removed the witness requirement and repealed the named-substance schedule. Those changes have their own entry, because they altered the original arrangement.

This event appears in the history of the [United States](/country/us/) as a subnational restriction. It adds evidence of nineteenth-century sales regulation without assigning a national score change to a rule whose actual jurisdiction was limited to particular New York municipalities.
