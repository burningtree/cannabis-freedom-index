On 26 January 1965, the world's main drug treaty started applying to the Solomon Islands — extended from London, thirteen years before independence. The United Kingdom's ratification of the Single Convention on Narcotic Drugs carried a territorial notification that expressly named the British Solomon Islands alongside some thirty other territories.[@1]

## Covered from the capital

The protectorate itself signed nothing. As a British territory, its international obligations arrived through the administering power: one British instrument, one list of possessions, and the convention bound them all.[@1] For the Solomons, that meant the treaty's cannabis rules — cannabis and cannabis resin among the controlled drugs, production, trade, possession and use to be limited to medical and scientific purposes — became the islands' international law while the Union flag still flew.[@1,2] Seventeen years would pass before the islands assumed the same obligation under their own flag.[@2]

## Little changed on the ground

The extension confirmed rather than created the local ban. The protectorate's 1941 Dangerous Drugs Regulation already criminalised growing, possessing and passing on Indian hemp, and that framework survived independence into today's Dangerous Drugs Act.[@3] The continuity runs to the section numbers: section 39, the penalties provision the 1949 amendment had tweaked, is still the penalties section of the modern Act, now expressing its fines in penalty units.[@3] The treaty added an international obligation on top of offences the police could already charge.

The Solomons did not arrive alone. The same British notification swept in Fiji, Tonga and the Gilbert and Ellice Islands — essentially the whole British Pacific in one stroke — alongside Caribbean and African territories.[@1] The territory later became the independent Solomon Islands, which put the obligation in its own name with its 1982 succession.[@2]

## Extension, then succession

The British cover lasted until independence in 1978. Four years later, the new state [succeeded to the convention in its own right](/history/solomon-islands-single-convention-1982/), depositing its succession on 17 March 1982.[@2] Between the two dates the obligation never lapsed in practice: the same statute book, the same offences, first under a British notification and then under a Solomon Islands one. The [Solomon Islands profile](/country/sb/) shows where that unbroken line has led.
