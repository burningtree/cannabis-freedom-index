The country best known for tolerating cannabis spent the twenty years before that
jailing people for a single joint.

## Two steps to a ban

The Netherlands [restricted the trade](/history/netherlands-import-ban-1928/) in 1928,
when a revised Opium Act prohibited importing cannabis. Possession and use were not
touched.

In 1953 a further revision made both illegal, with a maximum of four years in prison.

## How it was applied

Harshly, and to few people. After 1953 police raids and eight-month prison sentences
for minimal amounts, as little as one marijuana cigarette, became the norm.
[Amsterdam's narcotics section](/history/netherlands-amsterdam-narcotics-section-1947/)
consisted of two detectives until 1966. In 1962 the police reported only a handful of
drug seizures in the whole country.

## What broke it

Numbers. From 1964 offences against the Opium Act rose every year, and from
[1967](/history/netherlands-cannabis-offences-surge-1967/) most of them concerned
cannabis: 76 per cent that year, 86 per cent by 1972. Estimates put drug users at 2 to
3 per cent of the population by 1970, and far higher among the young in Amsterdam.

Two commissions, [Hulsman](/history/netherlands-hulsman-commission-1971/) and
[Baan](/history/netherlands-baan-commission-1972/), recommended taking cannabis use
out of the reach of the criminal courts.

## The reversal

In [1976](/history/netherlands-toleration-1976/) the Opium Act was revised again.
Possession of up to 30 grams became a minor offence that prosecutors would not pursue.
The 1953 offence was never repealed. The Netherlands stopped enforcing it.

In the index the 1953 amendment is the country's sharpest fall: from a state in which
only the trade was restricted to full prohibition. The [Netherlands](/country/nl/)
recovered part of that in 1976 and has scored between the two ever since.
