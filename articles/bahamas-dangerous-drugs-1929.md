On 27 February 1929 the Bahamas passed a Dangerous Drugs Act dealing with drug offences including cannabis.[@1] The colony could hardly know it was writing the frame for the next ninety-five years: the Act survived every revision of the statute book and still underpins Bahamian cannabis law today.[@1,2]

## An act that kept being revised

The 1929 Act lived on as Chapter 12 in the 1938-39 revised laws, and later as Chapter 44.[@1,2] Under it the Governor in Council made detailed rules: 1952 dispensing rules forced every druggist to keep a narcotics register — date, patient, quantity, prescribing physician — and to lock all narcotics in a special cupboard, while companion sale rules confined selling to licensed druggists.[@2]

The Act grew by amendment rather than replacement.[@3] A 1959 amendment rewrote the possession offence: anyone found with a covered drug without proper authority, or anyone owning premises where it was kept, was guilty unless they proved it had been deposited there without their knowledge or consent.[@3] The same year's machinery showed how the net widened — an Order in Council extended Part IV of the Act to normethadone at the United Nations' prompting, the same extension power Barbados would use for cannabis preparations.[@3]

## Indian hemp defined, then deleted

In 1962 Act 26 gave Indian hemp a full legal definition: every part of the Cannabis sativa plant whether growing or not, the resin from any part of it, and every compound, mixture or preparation of plant or resin.[@1] The same amendment added a conspiracy offence, punishing agreements to commit drug offences as the offences themselves even where the other party sat outside the colony's courts.[@1]

The definition lasted sixty years.[@1] In 2023 it was deleted from the Dangerous Drugs Act along with every other reference to Indian hemp, and a Cannabis Bill set the framework for legal medicinal and religious use instead.[@1]

## A new act beside the old one

The [Cannabis Act of 2024](/history/bahamas-cannabis-act-2024/) received assent that July, laying out medical and religious access with licences and a $250 fixed penalty for possession under 30 grams — but with different provisions commencing on minister-appointed dates, so enactment alone changed little on the ground.[@4] Licensing was still delayed into 2026.[@4] From the 1929 Act through Chapter 44 to the dual regime of today, the Bahamas never repealed its first drug law — it built around it, and the 1929 commencement remains the base layer under [the country's](/country/bs/) scores.[@1,4]
