In the 1840s the most advanced medical profession in Europe took hashish seriously as a
drug. The man who made it respectable was a Paris psychiatrist who had first tried it
in Cairo.

## The traveller

Jacques-Joseph Moreau de Tours trained at the Salpêtrière under Esquirol, who in the
mid-1830s sent him to accompany a wealthy patient on a long journey to the East. In
Cairo a French doctor, Louis-Rémy Aubert-Roche, introduced him to hashish.

He recorded the experience closely. At the start, he wrote, "while preserving the most
perfect consciousness of oneself, one feels as if carried away in a dream". He felt he
had "fallen asleep without ceasing being awake".

## The idea

Moreau belonged to the school that held insanity to be a disorder of the brain,
treatable with medicine like any other. Hashish seemed to offer something no doctor had
had before: a way to produce the symptoms of madness, in a sane person, for a few hours.
A physician could study delirium from the inside.

Du Hachisch et de l'Aliénation Mentale appeared in the autumn of 1845, describing the
intoxication as a "powerful and unique means of exploration" of mental illness.

His opponents, mostly Catholic physicians who saw insanity as a moral disorder, said
hashish only made people irrational and violent, and wanted it prohibited throughout
the French empire.

## The reception

The book won an honourable mention from the Academy of Medicine in 1846. The American
Journal of Insanity called the discovery of much importance to the civilised world.
German psychiatrists discussed it at their congress in Kiel that September and reported
trials of their own, and reviews appeared in Milan.

Between 1840 and 1860 dozens of French pharmacists and physicians staked careers on
cannabis medicines. A dose of hashish tincture cost about as much as a bottle of cheap
wine.

## The flaw in it

Moreau's science rested on an assumption he never examined. He took the effects of
hashish to explain what he saw as the irrationality of the Orient, citing
[Sacy's assassins](/history/france-sacy-assassins-lecture-1809/) and attributing Arab
belief in genies to habitual use. The medicine and the prejudice came as a pair, and
when the medicine fell out of use the prejudice remained.
