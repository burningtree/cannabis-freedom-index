A United Nations survey dated 30 March 1955 recorded 285 cannabis prosecutions in Southern Rhodesia in 1952. Of those prosecuted, 282 were Africans under the colony’s racial classifications. The survey described a system in which police destroyed small crops, traffickers usually went to prison and smokers were generally offered a fine.

## Cannabis grew beside homes and food crops

The report described cannabis, known locally as dagga or mbanje, growing in small patches in gardens and near homesteads. It said cultivation usually met the grower’s own needs, although larger crops supplied some trade.

Growers sometimes concealed the plants among maize. Police patrolled areas reserved for Africans, destroyed cannabis plants they found and prosecuted growers. Chiefs were required to prevent cultivation in their areas.

These descriptions came from a UN compilation drawing on colonial government information and earlier annual reports. The survey characterised habitual users chiefly as elderly African men and described established practices of smoking dried leafy material.

## Prosecutions fell overwhelmingly on Africans

The 1952 table divided defendants into the colony’s racial categories: 282 Africans, two people classified as Coloured and one European. The survey also reported 310 prosecutions in 1953, involving 308 men and two women.

It described relatively small quantities and little organisation in the trade. Courts generally punished traffickers more heavily than smokers, and younger men more heavily than older men. Quantity and previous history affected the sentence.

For smokers, the reported ordinary practice was to offer a fine. Traffickers usually received imprisonment.

## The law allowed severe penalties and medical exceptions

The [1952 law](/history/southern-rhodesia-pharmacy-poisons-act-1952/) allowed up to ten years in prison, a £1,000 fine, or both for narcotics offences. Special rules treated inadvertent bookkeeping and prescription offences differently.

Possession was restricted to authorised people and those holding the required certificate, order or prescription. Medical practitioners, pharmacists and other specified professionals could handle controlled products within that system. The survey said medical and veterinary use of cannabis was very rare.

Growing required a licence, and the survey reported that none had been issued in recent years.

## Later production licensing had a long legal background

The colony’s [1956 consolidated drug law](/history/southern-rhodesia-dangerous-drugs-act-1956/) followed this period. Decades later, [Zimbabwe’s 2018 regulations](/history/zimbabwe-medical-2018/) created a detailed licensing system for medicinal and scientific cannabis production.

The 1955 survey preserves an earlier picture of [Zimbabwe’s](/country/zw/) cannabis history: small household crops, established smoking practices, limited medical exceptions and prosecution concentrated on the African population.
