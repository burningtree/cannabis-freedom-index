The US Virgin Islands Supreme Court ruled on 21 November 2012 that eligible first offenders placed on probation without a conviction could keep their driving privileges. It reversed a two-year licence suspension imposed on lawyer Lee J. Rohn following a cannabis-possession plea.[@1]

## Cannabis in luggage led to a plea

The case began after cannabis was found in Rohn’s checked luggage at St. Croix’s airport. Prosecutors charged possession with intent to distribute in 2003. Years later, the parties agreed that she would plead to simple possession, with prosecutors recommending the territory’s special first-offender probation procedure.[@1]

At the plea hearing, the judge warned that a guilty plea would also cost Rohn her licence for two years. Both Rohn and the prosecution argued that this penalty did not apply when a defendant received probation without a conviction.[@1]

## The trial judge treated the driving ban separately

In an [April 2011 ruling](/history/usvi-first-offender-driving-ruling-2011/), the Superior Court held that the licence penalty applied even to this group. It reasoned that revocation was a civil consequence operating separately from the criminal proceedings. The judge also upheld the law’s constitutionality.[@2]

The underlying driving statute imposed two years without a licence for simple possession and five years for other drug offences. Its text referred both to guilty pleas and convictions, which made the consequence of a plea central to the dispute.[@3]

The judge eventually placed Rohn on six months’ probation without adjudicating her guilty. Her driving suspension was held pending appeal, and the possession proceedings were dismissed after she completed probation.[@1]

## The Supreme Court protected probation without conviction

The Supreme Court read the two laws together. The first-offender procedure required probation without a judgment of guilt and said a successful discharge was not a conviction. That special protection prevailed over the general licence-revocation scheme.[@1]

The court therefore reversed both the trial judge’s interpretation and the part of Rohn’s later sentence suspending her driving privileges. The result protected defendants who qualified for that probation procedure from this additional penalty.[@1]

## The dispute reached beyond driving under the influence

Rohn’s challenge concerned the consequences of a possession plea following an airport incident. The trial court had defended licence revocation as a way to deter movement of drugs and protect road safety, even where the offence involved no impaired driving.[@2]

The appeal established how the first-offender route constrained that wider penalty. It forms part of the [US Virgin Islands timeline](/country/vi/#history), linking the [1971 drug law](/history/usvi-controlled-substances-law-1971/) to later disputes over how possession defendants were punished.
