On 1 September 1939 Ordinance XXXI brought Malta a consolidated dangerous-drugs law with a part of its own for cannabis. Its Part III banned importing or exporting cannabis resin and made it an offence to possess, produce, sell, deal in or cultivate the plant — medicinal preparations excepted, and transit through Malta left alone.[@1,2]

## A colonial consolidation

The ordinance set out to amend and consolidate the law on importing, exporting, making, selling and using opium and other dangerous drugs, and it has been amended ever since: the consolidation lists amending acts and legal notices from 1963 to 2025.[@2] Its cannabis sections, articles 7 and 8, were rewritten in 1980 and 2021, so the text on screen is not the 1939 wording — but the shape survived: resin at the border, the plant in the possession and cultivation offences.[@2]

## Growing drew a mandatory sentence

Growing later carried a mandatory prison sentence, even for a single plant.[@1] The present text shows how: cultivation under article 8(c), unless the court is satisfied it was for the offender's exclusive use, draws at least six months' imprisonment before the magistrates — while trafficking-tier cultivation draws at least four years before the higher court, up to life.[@2] Those floors reflect later amendments rather than the 1939 wording, whose substitution notes stand beside the articles.[@2]

## What came next

The ordinance stayed Malta's framework through decades of amendments, visible in the consolidation's long amendment list.[@2] Malta's current law is on [its profile](/country/mt/).
