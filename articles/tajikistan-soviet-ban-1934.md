Soviet restrictions on sowing Indian hemp took effect from 1935, including in the Tajik republic. The decree allowed crops intended exclusively for medical and scientific needs, while directing that unauthorised sowing bring criminal responsibility and compulsory confiscation of the crop.[@1]

## A Union decree controlled the crop

The Soviet Central Executive Committee and Council of People’s Commissars signed the decree in October 1934. It covered both opium poppy and Indian hemp and set its commencement for the following year. Mikhail Kalinin, Vyacheslav Molotov and Avel Yenukidze signed the published text.[@1]

The rule linked sowing to official permission and limited the purposes for which the crop could be authorised. For the opium-poppy crop, the decree also described central planning, regional allocation and accounting arrangements. Its criminal-liability clause expressly covered unauthorised sowing of either poppy or Indian hemp.[@1]

## Confiscation accompanied criminal liability

The decree required confiscation of unauthorised crops and instructed the republics to make corresponding additions to their criminal codes. Enforcement therefore involved both taking the plants and bringing the unauthorised grower within criminal law.[@1]

The medical and scientific exception sat within that control system. It tied permission to the crop’s intended purpose, with the decree requiring those needs to be its exclusive destination. Growing Indian hemp outside the permitted route exposed the crop to confiscation.[@1]

## Independent Tajikistan later replaced its criminal code

Tajikistan introduced [a replacement Criminal Code in September 1998](/history/tajikistan-criminal-code-1998/). Its commencement instruments repealed the code adopted in 1961 and the amendments made to that earlier code. They also provided for reconsidering sentences where the new law removed criminal liability or reduced punishment.[@2]

A [general drug law followed in December 1999](/history/tajikistan-narcotics-law-1999/). In its later consolidated text, scientific cultivation of drug-containing plants was reserved for licensed research institutions. Production and handling of controlled drugs were subject to licences and state quotas.[@3]

## Scientific growing remained a controlled activity

The later drug law defined cultivation as sowing and growing controlled plants. Alongside its scientific route, the Criminal Code retained an offence for unauthorised cultivation, divided into quantity bands.[@3,2]
