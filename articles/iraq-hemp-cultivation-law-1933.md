In May 1933 the Kingdom of Iraq made growing cannabis a crime for the first time: anyone cultivating Indian hemp or opium poppy faced having the crop uprooted under official supervision and being billed for the work.[@1] A first offence meant a fine of up to ten dinars, while repeat growers risked up to three months in prison, a fine of up to twenty dinars, or both — and anyone who knew of such a crop had to report it.[@1]

## Iraq's first drug law targeted the crop, not the user

The 1933 measure is described in Iraqi legal scholarship as the country's first drug law, opening a national criminal policy against drugs built on criminal law and enforced by police, customs and border guards.[@2] It did not regulate possession, sale or pharmacy trade; it went after cultivation itself, ordering destruction of the plants where they stood.[@1,2] The law took effect on publication in the official gazette on 8 May 1933, a week after signature.[@1]

The approach was agricultural as much as criminal: uprooting happened under agricultural supervision, and the grower paid the cost.[@1] The duty to report extended liability beyond growers to neighbours and landowners who stayed silent.[@1]

## A broader drug law followed within five years

The cultivation ban stood alone only briefly. In 1938 Iraq passed a wider Dangerous and Narcotic Drugs Law that restricted possession of cannabis and its resin to authorised professionals and prescription channels, with licences, permits and registers for the legal trade.[@2]

The [1938 law](/history/iraq-dangerous-drugs-law-1938/) covered what the 1933 ban had left untouched: buying, selling, import and pharmacy supply rather than the field.[@2]

## Both early laws fell to the 1965 consolidation

The 1965 Narcotics Law repealed the 1933 cultivation ban by name, alongside the 1938 law and their amendments, and it took effect on publication in the gazette.[@3] It kept unauthorised growing restricted but introduced an exception the 1933 ban had never allowed: supervised crops grown exclusively for fibre and seeds.[@2]

That consolidation closed the kingdom-era chapter of Iraqi cannabis control, moving from a crop-destruction order to a licensing system. The [1965 law](/history/iraq-narcotics-law-1965/) itself anchored [Iraq](/country/iq/) drug policy for decades, until later laws rewrote the penalties again.
