Mozambique's new drug law took effect on 13 March 1997, raising ordinary trafficking penalties to sixteen to twenty years while giving unauthorised cannabis growers a separate sentence of three days to one year. Using cannabis or possessing it for oneself carried up to two years plus a fine, with possible waivers for occasional users.

## Replacing Portugal's drug law

The law repealed the [Portuguese legislation extended to Mozambique in 1970](/history/mozambique-portuguese-drug-law-1970/). That earlier law had prescribed two to eight years for a broad range of unauthorised dealings, including growing and selling, and up to two years for personal use or supply without profit.

Parliament said the replacement would put principles of the 1988 UN trafficking convention into domestic law and strengthen the response to illicit drugs. It also created a central office to coordinate prevention and drug control. President Joaquim Chissano promulgated the law after parliament had approved it in December 1996.

## Growers and sellers faced different sentences

The general trafficking offence covered selling, giving, transporting and other dealings in controlled drugs, including cannabis and its resin and oil. Its sixteen-to-twenty-year sentence sat alongside a lesser offence for small quantities or substantially less serious circumstances, carrying two to eight years plus a fine.

Cannabis cultivation received its own rule: three days to one year for growing without authorisation. Growing for personal use carried up to one year or a fine. Courts could waive punishment for occasional users under that personal-growing rule.

## Three days' supply mattered

For consumption and personal possession, the ordinary maximum was two years in prison, together with a fine. When the amount exceeded three days' average individual consumption, the law specified a minimum of one year plus a fine.

Occasional users could receive a waiver under the ordinary personal-use rule. A separate exemption for minors required that they had not reoffended, promised a magistrate they would stop, and accepted treatment if dependent. People seeking treatment voluntarily were promised anonymity, while courts could attach treatment to suspended sentences for dependent offenders.

## The distinction reached the Supreme Court

The gap between ordinary and small-scale trafficking had substantial consequences. In [May 2024](https://tracker.cannabisfreedom.fyi/report/mozambique-cannabis-seller-sentence-reduced-2024/), the Supreme Court reviewed a seventeen-year sentence for a Maputo seller found with 590 grams of cannabis.

The judges treated his direct sales, simple methods and lack of an organisation as small-scale trafficking and imposed six years instead. The case showed how much the classification of an offence could change the punishment under [Mozambique's drug law](/country/mz/).
