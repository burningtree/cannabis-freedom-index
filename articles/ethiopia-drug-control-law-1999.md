Ethiopia's drug law of June 1999 exposed people who allowed illicit narcotic
drug use on their premises to seven to fifteen years in prison and a fine.
It also punished promoting drug abuse and supervised
the licensed medicine trade.

## Hosting carried a much heavier penalty

The [earlier penal code](/history/ethiopia-penal-code-1957/) had already
punished knowingly providing a place for drug use, even in private. Its
ordinary penalty began at three months in prison, with heavier punishment
available in specified cases.

The new law put an owner or occupier who caused or permitted illicit
narcotic drug use in a house, compound or other place within a separate
offence carrying seven to fifteen years and a fine of 30,000–50,000 birr.
It made providing the setting for consumption a serious offence in its own
right.

The same penalty covered publishing or displaying material that promoted
or encouraged drug abuse. Administering narcotic drugs without a prescribing
licence, prescribing without sufficient reason or above the standard dose,
and supplying against a prescription known to be forged, altered, cancelled
or expired were also covered.

## A new authority supervised controlled medicines

The government said it wanted both safe, effective medicines and a system
that deterred illicit drug production, trafficking and use. The law created
a Drug Administration and Control Authority to oversee that work.

Its definition of narcotic drugs included drugs controlled by the UN
conventions Ethiopia had ratified. Cannabis therefore fell within the
treaty-based definition following Ethiopia's
[accession to the Single Convention](/history/ethiopia-single-convention-1965/).

Importing, exporting, manufacturing or distributing narcotic drugs required
a special licence. Each import or export shipment also needed its own
permit, valid for ninety days. The law prohibited sending these drugs
through the post or by ship.

## Prescriptions and stocks had to be controlled

Only registered practitioners with a special licence could prescribe
narcotic drugs. Prescriptions needed special paper, and practitioners could
not prescribe controlled drugs for themselves.

Stocks and their invoices, registers and prescriptions had to be kept in a
locked metal cupboard or special room, with the key held by the authorised
professional. Businesses had to keep records and report to the authority.
Property used in or derived from the specified offences was liable to
confiscation.

## Later laws replaced the code and the medicine controls

In 2005, Ethiopia [replaced its older penal code](/history/ethiopia-criminal-code-2005/)
with rules distinguishing trafficking from private use. The replacement
preserved special criminal laws. In January 2010, a
[wider health law](/history/ethiopia-drug-permits-health-law-2010/)
repealed the 1999 law while retaining controlled-drug permits and the
seven-to-fifteen-year penalty for advertising that encouraged drug abuse.
[Ethiopia's drug controls](/country/et/) continued through successive laws
governing both illicit supply and the medicine trade.
