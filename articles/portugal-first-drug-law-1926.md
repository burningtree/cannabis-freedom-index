On 27 August 1926 Portugal folded the international opium conventions into
domestic law, restricting the import and sale of narcotics, cannabis
included.[@1] It was the country's first cannabis restriction — and then, for
almost forty years, nothing followed.[@2]

## An empire that already knew the plant

Portugal had met cannabis centuries earlier in Goa, taken in 1510: the
physician Garcia da Orta described its Indian uses in 1534, and a 1919
glossary still recorded Goans smoking or chewing bhang, which "intoxicates
like opium".[@3] The Portuguese carried the plant to Brazil, where Rio de
Janeiro banned cannabis from the city streets as early as 1830 — punishing
its use by enslaved Africans.[@3]

## Copying the conventions

Portugal had begun adapting its framework to the 1912 Hague Opium Convention
with a 1924 law; Decree-Law 12,210 of August 1926 completed the job.[@1] Like
most early European drug laws, the decree worked at the borders and the
pharmacy counter: it controlled imports and sales rather than punishing
users, and cannabis entered the schedules as one narcotic among others.[@1]
Nothing in the 1920s framework made the user a criminal; Portugal waited
until 1970 for a law that did.[@2]
The European drugs agency EMCDDA dates Portuguese drug legislation to this
1920s adaptation — and notes that no other illicit-drug legislation followed
for almost four decades, until drug addiction earned a passing mention in the
1963 mental-health law.[@2]

## The forty-year silence

That silence lasted until drug use became visible as a social problem in the
1970s.[@2] The [major update of 1970](/history/portugal-use-criminalised-1970/)
finally defined narcotic products, set up schedules, and built a punishment
regime: personal possession could draw up to two years in prison or a fine,
traffickers faced two to eight years, and consumption that endangered others
or encouraged them drew six months to two years.[@1,2] Portugal ratified the
1961 Single Convention the next year and opened its first addiction treatment
service in 1973.[@1]

## What came next

The 1970 framework survived the 1974 revolution and was reworked in 1983 —
harsher against trafficking, softer on consumption, to push users toward
treatment — and again in 1993.[@1] The decisive break came in 2001, when
Portugal took drug use out of criminal law and sent users before dissuasion
commissions instead of courts; medical cannabis followed in 2018.[@1] The
[Portugal profile](/country/pt/) follows that arc from the 1926 decree to
the commissions.
