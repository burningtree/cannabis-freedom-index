Iraq sharply increased drug penalties at the end of 1968, exposing specified
trafficking offenders to death or lifelong hard labour. Cannabis growers and
suppliers were covered, while people possessing or growing for their own use
faced a separate increase to three to ten years in prison plus a fine.

## The previous penalties were already severe

Under the [1967 amendment](/history/iraq-drug-penalties-1967/), unauthorised
personal possession or growing carried one to five years in prison and a fine
of 100–500 dinars. Supplying drugs, including giving them away, and growing
for trade could bring a term of hard labour. Customs and drug-control
officials faced lifelong hard labour for the specified supply offences.

The government's explanation for the next amendment said those penalties
were no longer deterring smugglers and other offenders. It presented stronger
punishment as a way to stop drugs entering and spreading through society.
The new penalties took effect when published on 30 December 1968.

## Trafficking could lead to lifelong hard labour or death

The principal trafficking offences now carried lifelong hard labour and a
fine of 3,000–10,000 dinars. They included supplying drugs, holding them for
trade, and growing cannabis for trade. The list also covered moving the
specified drug plants or their seeds for that purpose.

Death or lifelong hard labour became possible for repeat offenders in the
specified import, production and supply group. The same alternatives applied
to leaders of groups committing the listed trafficking offences and to
customs officials and other public employees with drug-related responsibilities.
The wording reached both organised supply and officials whose work connected
them to controlled drugs.

## People growing for themselves faced longer imprisonment

For unauthorised possession or growing intended for personal use, the minimum
rose from one year to three and the maximum from five years to ten. The fine
increased to 500–1,000 dinars.

The distinction between supplying others and personal use therefore remained
central to the punishment. Both routes now exposed offenders to substantially
longer confinement than the previous amendment had provided.

## Later reforms widened punishment and introduced limited treatment

The [1979 reform](/history/iraq-drug-treatment-hemp-reform-1979/) raised the
personal-use maximum again, to fifteen years. It also let courts order six
months of treatment instead for people whose dependence arose from a medical
condition, with earlier release supported by medical evidence of recovery.

In [2002](/history/iraq-drug-trafficking-death-2002/), death or life imprisonment
became the penalty across the principal trafficking list, alongside property
confiscation. Capital punishment was then
[suspended in 2003](/history/iraq-death-penalty-suspension-2003/), before a
[2004 order](/history/iraq-drug-death-penalty-return-2004/) restored it for
drug trafficking aimed at financing or assisting offences against the government.
These amendments shaped [Iraq's drug law](/country/iq/) until the
[2017 replacement](/history/iraq-narcotics-law-2017/).
