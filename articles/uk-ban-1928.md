Britain brought cannabis into its dangerous-drugs system in September 1928. The change followed the 1925 Geneva convention and passed through Parliament with little debate, despite Britain having no comparable domestic cannabis problem.

## A treaty-driven bill

The Dangerous Drugs Act 1925 was designed principally to let Britain ratify the international agreements concluded at Geneva. Introducing the bill in the House of Lords, the government described its provisions as uncontroversial and said its first clause would extend existing dangerous-drugs law to coca leaves and hemp.

The international convention itself imposed limited controls on cannabis trade. It required import certificates where cannabis remained lawful and prohibited exports to countries that had banned it. It did not require governments to prohibit domestic production, trade or consumption.

## Controls arrive in 1928

The 1925 legislation and accompanying regulations took effect three years later. Cannabis, then described in law as Indian hemp, entered a framework that controlled importation, exportation, manufacture, sale and possession. Access was confined to authorised medical channels rather than an ordinary retail market.

The Transnational Institute notes that Britain's domestic restrictions went beyond the Geneva convention. Its history also finds an absence of significant cannabis-related problems in the European countries that adopted bans after the treaty.

Cultivation was an important limit to the 1928 change. Growing the cannabis plant was not made a specific offence at this point. Parliament added that offence in [1964](/history/uk-cultivation-offence-1964/), closing a gap that the original regime had left.

## A durable foundation

The ban established the basis on which later British cannabis policing developed. Subsequent laws consolidated and expanded control, while the Misuse of Drugs Act 1971 eventually replaced the earlier framework and created the modern classification system.

Medical cannabis did not return to lawful prescription under the modern regime until [2018](/history/uk-medical-cannabis-2018/).

## What moved in the index

The change cut the [United Kingdom's](/country/gb/) possession, sharing and consumption scores from 10 to 1, enforcement from 10 to 4 and access from 8 to 0. Cultivation fell from 10 to 3 because growing was not yet directly prohibited. The score fell sharply even though the treaty behind the law required a narrower system of trade controls.
