Niger's pharmacy law, issued on 10 January 1997, kept narcotic medicines behind prescriptions. It also required authorisation before medicines could be sold or supplied free, placing the product and the way it reached a patient under separate controls.

## The schedules came from earlier national rules

The law referred directly to the [1977 implementing decree](/history/niger-poisonous-substance-schedules-1977/) made under the [1974 ordinance](/history/niger-national-drug-regulation-1974/) on poisonous substances and narcotics. Its tables provided the basis for three controlled lists in the pharmacy legislation.

Medicines and poisonous substances from table A entered list I, those from table C entered list II, and those from table B entered the narcotics list. Medicines on all three lists could be dispensed only on prescription.

The law also adopted the narcotic and psychotropic lists of international conventions ratified by Niger. It left detailed rules on authorised prescribers and the prescribing and dispensing of controlled substances to decrees.

## Medicines needed permission to reach the market

A pharmaceutical speciality, generic medicine or comparable product needed marketing authorisation from the health minister before it could be sold or supplied without charge. This control concerned the medicine itself, alongside the prescription needed to dispense a controlled product.

Breaching that authorisation rule carried ten days to six months, a fine of 100,000–500,000 CFA francs, or both. Authorities could close an establishment temporarily, and a court could prolong closure or make it permanent.

The distinction between a permitted medicine and an authorised supplier mattered throughout the law. It applied to free distribution as well as commercial sales.

## Pharmacists had defined dispensing responsibilities

In pharmacies, national hospitals, departmental hospital centres and private hospitals equipped with pharmacists, pharmacists alone could prepare and dispense pharmaceutical products. Medicine depots and facilities without pharmacists had their own rules for authorised health workers.

The law also addressed substitution of medicines by pharmacists and retained special controls over narcotics. These provisions placed responsibility on identified professionals and establishments when medicines passed to the public.

## The later drug ordinance addressed illicit supplies

The [1999 drug ordinance](/history/niger-drug-ordinance-1999/) replaced the older national drug ordinance and its implementing decree. It explicitly prohibited cannabis cultivation and set separate penalties for small personal possession, purchase and growing.

That later law also prohibited using controlled drugs outside a medical prescription. The pharmacy rules and illicit-drug offences formed connected parts of [Niger's](/country/ne/) controls: one regulated medicines and their dispensing, while the other set punishments for unauthorised activities.
