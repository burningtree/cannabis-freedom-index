In 1942 the United States government made a film urging farmers to grow the plant it
had spent the previous five years taxing out of legal trade.

## The law it sat beside

The [Marihuana Tax Act](/history/us-marihuana-tax-act-1937/) of 1937 did not ban
cannabis outright. It taxed and registered every transaction so heavily that legal
supply became impractical.

## Why the government changed its mind

Industrial fibres, many of them imported, ran short during the war. After Japan took
the Philippines the supply was cut off, and the navy needed rope. The Department of
Agriculture and the army urged farmers to plant hemp. Tax stamps were issued under
the 1937 Act, which was eased for the purpose but never amended, and about 400,000
acres were grown between 1942 and 1945.

## The film

The USDA National Agricultural Library catalogues *Hemp For Victory* as a
black-and-white sound film of 14 minutes and 13 seconds, dated circa 1942. It describes
hemp growing and harvesting, and the manufacture of fibre and coarse cloth for military
applications. This official record establishes the film's existence and subject.

## Herer's account of its disappearance

Herer reproduces the film's narration in Chapter 9 and describes its disappearance
from official records elsewhere in the book. Wikipedia recounts copies reaching the
Library of Congress in 1989 and a National Archives recovery in 1990. The USDA catalogue
does not establish those later events or explain why earlier researchers reportedly
could not locate the film. They should remain a separately sourced rediscovery account,
not proof of deliberate suppression.

The episode moved no score for the [United States](/country/us/): encouraging licensed
fibre production did not give adults permission to grow recreational cannabis.
