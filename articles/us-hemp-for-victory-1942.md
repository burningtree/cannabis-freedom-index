In 1942 the United States government made a film urging farmers to grow the plant it
had spent the previous five years taxing out of legal trade.

## The law it sat beside

The [Marihuana Tax Act](/history/us-marihuana-tax-act-1937/) of 1937 did not ban
cannabis outright. It taxed and registered every transaction so heavily that legal
supply became impractical.

## Why the government changed its mind

Industrial fibres, many of them imported, ran short during the war. After Japan took
the Philippines the supply was cut off, and the navy needed rope. The Department of
Agriculture and the army urged farmers to plant hemp. Tax stamps were issued under
the 1937 Act, which was eased for the purpose but never amended, and about 400,000
acres were grown between 1942 and 1945.

## The film

*Hemp for Victory* is a black-and-white film of about 14 minutes, produced by the
Department of Agriculture and directed by Raymond Evans. It tells the history of hemp,
shows how to grow and harvest it and how it becomes rope, cloth and cordage, and
borrows scenes from the 1926 silent film *Old Ironsides*. Its message to farmers was
to grow as much as they could.

When the war ended, so did the exception.

## The film that did not exist

For decades afterwards the film was almost unknown. The Department of Agriculture's
library and the Library of Congress told people who asked that no such film had been
made by any branch of the government.

A copy had survived on tape. The activist Jack Herer was given it in 1984, and on 19
May 1989 he and two others donated VHS copies to the Library of Congress. In May 1990
John Birrenbach recovered a copy from the National Archives, the first to come from a
government source. The film is now in the public domain and can be downloaded from
the National Archives.

The episode moved no score for the [United States](/country/us/): the tax law stayed
as it was, and only licensed fibre growing was encouraged.
