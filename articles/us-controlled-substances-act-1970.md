In 1970 the United States put marijuana in the same legal category as heroin. The
official who proposed it meant it as a placeholder, to last until a commission had
studied the question. The commission reported in 1972. Most of the placeholder is
still there.

## A law needed in a hurry

In [1969](/history/us-leary-1969/) the Supreme Court struck down the
[Marihuana Tax Act](/history/us-marihuana-tax-act-1937/), the basis of federal
cannabis law since 1937. President Nixon's administration was already preparing a
comprehensive drug measure.

It moved quickly and with almost no opposition. The bill passed the House on
24 September 1970 by 341 votes to 6 and the Senate on 7 October by 54 to 0. Nixon
signed it on 27 October.

## Five schedules

The Act sorts drugs into five schedules by two tests: potential for abuse, and
accepted medical use. Schedule I is for drugs with a high potential for abuse, "no
currently accepted medical use" and no accepted safety under medical supervision.

Marijuana went there, with heroin. The
[recommendation](/history/us-egeberg-recommendation-1970/) came from Roger Egeberg,
the assistant secretary of health, who advised placing it in Schedule I temporarily,
until the commission created by the same Act had reported.

In one respect the law was milder than what it replaced. It abolished the mandatory
minimum sentences of the [1950s](/history/us-narcotic-control-act-1956/) and made
simple possession a misdemeanour.

## The commission

Nixon appointed the thirteen members, with a former governor of Pennsylvania,
Raymond Shafer, in the chair. Their report, "Marihuana: A Signal of
Misunderstanding", was presented on [22 March 1972](/history/us-shafer-commission-1972/).

It found that users were not the dangerous figures of public belief but "more timid,
drowsy and passive", and that cannabis did not threaten society. It recommended
ending criminal penalties for possession. Shafer told Congress:

> The criminal law is too harsh a tool to apply to personal possession even in the
> effort to discourage use.

Nixon had answered in advance. "Even if the commission does recommend that it be
legalized, I will not follow that recommendation." Neither he nor Congress acted.

## What followed

The states did. [Oregon](/history/us-oregon-decriminalisation-1973/) decriminalised
possession in 1973 and ten more followed by 1978. Federal law went the other way:
mandatory minimums returned in [1986](/history/us-anti-drug-abuse-act-1986/).

Schedule I proved hard to leave. In [1988](/history/us-dea-judge-young-1988/) the
drug agency's own judge said cannabis should be moved, and was overruled. Only in
[2026](/history/us-medical-schedule-iii-2026/) did state-licensed medical cannabis
move to Schedule III.

In the index the Act is recorded as a slight easing for the
[United States](/country/us/): enforcement rose from 0 to 2 with the end of
mandatory prison, while every other score stayed where the 1950s had left it.
