A fruit-fly inspection at Saipan airport led to a cannabis conviction that survived a search challenge in February 1987. The court ruled that agricultural quarantine officers could inspect Daniel Atalig's boxes without a warrant or his consent and that the cannabis they found could be used as evidence.

## A quarantine led inspectors to the boxes

The Department of Natural Resources had imposed an emergency quarantine in June 1981 after fruit flies were discovered on Rota. It prohibited fruit and vegetable shipments from Rota to other points in the Commonwealth, and inspectors began examining cargo arriving from that island.

Atalig flew from Rota to Saipan on 12 September. At the inspection area, an officer checked that he had boarded on Rota and asked him to open two boxes. Inside were deer meat and five pounds of cannabis.

## Atalig challenged both the search and the lack of a jury

He was charged with possession under the Trust Territory Code. After the trial court rejected his requests for a jury and for exclusion of the cannabis, he entered a no-contest plea while reserving the right to appeal those rulings.

His sentence was one year in prison, suspended except for the first thirty days, and a thousand-dollar fine.

An initial appeal succeeded on the jury question, but the Ninth Circuit reversed that decision. The case then returned to the search issue, leading to the February 1987 ruling.

## The court treated the inspection as agricultural protection

The appellate judges relied on the exception allowing administrative inspections for quarantine purposes. They stressed the authorised programme, the lack of individual discretion about whom to inspect and the delay that obtaining warrants would cause.

Atalig did not argue that the quarantine order was invalid or that it concealed a criminal investigation. The trial court had found that his cargo was searched because he arrived from Rota.

The judges also rejected his argument that the inspection should have happened before departure. Checking on arrival in Saipan was constitutionally permissible, they held.

## Cannabis enforcement continued through other routes

The decision left the cannabis evidence available against Atalig. Years later, a [federal assessment in 2003](/history/cnmi-eradication-imports-assessment-2003/) described crop destruction, undercover purchases and border interception as parts of cannabis enforcement.

The [Northern Mariana Islands' 2018 legalisation](/history/cnmi-cannabis-act-2018/) later allowed capped adult possession and home cultivation. The [current profile](/country/mp/) describes those permissions, alongside the continuing limits on transporting cannabis between islands.
