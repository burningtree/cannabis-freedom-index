Bangladesh's 2002 narcotics amendment set different investigation deadlines depending on how a suspect was caught.[@1] Where someone was caught in the act by police, or caught by another person and handed to police, investigators had fifteen working days from arrest.[@1] Other cases normally allowed sixty working days from the initial information or the relevant investigation order.[@1]

## Drug cases already carried substantial prison terms

The deadlines were added to the [1990 narcotics law](/history/bangladesh-narcotics-control-act-1990/), which covered cannabis alongside other controlled drugs.[@1] Its penalty table prescribed six months to three years for ganja or herbal cannabis up to five kilograms, and three to fifteen years above that amount, with a fine.[@1]

The [2000 amendment](/history/bangladesh-drug-treatment-licensing-amendment-2000/) had already introduced supervised drug deliveries and allowed courts to order treatment instead of, or alongside, punishment for unlawful drug use.[@1] The next amendment added a timetable for the investigation itself.[@1]

## Delays had to be explained in writing

If a reasonable cause prevented completion within the initial fifteen- or sixty-day period, the investigator had to record the reasons and complete the investigation within seven additional working days.[@1] The officer also had to notify the supervising officer or the authority that ordered the investigation, giving the reasons in writing.[@1]

If the investigation still remained unfinished, the officer had to report that failure within twenty-four hours of the extended deadline.[@1] The supervising or ordering authority could then transfer the investigation to another officer.[@1]

## A replacement investigator had a shorter timetable

After a transfer, the new investigator had seven working days from the order in cases where the suspect had been caught in the act.[@1] Other transferred investigations had thirty working days.[@1] Failure to meet that second timetable also required a written report within twenty-four hours.[@1]

These duties gave the supervising authority a record of who held the case, what deadline applied and why it had not been completed.[@1]

## Responsibility could follow the officer

After reviewing the explanation, the supervising or ordering authority could decide that the investigating officer was responsible for failing to finish on time.[@1] The authority could then record incompetence and misconduct in the officer's annual confidential report and take appropriate action under the service rules.[@1] The amendment therefore joined the drug-investigation timetable to a system of reporting, reassignment and disciplinary accountability in [Bangladesh](/country/bd/).[@1]
