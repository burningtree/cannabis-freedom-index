Cannabis came under international control almost by accident. It was not on the
agenda of the conference that did it, and most of the delegates who voted knew next
to nothing about it.

## A conference about opium

The Second Opium Conference met in Geneva in November 1924 to limit the production of
opium, morphine and cocaine. Cannabis had been raised a year earlier by
[South Africa](/history/south-africa-league-request-1923/), which had just banned it
at home, and the League of Nations had only begun asking governments for information.

On the second day the Egyptian delegate, [Mohamed El Guindy](/history/geneva-egypt-proposal-1924/),
asked that hashish be added. It was, he said, "at least as harmful as opium, if not
more so". Britain objected that the subject was not on the agenda. El Guindy
submitted a formal proposal anyway.

## The speech

His case rested on madness. Occasional use in small doses, he conceded, "perhaps does
not offer much danger"; but the habitual user's "intellectual faculties gradually
weaken and the whole organism decays", and hashish accounted for "30 to 60 per cent"
of the insanity in Egypt. Left uncontrolled, it would "become a terrible menace to
the whole world".

The figures did not match Egypt's own records. Cairo's main asylum admitted 715
patients in 1920-21 and attributed 19 of them, 2.7 per cent, to hashish, fewer than
the 48 put down to alcohol.

No one checked. Most countries present had no experience of cannabis and deferred to
those that did. Egypt's motive was practical: its own ban was being undone by
smuggling from Greece, Turkey and the Levant. Turkey, Greece, South Africa and Brazil
supported it.

## What was agreed

A sub-committee of sixteen nations recommended complete prohibition; only Britain,
India and the Netherlands opposed it. That went too far for the conference, because
it reached into domestic law, and the drafting committee cut the text back over what
its chairman called El Guindy's "somewhat uncompromising insistence".

The convention signed on 19 February 1925 did two things for "Indian hemp". It banned
exports to countries that prohibited it, and it required an import certificate for
countries that allowed it. It did not oblige any country to ban cannabis at home.

## What followed

The treaty still changed domestic law. Governments began to legislate with it in
mind: Britain [banned cannabis](/history/uk-ban-1928/) in 1928, Germany
[added it](/history/germany-opium-law-1929/) to its Opium Law in 1929, and France
restricted cultivation in Morocco in 1932, citing its obligations under the
convention.

The larger effect was to establish, with no evidence examined, that cannabis belonged
on the same list as opium. Every later treaty started from there, and the
[Single Convention of 1961](/history/un-single-convention-1961/) completed what the
Geneva sub-committee had wanted.

The index records the convention for context. A treaty binds governments, so it
moves no country's score until a national law follows.
