South Africa banned dagga in November 1922. In December its own health department told
the police not to enforce the ban in much of the country.

## The objection

The new law classed dagga as a habit-forming drug and made possession an offence
everywhere in the Union. Officials responsible for African affairs thought this
unworkable and said so.

C. A. Wheelwright, the chief native commissioner in Natal, warned against making
criminals "by a stroke of the pen" of older men for whom the habit was lifelong. If
dagga could be outlawed as a noxious weed, he asked, would tobacco, tea and grapevines
be next? He travelled to Pretoria with the chief magistrate of the Transkei to argue
the case.

Their position, as put to the health department:

> In many parts of Zululand the smoking early in the morning by the older men of a
> pipe-full of Insangu is a custom so deep-rooted as almost to amount to a religious
> rite.

Enforcing the law there, they said, would be impossible and might provoke "very
undesirable incidents".

## The answer

The law was not changed. Instead the secretary for public health wrote that conditions
in the towns and in remote African areas were very different, and that

> the police will exercise judgement and discrimination, and avoid drastic or
> ill-advised attempts to enforce the letter of the law in remote localities where
> moderate Dagga smoking by some of the natives is of little importance.

## What it meant

A national prohibition applied by geography and race. Intensive policing was confined
by design, and in the remote reserves smoking was largely left alone.

The historian Thembisa Waetjen draws out the consequence. Because the law was not
enforced on communal land, that land became the place where dagga was grown for sale.
The uneven ban created the illegal trade's geography, and with it a field for
corruption among police and white landowners. By 1929 the press was reporting a
national smuggling network run along the railways.

## The longer view

The instruction is a rare document: a state admitting, in writing, that a law it had
just passed could not be applied to the people who used the drug most. It took
[South Africa](/country/za/) until
[2018](/history/south-africa-constitutional-court-2018/) to say the same thing in a
court.

The index lists it for context. The written law is what the score follows, and that
did not change.
