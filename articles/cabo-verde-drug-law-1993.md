Cabo Verde's drug law distinguishes small personal consumption from trafficking. A small amount
is not automatically legal, and a trafficking conviction cannot be understood simply by comparing
its sentence with the personal-consumption maximum.

## Separate offences

Article 20 covers consuming, cultivating, acquiring or possessing a small quantity intended for
personal consumption. It allows up to three months in prison or a fine of up to thirty days.
It supplies no fixed gram threshold.

Article 3 covers unauthorised production and supply, including transfers to another person, with
an ordinary four-to-twelve-year band for Table I/II substances. Article 6 provides a lesser-trafficking
band of one to five years where the unlawfulness is considerably reduced. It does not convert
that conduct into lawful possession or gifting.

## Conditional relief

The article 20 waiver requires three conditions together: minority, no repeat offence and a
solemn undertaking before a magistrate not to repeat the conduct. It is discretionary and does
not cover every adult first offender.

Article 22 already allowed sentence suspension for a dependent offender voluntarily undertaking
treatment. Article 21 protects voluntary attendance through anonymity and professional secrecy;
court-supervised treatment has reporting requirements under article 23. These are different routes.

## Read the corrected publication

The law was published on 12 July 1993, with commencement specified thirty days after publication.
On 29 November it was republished because the earlier printing had been incomplete and inaccurate.
For example, the corrected article 9 uses a seven-day quantity test for its user-trafficker rule,
where the July printing used five. That rule is distinct from article 20.

The earlier Decree-Law 92/92 supplies a controlled-drug licensing framework, including scientific
or teaching cultivation. Such powers do not establish that cannabis medicines are currently
available. Later amendments, issued licences and representative sentencing remain research gaps.
