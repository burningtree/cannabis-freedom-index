A Tajik drug-policy review dated 2010 called for changes to policing, treatment and patient confidentiality. The work brought together health officials, the Drug Control Agency and civil-society representatives to examine how drug laws affected people seeking care or dealing with the justice system.[@1,2]

## The review joined health and criminal justice

The ROST report examined legislation and practices across several areas of life, including treatment, employment and family rights. Its working process involved officials and organisations concerned with drug use, rather than treating the subject solely as an enforcement problem.[@1]

The [general drug law adopted in 1999](/history/tajikistan-narcotics-law-1999/) already linked policing and health care. Its later consolidated text prohibited non-prescribed use, provided for medical examination and registration, and set out voluntary treatment alongside references to compulsory treatment.[@3]

## Arrest targets could work against patient rights

The reviewers criticised the weight placed on arrests and convictions as measures of successful drug control. Their recommendations called for law enforcement to focus more on dealers and larger supply networks and to respect procedural safeguards for people detained.[@2]

The review also examined discrimination affecting people who use drugs. It considered how a person’s status could affect employment and family life, and questioned rules that treated drug dependence itself as sufficient grounds for adverse consequences.[@1]

## Confidentiality mattered when people sought care

The study identified weaknesses in the protection of medical information. Its recommendations sought clearer confidentiality rules and better understanding of those rules among health staff and law-enforcement officials.[@1]

The statutory treatment system gave voluntarily admitted patients specified rights, including anonymous treatment subject to exceptions and protection of their home and job during inpatient care. At the same time, the law required registration after findings of unlawful use and follow-up observation after treatment for dependence.[@3]

## The penalties remained part of the wider picture

The Criminal Code treated personal possession and supply separately. In its later consolidation, even the lowest personal-possession band could bring imprisonment, with a fine available as an alternative; larger amounts and aggravated cases carried longer terms.[@4]

Later changes [extended offences to drug analogues](/history/tajikistan-drug-analogues-2021/) and [replaced the quantity annex](/history/tajikistan-quantity-list-2023/).[@4] The [Tajikistan profile](/country/tj/) explains the statutory controls alongside the review’s account of the pressures faced by people who use drugs.
