Ceylon restricted cannabis sales to licensed dealers in 1867 through a law
covering both opium and bhang. The colonial government chose a supervised
trade, with cannabis still sold under local licensing rules decades later.

## Officials wanted places they could supervise

The Legislative Council sent the proposal to a committee headed by R. F.
Morgan, the Queen's Advocate, the colony's senior government lawyer. The
committee supported licensing as a way to restrict use and help police watch
the places where people consumed the drugs.

Historian C. G. Uragoda records the committee's hope of giving police “due
surveillance” over those premises. Cannabis, described as bang or bhang, was
included alongside opium in this attempt to control who could sell drugs.

## A licence came with rules for the shop

The later [Colombo licensing notice](/history/sri-lanka-colombo-bhang-licences-1893/)
shows what supervised cannabis commerce looked like in practice. In 1893,
the municipal council advertised four retail licences for the coming year,
to be offered at public auction.

Licensed shops could sell opium or bhang only between six in the morning and
nine at night. They were open to inspection, and the licence holder had to
prevent disorder on the premises.

Permission also extended to the people behind the counter. An agent or servant
needed registration with the council and the chairman's approval before selling
either drug under someone else's licence. A dealer could therefore face
restrictions on both the premises and the staff running the business.

## Local limits gave way to prohibition

Later in 1893, a [new law for designated towns](/history/sri-lanka-municipal-bhang-licensing-1893/)
let local authorities decide the annual number of retail licences, within
ceilings of five for a municipal council and three for a local board. The
number had to be announced publicly in the gazette and newspapers. Licensed
trade was becoming a tightly bounded local market.

The [1897 prohibition](/history/sri-lanka-import-ban-1897/) changed the terms
for cannabis sellers and customers. It prohibited bhang and ganja imports and
made possession and sale offences, with a first conviction carrying up to
three months in prison, a fine, or both. It also removed cannabis from key
parts of the earlier licensing laws.

Over those thirty years, [Ceylon's cannabis policy](/country/lk/) moved from
choosing who could sell to prohibiting the trade and punishing possession.
