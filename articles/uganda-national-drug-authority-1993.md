Uganda's 1993 national drug law placed cannabis controls within a new authority
responsible for the country's medicines and drug supply. It prohibited smoking
cannabis, hosting people smoking it and possessing smoking utensils, while
requiring written ministerial consent to grow plants yielding narcotics.

## Treaty commitments became part of the drug authority's work

Uganda had joined the [Single Convention in 1988](/history/uganda-single-convention-1988/)
and the [UN trafficking convention in 1990](/history/uganda-1988-convention-1990/).
The new national policy expressly included compliance with international drug
controls and action against drug abuse.

The authority's responsibilities also reached everyday health care: making
essential medicines available, regulating pharmacies and checking drug quality.
Cannabis control sat inside that wider effort to organise how medicines were
produced, imported, sold and used. The statute took effect on 3 December 1993.

## Smoking offences reached the room and the pipe

The law classified cannabis and cannabis resin as Class A drugs, its narcotics
category. Possession of internationally controlled narcotics required a lawful
excuse, which the holder had to prove.

The smoking rule went further than the act of lighting cannabis. It prohibited
frequenting a place used for smoking it, allowing premises to be used for that
purpose, and holding pipes or other utensils for smoking. A person could
therefore fall within the rule through the place or equipment involved.

## Growers needed consent and suppliers needed authorisation

Cultivation required the health minister's written consent. Before granting it,
the minister had to consult the drug authority and could impose conditions.
The grower had to be able to prove that consent.

Supplying internationally controlled narcotics was limited to medical, dental
and veterinary purposes. Import and export permits were confined to those
uses too. A wholesale drug licence itself prohibited supplying Indian
hemp prepared for smoking. These rules made separate controls over cultivation,
professional supply and smoking part of the same system.

## Later legislation replaced the main narcotics offences

For a Class A offence without another specified punishment, the law provided
up to five years in prison, a fine of up to two million shillings, or both.
It also contained separate, heavier rules for repeated narcotics offences.

The [law that commenced in 2016](/history/uganda-narcotics-act-2015/) replaced
the principal possession, smoking and cultivation sections. It raised the
possession prison range to ten to twenty-five years, alongside a fine option,
and made trafficking punishable by life imprisonment plus a fine. That later
law became the subject of the [2023 constitutional challenge](/history/uganda-court-nullifies-act-2023/)
that helped reshape [Uganda's cannabis controls](/country/ug/).
