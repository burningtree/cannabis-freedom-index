In 1928 South Africa moved its national dagga provisions out of the 1922
customs law and into a pharmacy act. Only cannabis species were now named in
the schedule; Leonotis, the "wild dagga" the earlier law had also listed,
dropped out.[@1]

## A customs-law prohibition

Section 14 of the Customs and Excise Regulation Amendment Act (No. 35 of
1922), gazetted on 10 November, had classed both "dagga" and "Indian hemp" as
Schedule One habit-forming drugs, alongside coca leaves, cocaine,
diamorphine, ecgonine, morphine and three forms of opium. A year later
Smuts's government asked the League of Nations to put cannabis on the
international controlled list; from 1925, when the League provided for
cannabis restrictions, the Union recorded annual dagga arrests and seizures
with the other controlled drugs. The [1922 national ban](/history/south-africa-national-ban-1922/)
and the [1923 League petition](/history/south-africa-league-request-1923/)
tell that part of the story.[@1]

## Twelve years toward a pharmacy act

Getting a national pharmacy law had taken almost two decades, with drug and
medicine rules defaulting to the provinces. The Cape Town police
commissioner once offered two routes: schedule dagga as a Division I poison
like opium, with chemists keeping records, or declare it a noxious weed and
eradicate the wild plant, licensing only approved growers. Law advisers
rejected the weed option — dagga could not strictly be treated as noxious —
and the Cape pressed Pretoria for a national pharmacy act instead.[@1]

The Union's 1916 draft, the Opium and other Habit-Forming Drug Regulation
Bill, was a narcotics law rather than a pharmacy act: it would have banned
use, possession, traffic and unlicensed cultivation. Confusingly, it defined
"dagga" as Leonotis species — wild, red or klip dagga — and "Indian hemp"
as Cannabis sativa or indica, banning pipes and smoking materials for all
three alongside opium. The bill was never presented in parliament, though it
signalled the policy shift; at the same time the state weighed commercial
markets for dagga fibre, oil and birdseed. The 1928 act settled the botany:
cannabis species only.[@1]

## Enforced unevenly by design

A month after the 1922 law was gazetted, the health secretary told native
affairs that police should use judgement and avoid drastic enforcement in
remote localities where moderate smoking mattered little for public order.
Native affairs officials had protested a ban imposed without consulting
those affected. The law mapped onto segregation: intensive policing
concentrated in white civic spaces while communal land became important for
commercial growing, and police and white landowners colluded with African
growers for profit.[@1]

By the late 1920s dagga's reputation as a dangerous drug was fully
developed, with a growing smuggling trade: the Cape Times reported rising
"evil" in 1929, magistrates named rural districts as supply centres feeding
the rail network, and Paarl served as a disposal depot. The [December 1922
compromise](/history/south-africa-enforcement-compromise-1922/) and the
[country profile](/country/za/) carry the later history.[@1]
