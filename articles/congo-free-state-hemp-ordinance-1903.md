The Congo Free State prohibited growing, selling, transporting and holding
cannabis for smoking in 1903. Growers faced destruction of their crops, and
village chiefs could be made liable for fines when they failed to report offences.
The law also offered a brief opportunity to surrender cannabis for compensation.

## The ban reached the supply network

The ordinance targeted the activities that supplied cannabis to smokers.
Historian Chris Duvall describes its effect on informal African trade: farming,
selling, carrying and possessing the plant all became offences. The prohibition
reached people along the chain from cultivation to consumption.

The distinction between the plant and its intended use mattered. The law named
cannabis for smoking, a locally traded product. Duvall places the restriction
within a wider colonial pattern in which authorities treated African cannabis
markets as having little economic value while seeking opportunities in the
European pharmaceutical trade.

## Growers had fifteen days to declare their crops

The law gave people fifteen days after it took effect to declare fields under
cannabis and present stocks to the nearest government post. In return, they
were to receive payment for the value of cannabis destroyed, whether already
harvested or still standing.

That offer gave growers and holders a short route out of the prohibited trade.
Afterwards, authorities were to destroy unlawful plantations at the offenders'
expense. Confiscated cannabis was also to be destroyed.

The scheme therefore combined compensation for declared supplies with financial
liability for crops the authorities removed as illegal plantations.

## Village chiefs were drawn into enforcement

Offenders faced a fine of 100 to 1,000 francs, imprisonment for fifteen days
to one year, or either punishment alone. Conviction also brought crop destruction
and confiscation of the cannabis seized.

Village chiefs were jointly liable for fines and court costs unless they had
reported offences committed in their villages. The rule gave chiefs a financial
reason to inform the authorities about cultivation, possession and supply.
Enforcement responsibilities therefore extended beyond the person directly
accused of breaking the prohibition.

## Consumption was added explicitly later

A [1917 amendment](/history/belgian-congo-consumption-offence-1917/) expressly
extended the prohibition to smoking cannabis and consuming it in any other way.
It also required seizure and confiscation of equipment intended or used for
consumption.

Those additions connected the earlier controls on farmers and suppliers with
an explicit offence directed at users. The amended colonial text remained in
the legal collections of what became [DR Congo](/country/cd/) long after the
original prohibition.
