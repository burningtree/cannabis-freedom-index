The first international drug convention did not control cannabis. Delegates
at The Hague briefly considered the subject, but had neither the information
nor government instructions needed to negotiate a cannabis regime.

## A late Italian initiative

Cannabis reached the conference agenda through Italy. According to the report
of the British delegation examined by historian James H. Mills, Britain was
not told of the proposed topic until roughly two weeks before the meeting.
Italy itself arrived without a specific proposal, leaving the conference to
decide what action, if any, should follow.

The programme committee saw basic obstacles. Delegates had no statistics with
which to assess international cannabis trade, no instructions from their
governments and no agreed scientific definition of the preparations that might
be regulated. Its chairman also questioned whether cannabis belonged within
an international problem that had been organised around opium and opiates.

One suggested answer was that countries facing cannabis abuse could take their
own domestic measures. That left no shared definition, evidence base or model
rule from which the conference could construct an international obligation.

## A request for research

Instead of drafting controls, the conference adopted a resolution asking
participating governments to study Indian hemp statistically and
scientifically. The possible objective was domestic legislation or an
international agreement if regulation proved necessary. Britain successfully
pressed for wording that made the conditional nature of any future action
clearer.

The result was therefore a recommendation for governments to gather knowledge,
not an agreement that cannabis required control. It carried no timetable and
specified no offences, trade restrictions or penalties.

The International Opium Convention signed on 23 January 1912 focused on
opium, morphine and cocaine. Cannabis did not appear in its operative text.
That distinction corrects an easy misconception: discussion at a diplomatic
conference was not the same as inclusion in the resulting treaty.

## Control comes later

Cannabis returned to the international agenda after
[South Africa's 1923 request](/history/south-africa-league-request-1923/).
Egypt then advanced a prohibition proposal at
[Geneva in 1924](/history/geneva-egypt-proposal-1924/). The resulting
[1925 convention](/history/geneva-opium-convention-1925/) imposed controls on
international cannabis trade, thirteen years after The Hague had stopped at a
request for study.

The index treats the 1912 resolution as international context. No score moved:
the signed convention contained no cannabis rule, and the resolution itself
changed no country's domestic law.
