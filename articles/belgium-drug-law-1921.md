On 24 February 1921 Belgium adopted the drug act that anchored its narcotics enforcement for decades: the government communication sent to the United Nations in 1952 still shows its machinery at work.[@2] The act itself named no cannabis preparation that can be shown here — its original text has not been verified — but every later regulation drew its authority and its penalties from it, including the 1930 royal order that expressly put Indian hemp under control.[@1,2]

## The act behind the orders

The government text communicated to the UN reproduces the Royal Order of 31 December 1930, as amended in 1951, and names the 1921 act as its legal backbone throughout.[@2] Article 2 puts the Narcotics Service — the public-health department section — in charge of enforcing "the provisions of the Act of 24 February 1921 and of the Royal Orders made under it".[@2] Article 28 punishes offences against the order "in accordance with the Act of 24 February 1921".[@2] The same order repealed the first implementing decree, the Royal Decree of 6 September 1921.[@2]

## How cannabis entered the system

The 1930 order's catalogue reaches Indian hemp expressly: item 19 covers Indian hemp, its resin, resin-based preparations — hashish, esrar, chira, djamba — and hemp extract and tincture, while exempting external-use preparations of the extract or tincture.[@2] So cannabis stands controlled through the order and punished through the act. The 1921 date alone cannot say when the plant entered the list, but the combined texts show the route it took.[@2]

## What came next

[The 1930 order](/history/belgium-royal-order-1930/) built the permit-and-prescription system — yearly ministerial authorisations, locked cabinets, inspectors checking registers — on this foundation. Belgium's current law is on [its profile](/country/be/).
