Thirteen foreign nationals were arrested in a drug raid at Kabul's
Shak-Foladi Hotel on 16 May 1970. The seizure of hashish and opium
drew attention to travellers' role in connecting Afghanistan's drug
markets with buyers abroad.

## A hotel raid exposed the visitors' market

Historian James Bradford records 200 grams of hashish and 100 grams
of opium seized in the raid. He identifies the foreign nationals'
involvement as the reason the episode mattered to Western drug-control
officials, despite the relatively small seizure.

Afghan bazaars already sold hashish to local people and visitors.
Bradford describes a market that also offered ways to conceal the drug
for a journey abroad. The trade joined local sellers to travellers
moving along routes through South and Central Asia.

## American attention followed the travellers

An agent from the US Bureau of Narcotics and Dangerous Drugs was
stationed in Kabul later in 1970. American officials were concerned
that people buying drugs while travelling could supply markets back
home.

That concern also appeared in a
[diplomatic conversation the following year](/history/afghanistan-us-drug-cooperation-1971/).
Secretary of State William Rogers asked Afghan foreign minister Musa
Shafiq whether hippies continued to cause problems. Shafiq described
growing numbers of young visitors staying beyond their permitted time
and increasing demand for drugs.

For American officials, Afghanistan's market was connected to developments
elsewhere. Rogers warned that success against poppy production in Turkey
could encourage buyers to seek supplies in Afghanistan.

## An existing prohibition was difficult to enforce

Shafiq told Rogers that hashish and opium cultivation were prohibited
under Islamic law. He also acknowledged that the prohibitions had
not always been enforced, especially in remote mountain areas.

Ambassador Malikyar added that poppy provided an income in poor,
isolated communities and that affected farmers needed a replacement
cash crop. The talks linked the drugs encountered by visitors with
the difficulty of controlling supply at its rural source.

## Arrests became part of a larger campaign

The [1972 enforcement drive](/history/afghanistan-smuggling-crackdown-1972/)
brought new anti-smuggling units and rising arrest totals. Bradford
records 72 smuggler arrests in 1971, 221 in 1972 and 584 in 1973,
while concluding that the trade continued despite the intervention.

The hotel raid belongs near the start of that increasingly visible
response. It shows foreign visitors becoming a focus of enforcement
as [Afghanistan's hashish market](/country/af/) reached further into
international trade.
