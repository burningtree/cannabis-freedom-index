[Cuba](/country/cu/) deposited its accession to the Single Convention on Narcotic Drugs on 30 August 1962. The [UN depositary record](https://treaties.un.org/Pages/ViewDetails.aspx?src=TREATY&mtdsg_no=VI-15&chapter=6&clang=_en) marks the act as accession, rather than a signature followed by ratification. It placed the revolutionary government within an international system controlling cannabis alongside other narcotic drugs.

The date records Cuba's acceptance of the treaty. It should not be mistaken for the treaty's entry into force: the convention became operative internationally on 13 December 1964 after the required number of instruments had been deposited.

## An international obligation, not a local sentence

Article 4 of the [original convention](https://treaties.un.org/doc/Treaties/1964/12/19641213%2002-14%20AM/Ch_VI_15.pdf) directs parties, subject to its provisions, to restrict controlled drugs to medical and scientific purposes. Its coverage includes production, manufacture, trade, use and possession. Cannabis and cannabis resin were included in the control system.

That framework called for legislative and administrative action by states. It did not itself specify a Cuban prison term for possessing cannabis. The distinction matters when reading a country timeline: an international undertaking and a domestic criminal offence are related documents, but they answer different questions.

Article 36 addressed punishable conduct and serious offences, subject to constitutional limitations. A later Cuban decision to make certain drug offences punishable by death cannot be described as a sentence required by the Single Convention.

## The plant was not one undifferentiated category

Article 28 required controls when cultivation was permitted for the production of cannabis or cannabis resin. It separately excluded cultivation exclusively for industrial fibre and seed, or horticultural purposes, from the convention’s application.

This exception is often lost in descriptions of the treaty as a ban on every use of the plant. It did not, however, give a Cuban resident an automatic domestic right to grow hemp. A treaty exclusion leaves room for national legislation; it does not override a country's own cultivation offence.

## One stage in a longer history

Cuban anti-cannabis rhetoric was already visible in [Gil Carballo’s 1937 book](/history/cuba-gil-carballo-book-1937/). Treaty accession therefore cannot serve as proof that cannabis first became a political or policing concern after the Revolution. The date of Cuba’s first domestic restriction remains unresolved in the sources used here.

Cuba later [acceded to the 1972 amending Protocol in 1989](/history/cuba-single-convention-protocol-1989/). The [depositary record](https://treaties.un.org/Pages/ViewDetails.aspx?src=TREATY&mtdsg_no=VI-17&chapter=6&clang=_en) confirms that separate action. Its [1999 amendment](/history/cuba-law-87-1999/) and [2022 Penal Code](/history/cuba-penal-code-2022/) show how domestic law subsequently defined cultivation, possession and aggravated trafficking. Those statutes, rather than the accession date alone, supply the penalties faced within Cuba.
