Canada brought cannabis into its federal narcotics regime in 1923. The decision was consequential, but the surviving parliamentary record supplies almost no explanation for it.

## One new name in a schedule

The Opium and Narcotic Drug Act of 1911 initially controlled four drugs. Parliament repeatedly expanded its schedule during the following decades, and cannabis was one of the substances added in 1923.

The Senate of Canada's later historical review found only a terse remark from health minister Henri-Séverin Béland during consideration of the measure: there was a new drug in the schedule. The health effects of cannabis had not previously been discussed in Parliament, and the review concluded that the circumstances behind its inclusion remain obscure.

## A solution without a problem

Emily Murphy's 1922 book *The Black Candle* included a chapter presenting marijuana as a menace. Yet the archival research cited by the Senate report did not find that her campaign caused the 1923 scheduling decision.

The domestic setting was striking. The report found no reliable account of non-medical cannabis use in Canada before the 1930s. Cannabis was virtually unknown in the country and its use was not regarded as a problem. One historian therefore described its criminalisation as a solution without a problem.

The change nevertheless placed cannabis within a system built to restrict possession, distribution and lawful supply. It arrived during a period in which Canada's Narcotics Division was becoming a central source of proposed offences, enforcement powers and amendments to drug law.

## What 1923 did not do

The date is often treated as the start of a complete cannabis ban, but the legal sequence was not quite that simple. In 1932 Parliament still discussed limited cannabis preparations in medicines, and commercial hemp continued to be grown in Ontario.

Most importantly, the 1923 scheduling did not create the later cultivation offence. Parliament [prohibited unlicensed cannabis growing in 1938](/history/canada-cultivation-ban-1938/), ending the remaining lawful cultivation.

## What moved in the index

The 1923 measure took [Canada](/country/ca/) from an unregulated starting point to prohibition for possession, enforcement, sharing and consumption, and removed ordinary legal access. Those scores fell from 10, 10, 10 and 10 to 1, while access fell from 8 to 0. Cultivation stayed at 10 until the distinct 1938 ban.
