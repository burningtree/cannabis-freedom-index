In 1936 the British gave Palestine its toughest hashish law yet — on paper. The Dangerous Drugs Ordinance of that year, the latest version of the 1925 law, folded in the Opium Advisory Committee's recommendations: higher penalties for traffickers, a transit offence stated more plainly than ever, and, for the first time, a direct assault on the places where hashish was smoked.[@1]

## Article 14: the cafés

The novelty was Article 14, absent from the 1925, 1928 and 1932 texts. It created "offences in connection with premises used for sale or smoking of opium or hashish" — outlawing the use of premises for preparing the drugs for smoking and the possession of pipes and other smoking utensils.[@1] The target was visible across the country: low-class cafés in Jaffa, Haifa, Jerusalem, Acre and Nablus serving hashish openly or stealthily, and by 1939 even cafés in the heart of Tel Aviv serving Jewish clients.[@1] Patrons and owners were regularly arrested, and police ran repeated coordinated café raids through the 1930s.[@1]

## Transit, stated plainly

The ordinance also reiterated, more clearly than its predecessors, that anyone conveying dangerous drugs through Palestine in transit was guilty of an offence — a clause aimed at the Lebanon-Syria-to-Egypt corridor that made the country the Levant's smuggling theatre.[@1] Like the penalty increases, it tracked the OAC line on trafficking and consumption.[@1]

## A dead letter

None of it worked. The ordinances remained a dead letter: the administration lacked the infrastructure, the funds and the will, and hashish policing ranked below immigration control and communal tensions — during the 1936–39 revolt the camel corps meant to watch the desert "melted away into a virtual state of non-operation".[@1] The users were overwhelmingly lower-class Arabs, the smugglers everyone from peasants and Bedouins to railway workers and international rings, and the hashish kept moving south.[@1] The [country page](/country/ps/) follows the law from this ordinance through the end of the Mandate.
