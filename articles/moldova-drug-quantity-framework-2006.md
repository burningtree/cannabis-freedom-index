Moldova’s government adopted drug-quantity classifications on 23 January 2006, distinguishing small, large and especially large amounts.[@1] Those categories helped decide whether possession or cultivation led to administrative proceedings or a criminal case, and which punishment applied.[@1]

## The amount affected the legal consequences

The government decision covered controlled drugs and the plants containing them, alongside the quantities used to classify illegal activity.[@1] The criminal code used those classifications together with what a person had done and whether the drugs were intended for transfer to someone else.[@1]

The court later explained that the quantity could affect the classification of the offence and the individual punishment.[@1] Personal handling and conduct intended to supply another person remained distinct parts of that system.[@1]

## A parliamentarian challenged who set the lines

MP Elena Bodnarenco challenged the government’s authority in 2015, arguing that parliament should set quantities affecting criminal liability through legislation.[@1] She argued that approval of a drug list did not itself authorise ministers to decide the amounts separating the categories.[@1]

The government and other state institutions defended the delegated approach.[@1] Their arguments included the need to respond promptly when new substances appeared, and the connection between a controlled-drug list and the quantities necessary to apply it.[@1]

## The court upheld the government’s role

On [13 October 2015](/history/moldova-quantity-tables-upheld-2015/), the Constitutional Court rejected the challenge and upheld the quantity rules.[@1] It said parliament could delegate this part of the drug-control system while retaining the criminal offences and punishments in legislation.[@1]

The court treated drug categories and quantities as connected elements of the list.[@1] It also explained that a criminal rule could rely on related regulations for details needed to apply it, helping legislation remain workable as the controlled-substance field evolved.[@1]

## The categories continued into later reforms

The [August 2026 reform](/history/moldova-drug-penalties-2026/) retained the distinction between small administrative possession and larger criminal handling, while raising fines and supply penalties.[@2] Small personal possession became punishable by a 200–300-unit administrative fine, while ordinary illegal supply carried six to ten years in prison.[@2]

The Interior Ministry began work on replacement quantity classifications in July 2026 and moved the proposal to the [government-promotion stage in September](/history/moldova-quantity-tables-draft-2026/).[@3] The [Moldova profile](/country/md/) therefore sets out the verified statutory tiers while keeping the current numerical boundaries as a separate research question.[@2,3]
