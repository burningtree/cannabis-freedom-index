In 1976, Swaziland quadrupled the money and nearly doubled the prison time behind its drug law. The King's Order-in-Council No. 2 of that year lifted the maximum penalty under the 1922 Act from a E500 fine and three years in prison to E2,000 and five years — keeping the option of prison with no fine at all.[@1]

## A legislature sending a message

The High Court, recording the change a decade later, read it as a statement of intent: dagga and other drugs were regarded by the legislature in a very serious light.[@1] The offences themselves did not change — possession, cultivation and supply had been criminal since 1922 — but the ceiling under which every sentence hung moved sharply upward.[@1]

## Bands, not just ceilings

The same 1986 judgment turned the new ceiling into working practice. Reviewing a E300 fine for 14.85 kg of dagga, the Chief Justice replaced it with three years in prison and laid down bands: personal-use amounts normally mean a fine for a first offender, while wholesale suppliers cultivating or holding for widespread distribution face at or near the maximum even the first time.[@1] Couriers and retailers face substantial custodial sentences; the isolated or social supplier may still escape with a non-custodial one.[@1]

The judgment also shows why ceilings mattered on the ground. Dagga sold for fifty cents a 10-gram packet and E400 a sack; Phiri's 14.85 kg haul was worth E700, more across the South African border.[@1] The Chief Justice accepted that even schoolchildren were smoking, yet warned courts not to go soft just because a rural family lived off the crop: cultivation remained a crime Parliament regarded as a social evil.[@1]

## The ceiling held

Three years later the High Court applied those bands to a grower caught uprooting plants in a 9,705-plant field, cutting four years to two with one suspended.[@2] The 1976 maxima are still the law cited for dagga sentences today — the five-year figure in the current [Eswatini profile](/country/sz/) traces straight back to this order. The later medical-cannabis bills would amend the same 1922 Act rather than replace it.
