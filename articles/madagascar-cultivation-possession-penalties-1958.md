On 23 May 1958 Madagascar's colonial administration made punishable what the 1915 ban had left out: growing and possessing rongony, the local smoking cannabis.[@1] The 1958 extension closed the circle of the colonial prohibition — and two years later independent Madagascar swept the whole regime into prison terms of up to five years.[@1]

## The 1915 base

The starting point was a decree of 9 October 1915, promulgated that December, which punished preparing, peddling, selling and consuming rongony with a fine of 100 to 3,000 francs and six days to twelve months in prison, or either penalty alone.[@1] Cultivation and simple possession were not among the offences.[@1]

## Closing the circle

The Representative Assembly's deliberation no. 58-34/AR of 17 April 1958 added exactly those missing offences, and the administration's order no. 145-CG of 23 May 1958 made it enforceable.[@1] Growing and holding Indian hemp now drew eleven days to a month in prison plus a fine of 36,001 to 60,000 metropolitan francs, rising to two months and 100,000 francs for repeat offenders.[@1] The order appeared in the colonial official journal of 31 May 1958.[@1]

## Why it did not hold

The government's own memorandum judged the regime a failure: the penalties, one year at most, were out of proportion to the gravity of an offence spreading fast, and the rules could not check the expansion of cannabis use.[@1] The answer came weeks after independence. President Tsiranana's ordinance of 28 July 1960 banned rongony cultivation across the entire territory and prohibited preparation, transport, sale, possession and consumption with it.[@1] Every breach now drew six months to five years plus 10,000 to a million francs; facilitators who lent vehicles or premises faced the same, with confiscation, closure of the premises and an automatic residence ban.[@1] The ordinance expressly repealed both the 1915 decree and the 1958 deliberation, wishing the rigorous measures would rid Madagascar of what it called a genuine international scourge.[@1]

The 1958 extension thus lived barely two years, a bridge between the colonial ban and the independence-era crackdown that a [1997 drug law](/history/madagascar-drug-law-1997/) would later rework — and the 1960 maximum still echoes in [Madagascar's](/country/mg/) scores today.[@1]
