Italy's Royal Decree-Law 151 replaced the
[1923 narcotics law](/history/italy-first-drug-law-1923/) with a framework that
combined criminal offences, controls over medical distribution and compulsory
confinement. Dated 15 January 1934, it took effect on publication on 19 February.
Article 21 expressly made publication the commencement date.

Cannabis was named in the decree itself. Its provisions also show why the
history of drug control cannot be reduced to a single penalty for all users
and suppliers: different conduct triggered different offences and powers.

## Indian hemp and clandestine commerce

Article 1 named Indian hemp alongside raw opium and coca leaves in its
provisions on clandestine or fraudulent commerce. The offence included holding
those materials for that purpose and carried one to three years' imprisonment
and a fine of at least 1,000 lire.

Article 3 separately addressed authorised operators who exceeded permitted
quantities or supplied unauthorised recipients or recipients lacking guarantees
of legitimate professional or scientific use. Articles 4 and 5 dealt with
improper medicinal supply and prescribing. Authorisation therefore brought
obligations as well as permission to participate in the controlled trade.

## Consumption in specified settings

Article 2 punished people responsible for premises used for gatherings to take
narcotics. Participants could also face imprisonment or a fine. This continued
the earlier law's attention to organised places of consumption.

Article 7 addressed people found in a state of serious mental disturbance
attributed to drug abuse in public places, places open to the public or private
clubs of any kind. The stated penalty was detention for up to six months or
a fine. Those conditions are part of the offence; describing the provision as
a penalty for any private consumption would omit its limits.

## Confinement alongside punishment

Article 12 allowed compulsory admission to a health institution for
detoxification when a person had been found in the specified state of serious
drug-related mental disturbance. This power operated independently of the
penalties in Article 7.

The decision belonged to the judicial authority following a report from public
security authorities or the persons identified in the referenced 1904 law.
In urgent cases, public security authorities could order provisional admission,
subject to definitive judicial measures. The decree thus made treatment a
potentially coercive intervention within its system of control.

## Repeal and parliamentary conversion

Article 20 expressly repealed Law 396 of 1923. Parliament later converted the
decree into law through Law 1145, dated 7 June and published on 25 July 1934.
That later step should be distinguished from the decree's February commencement.

The original documents establish the powers created and the conduct penalised.
They do not show how frequently cannabis consumers were prosecuted or confined.
For [Italy's timeline](/country/it/), that remains a separate question requiring
evidence about enforcement.
