Colombia's first marijuana law dates to 1928, according to the Colombian
historians cited by the anthropologist William Partridge. Smoking had reached
the country through Caribbean ports and up the Magdalena valley, travelling
with the workers who moved around the region during and after the building of
the Panama Canal.[@1]

## Hemp that never took root

Spain tried repeatedly to grow hemp in Colombia, with recorded attempts in
1607, 1610, 1632 and 1789, but no hemp industry ever developed. Native fibres
did the same jobs: tribute-collected cabuya replaced hemp in rope, sacks and
sandals, and cotton even replaced it in candle wicks for the mines.
Experiments continued into the twentieth century without result.[@1]

Smoking was a separate and newer arrival. Linguistic evidence points to West
African slaves carrying cannabis to Brazil first; its spread into Spanish
America is less documented. In the region Partridge studied, cannabis
competed with established intoxicants, above all tobacco and coca, of which
the colonists adopted only tobacco.[@1]

## A Caribbean innovation

Partridge follows earlier writers in tracing Colombian smoking to the
Magdalena valley, entered through Santa Marta, Barranquilla and Cartagena
from the Antilles and Panama. The canal works set off an intense interchange
of labourers and sailors around the Caribbean, and the first laws date to
those years: Costa Rica's to 1927 and Colombia's to 1928. Costa Rica
regulated opium in 1927 and
[banned marijuana in October 1928](/history/costa-rica-marijuana-decree-1928/).
Yet only around 1945 did the Colombian press begin reporting hidden
plantations on the Atlantic coast and in the Cauca valley.[@1]

## What came next

In 1939 Colombia prohibited cultivation outright: Resolución 645 ordered
plantations destroyed and punished growers as traffickers in drastic drugs.
The [1939 restrictions](/history/colombia-restrictions-1939/) replaced the
early regime with an eradication model. By the 1970s the Sierra Nevada had a
cannabis cash-crop economy: Partridge's fieldwork found highland families
growing it for 100 to 300 pesos a pound, against 12.50 pesos for a kilo of
coffee, selling through coastal middlemen with no local grower yet arrested.
The police commissioner grew it himself. That economy is described in the
[Santa Marta field study](/history/colombia-santa-marta-cannabis-economy-1973/),
and the [Colombia profile](/country/co/) follows the later history.[@1]
