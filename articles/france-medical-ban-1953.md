In 1953 France banned cannabis as a medicine, striking it from the pharmacopoeia.[@1] The ban closed a story that had begun in Paris more than a century earlier — and it took sixty years to undo.

## The golden age

In the 1840s Paris was the world capital of cannabis medicine. The army doctor Jacques-Joseph Aubert-Roche published the first French treatise on plague in Cairo in 1840 and treated typhus and plague with hashish.[@1] Jacques-Joseph Moreau, founder of psychiatry in France, swallowed dawamesk with writers and artists to map its effects on the mind, publishing his clinical study in 1845.[@2] The pharmacist Gastinel patented a hashish tincture in 1848 and dosed cholera patients with it — but in 1849 cholera killed some 7,000 people a day in Paris, and the tincture failed against the epidemic.[@1]

As the germ theory redefined disease and alienists warned of "hashish madness", medical interest faded.[@1] By the early twentieth century the 1840s experiments were a curiosity.

## The 1953 ban

In 1953 cannabis vanished from the pharmacopoeia, banned even as a medicine.[@1] France would spend the next half-century as one of Europe's most prohibitionist countries: the 1970 drug law made use and trafficking criminal, with cultivation punished as trafficking — up to 20 years — to this day. Yet the ban never erased the 1840s: Moreau's dawamesk sessions with Baudelaire, Gautier and Nerval at the Club des Hashischins remain the most famous drug experiments in French history.[@2]

## The slow return

Only in 2013 did a decree reauthorise cannabis medicines, followed by a national experiment in 2021 that formally ended in 2024 with no permanent regime.[@1] A reimbursement decree went to the Conseil d'État in July 2026, with first prescriptions under a permanent regime expected in 2027 at the earliest. Seventy years after striking cannabis from the pharmacopoeia, [France](/country/fr/) is still writing it back in.
