The American turn against marijuana in the 1980s did not start in Washington. It
started in a backyard in Atlanta, after a thirteen-year-old's birthday party.

## The moment

By the mid-1970s the United States seemed to be easing. The
[Shafer Commission](/history/us-shafer-commission-1972/) had recommended ending the
possession offence, a dozen states had reduced it to a civil fine, and in
[1977](/history/us-carter-decriminalisation-message-1977/) the president's own drug
adviser was working for decriminalisation.

Use among the young was rising with it. By 1978, 37.1 per cent of high-school seniors
said they had smoked in the past month, and 9 per cent smoked every day. Shops sold
pipes, bongs and rolling papers openly, a trade worth 250 million dollars in 1977.

## The party

In the summer of 1976 Marsha "Keith" Schuchard, a mother of three, found that her
daughter Ashley had been smoking marijuana at her own birthday party. Searching the
garden afterwards, she and her husband found butts, small plastic bags and home-made
roach clips.

The next morning she telephoned the parents of every child who had been there, and
gathered those who would come in her living room.

## The method

The parents agreed common rules: no paraphernalia in the house, no drug magazines, a
shared curfew. They went looking for what the local shops were selling to children. And
they offered alternatives, from sports and camping to part-time jobs.

Schuchard wrote the experience up, at the request of the head of the National Institute
on Drug Abuse, as a guide called Parents, Peers and Pot. Groups formed across the
country on the Atlanta model.

## The results

By 1980 the movement had a national federation, tens of thousands of members and real
influence in state capitals. By its historian's account it persuaded states to overturn
most of their decriminalisation laws and to regulate paraphernalia so heavily that many
shops stopped selling it.

In [November 1981](/history/us-nancy-reagan-parent-movement-1981/) Nancy Reagan made the
parents' cause her own, and "Just Say No" followed. By 1983 there were more than three
thousand groups.

## The end

The movement declined for a reason its members could be proud of: teenage marijuana use
fell, and the urgency went with it. By the mid-1980s public alarm had moved to crack
cocaine, and Congress brought back mandatory minimum sentences in
[1986](/history/us-anti-drug-abuse-act-1986/).

The index lists the movement for context. It moved no federal law, but it explains why
the decriminalisation of the 1970s stopped where it did, and why the
[United States](/country/us/) waited until 1996 for its next reform.
