Dutch cannabis policy is often described as a pragmatic accident. It was designed, by
two committees whose members overlapped, between 1968 and 1972.

## Two commissions

Both were set up in 1968. One was a private initiative of the national federation of
mental health organisations, chaired by Louk Hulsman, a professor of criminal law in
Rotterdam. The other was official, appointed by the under-secretary for health and
chaired from 1970 by Pieter Baan. Four people sat on both.

A 1969 survey of about a
thousand drug users had found addiction rare and imprisonment the most damaging
consequence of use.

## Hulsman's argument

The [Hulsman report](/history/netherlands-hulsman-commission-1971/), in October 1971,
put the case in terms of markets. If the law drives the users of every drug into one
criminal subculture, cannabis smokers will meet heroin there. The answer was to keep
the markets apart. It recommended taking cannabis use and small possession out of
criminal law altogether.

It found no evidence that cannabis led to harder drugs by its own action. Exposure
came from the company a banned drug forced people to keep.

## Baan's argument

The Baan report reached the health minister in February 1972. It proposed sorting drugs
by the risk they posed, "acceptable" or "unacceptable", and said plainly that
pharmacology alone could not be the state's guide: the setting in which a drug is used
matters as much.

It stopped short of legal supply, which it judged contrary to the spirit of the
[Single Convention](/history/un-single-convention-1961/). It suggested that trade in
cannabis under a quarter of a kilogram be treated as a minor offence.

## What the government did with it

The justice minister, Dries van Agt, thought the report too cautious. In
[1974](/history/netherlands-memorandum-1974/) the government said it would remove
cannabis from criminal justice entirely if the treaties allowed.

They did not, so the [1976 Opium Act](/history/netherlands-toleration-1976/) did what
Baan had proposed. Drugs were divided into two lists by risk, small possession became a
minor offence that prosecutors would not pursue, and the penalty for trafficking in
other drugs rose from four years to twelve.

That last part is often forgotten. The Dutch model was never general leniency. It was
a bargain: tolerance for cannabis, paid for with severity towards everything else.
