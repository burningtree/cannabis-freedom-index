Canada opened a federal legal-access system for medical cannabis on 30 July 2001.
The Marihuana Medical Access Regulations let qualifying patients obtain permission
to possess dried cannabis and a licence for the patient or a designated person to
produce it.

## Courts forced a workable exemption

Before the regulations, possession, production and distribution were generally
prohibited. An Ontario court held in 1997 that applying those prohibitions to a
person producing cannabis for medical use infringed the right to life, liberty and
security of the person under section 7 of the Charter.

The exemption system that followed was itself found inadequate by the courts in
2000 for people with a defined medical need. The federal government responded
with the Marihuana Medical Access Regulations, or MMAR, in 2001.

## Permission to possess and produce

Part 1 of the regulations authorised a permit holder to possess dried cannabis for
their own medical purpose. Applicants had to ordinarily reside in Canada and
provide medical declarations, with the precise requirements depending on the
category of symptoms.

The application also had to identify the planned source. Cannabis could be
produced by the patient, produced by a named designated person, or obtained under
the separate Narcotic Control Regulations from a named licensed dealer.

Part 2 created the production licences. A personal-use production licence allowed
the patient to produce and keep cannabis for their own medical use. A
designated-person licence allowed another eligible person to grow on the patient's
behalf. Maximum plant and storage quantities were tied to the authorised daily
dosage rather than being a single allowance for every patient.

The scheme was narrow and administrative, but it replaced ad hoc exemptions with
a durable nationwide federal regulatory path. Later court decisions found further limits too
restrictive, leading to replacement frameworks in 2013 and 2016 before the
[Cannabis Act took effect in 2018](/history/canada-cannabis-act-2018/).
