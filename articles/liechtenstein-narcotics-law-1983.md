Liechtenstein's narcotics law is dated 20 April 1983 and was published in the national law gazette on 30 July 1983, the day it came into force. It was signed by Prince Franz Josef and the head of government, Hans Brunhart. The law has been amended many times since, but it is still the one in force.

## Cannabis in the text

Article 2 defines narcotics as dependence-producing substances of three types: morphine, cocaine and cannabis. The list of raw materials names "Hanfkraut", the hemp plant, and the list of active substances names the resin of its glandular hairs.

Article 6 goes further for four substances, which may not be grown, made or put into circulation at all: smoking opium, heroin, hallucinogens such as LSD, and "hemp for obtaining narcotics and the resin of its glandular hairs (hashish)". The only opening is for research institutes, which the government may permit to grow hemp for their own needs.

## Penalties

| Offence | Penalty in the 1983 text |
|---|---|
| Growing hemp for narcotics, selling, supplying, possessing, buying (Article 20) | Strict arrest of up to three years or a fine of up to 100,000 francs |
| Serious cases: large quantity, gang, commercial dealing | One to twenty years, and a fine of up to 1 million francs |
| Consuming, or any Article 20 act done for one's own consumption (Article 21) | Arrest of up to six months or a fine of up to 5,000 francs |

Two provisions soften the regime for users. Under Article 21, "in minor cases punishment may be waived". Under Article 22, a court may suspend a prison sentence of up to two years for an offence committed because of dependence, if the person undergoes treatment.

## What came before

The law was not written on a blank page. Article 1 says that imports, exports and transit of narcotics follow the rules applicable in Liechtenstein under the customs treaty, and Article 30 repeals a Princely Ordinance of 13 July 1982. The text does not say what that ordinance contained.

## Why the index moved

The index dates Liechtenstein's prohibition to this law because it is the first national statute it has read that names cannabis and sets penalties for growing, selling, possessing and using it. All six scores fall at this point. Enforcement lands at 3 rather than lower because the 1983 text treats personal use as a minor offence that a court may leave unpunished, and lets courts put treatment in place of prison.
