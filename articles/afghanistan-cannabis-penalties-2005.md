Afghanistan's drug law of December 2005 gave personal cannabis use and
possession a prison term of one to three months and a fine. Holding more
than ten grams of hashish brought the trafficking penalty scale into play,
even when the person said it was for their own consumption.

## A replacement law combined punishment and treatment

The republic's new law aimed to prevent drug cultivation and trafficking,
regulate controlled substances and establish services for detoxification,
treatment and rehabilitation. It replaced an earlier drug law identified
in its repeal clause.

Cannabis and cannabis resin appeared in the controlled-drug table. The
law addressed the growing plant, the harvested drug and the person
using it through separate rules. That distinction matters in a country
where the [first national cannabis survey](/history/afghanistan-top-producer-2010/)
would later document a large resin harvest despite decades of prohibition.

## Ten grams changed which penalty scale applied

Unauthorised personal cannabis use or possession carried one to three
months in prison plus a fine of 5,000–10,000 afghanis. The law expressly
sent possession of more than ten grams of hashish to its trafficking
penalties.

For the controlled-drug group containing cannabis, trafficking less than
250 grams carried up to three months and a fine. The ranges rose with
weight: one to five kilograms brought one to three years, and more
than ten kilograms brought ten to fifteen years, with fines alongside
the prison terms.

The ten-gram rule therefore changed the legal route through which the
person was sentenced. A quantity inside the personal-use range still
exposed its holder to criminal punishment.

## Growers faced prison and destruction of the crop

Growing less than one jerib, a traditional land measure, of cannabis carried three to nine months
in prison and a fine of 5,000–20,000 afghanis. Larger plots attracted
additional punishment according to the excess area.

Owners, occupiers and cultivators had to destroy illicit plants growing
on their land. The law also required destruction without compensation
and doubled the farmer's penalty for someone who encouraged, caused,
incited or financed the cultivation.

## A doctor and court could open a treatment route

If a doctor certified drug dependence, a court could exempt the person
from prison and the fine and require attendance at a treatment centre.
This depended on a medical assessment and a court decision.

The [2010 replacement law](/history/afghanistan-hashish-penalties-eased-2010/)
then introduced a separate hashish trafficking scale and an exemption
from prosecution for medically certified dependent users with limited
personal quantities. The distinctions between growing, possession and
treatment continued to shape [Afghanistan's drug laws](/country/af/).
