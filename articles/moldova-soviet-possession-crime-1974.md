Soviet Moldavia came under the USSR’s April 1974 drug decree, whose personal-handling offence covered making, acquiring, holding, transporting or sending drugs without an intention to sell.[@1] The provision carried up to three years in prison or up to two years of corrective labour, placing personal possession within the criminal system.[@1]

## Possession had its own criminal provision

The offence distinguished handling drugs for oneself from handling them for supply.[@1,2] It reached acquisition and storage as well as manufacture and transport, so completing a sale was unnecessary for criminal liability under this distinct personal-handling provision.[@1,2] The later consolidated text retained that general personal-handling provision alongside the small-quantity paragraphs added in 1987.[@1,2]

Those later additions matter when reading the law's history: the consolidated version brings together rules adopted at different times.[@2] The separately dated [1987 amendment](/history/moldova-soviet-small-possession-1987/) identifies when first small-quantity acquisition and possession received an administrative tier.[@2]

## Small quantities later received a different penalty

Under the June 1987 amendment, acquiring or holding a small amount without intent to sell could bring a fine up to 100 roubles or one to two months of corrective labour, with twenty per cent of earnings withheld.[@2] In exceptional cases, authorities could instead impose up to fifteen days of administrative arrest.[@2]

Repetition within a year after an administrative penalty for the relevant possession or use violation could bring up to two years in prison, corrective labour for the same period, or a fine up to 300 roubles.[@2] The new tier therefore changed the consequences of a first small-possession case while retaining criminal exposure for repetition.[@2]

## Surrender and treatment could avoid liability

The amendment also provided relief for someone voluntarily surrendering drugs, covering acquisition and specified handling of those drugs.[@2] A person voluntarily seeking medical help for non-medical drug use could avoid the specified use liability and liability for handling the drugs consumed.[@2]

Later Moldovan law continued to distinguish quantity classes.[@3] In [2015, the Constitutional Court upheld](/history/moldova-quantity-tables-upheld-2015/) the government's role in defining small, large and especially large amounts, which helped determine the applicable liability and punishment.[@3] The [Moldova profile](/country/md/) describes the current tiers and their consequences.[@3]
