On 18 June 1946, Lebanon's parliament passed a narcotics law that banned cannabis cultivation outright.
Article 2 named *al-qinnab al-hindi*, "known as hashishat al-kayf" (حشيشة الكيف), and the opium poppy,
and said no licence could be granted to grow either. As passed, a grower faced one to three years in
prison and a fine of 100 to 10,000 Lebanese pounds.[@1]

## A crop the mandate never controlled

Cannabis was not a marginal crop. One estimate cited by Michelle Wazan put the share of the Lebanese
economy that depended on hashish production at 50 per cent between 1920 and the mid-1940s; in 1928, she
writes, 90 per cent of the crop went to Egypt.[@3] The French mandate had banned hashish production in
[1926](/history/lebanon-hashish-ban-1926/), but the ban was nominal: the French authorities had few
officials on the ground and relied on local authorities to apply it.[@3] The independent state inherited
a trade the mandate had never controlled.[@3]

## A ban with no licensing exception

The 1946 law was broader than cannabis.[@1] Article 1 banned the use, possession, manufacture, transport
and sale of narcotics, but let the health ministry license production and imports for medical and
scientific needs.[@1] Article 2 made cannabis and opium the exception: their cultivation was forbidden,
with no licensing exception.[@1] Article 3 set one to three years and 100 to 10,000 pounds.[@1] It
also reached beyond the grower: a landowner, village headman or guard who knew about a crop could be
punished, and the administration could destroy fields without waiting for a conviction.[@1] Moving
plants to save them, or stopping the destruction, brought the same penalty.[@1]

## The trade outlived the law

The law did not end the trade.[@2] J. Bouquet, writing in the UN *Bulletin on Narcotics* in 1951,
recorded that "large quantities of hashish of Syrio-Lebanese origin were seized in Egypt in 1947, 1948
and 1949". He also quoted the Egyptian narcotics bureau's 1937 line that "the Lebanese have this trade
and cultivation in their blood".[@2] Later accounts describe the law being applied to small farmers, not
the big growers.[@3] In 1950 a US narcotics agent named the speaker of parliament, Sabri Hamade, as the
Bekaa's biggest cannabis farmer. Hamade also sat on the parliamentary committee that began promoting
eradication that year, which, Wazan writes, was allegedly meant partly to eliminate competitors.[@3] The
[1951 campaign](/history/lebanon-eradication-campaign-1951/) sent armoured cars to protect work gangs
destroying crops.[@3]

## What came next

The 1946 law stayed in force until 1998, when Law 673 replaced it.[@1] Cannabis stayed
prohibited.[@4] In [2020](/history/lebanon-medical-cultivation-2020/), Law 178 created a licensing
exception for medical, therapeutic, scientific and industrial cultivation, but not for recreational
growing.[@4] The regulator was appointed only in July 2025, and [no cultivation licence had been
issued](/history/lebanon-no-cannabis-licence-2026/) by 17 August 2026.[@5] The Bekaa crop is still grown
outside the law, as it was when the 1946 law was signed.[@5] Read the current law and penalties on the
[Lebanon page](/country/lb/).
