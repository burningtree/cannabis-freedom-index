People convicted of cannabis offences under Sierra Leone's new pharmacy law faced at least fifteen years in prison from December 2001.[@1] The prohibition reached smokers, growers, people holding cannabis and people who let others smoke on their premises.[@1]

## A pharmacy law reached into people's homes

The law regulated pharmacists, medicines and their supply, but it also dealt directly with cannabis, using the older name Indian hemp.[@1] Possession without lawful excuse was prohibited, and the person holding the drug had to prove that excuse.[@1]

Smoking was a separate offence.[@1] So was frequenting a place used for smoking cannabis, permitting smoking on premises one owned or occupied, or possessing pipes and other equipment intended for it.[@1] A person could therefore come within the law through the place they visited or the equipment they kept, as well as through the drug itself.[@1]

## Fifteen years was the cannabis-specific minimum

The cannabis penalty said an individual convicted of an offence relating to Indian hemp must receive at least fifteen years in prison.[@1] The general penalty section also included special rules for people with two earlier convictions for particular cannabis offences.[@1]

Growing any plant from which a narcotic drug could be extracted required the Pharmacy Board's written consent.[@1] Written permission, rather than a household plant allowance, was the route the statute provided for cultivation.[@1]

The restrictions also followed cannabis across the border: the minister could not grant an import or export licence for Indian hemp prepared for smoking.[@1]

## Colonial controls stayed alongside it

The new Act replaced the 1988 pharmacy law and kept earlier regulations alive unless revoked.[@1] It expressly left the [colonial Dangerous Drugs Act](/history/sierra-leone-dangerous-drugs-ordinance-1926/) in force.[@1]

That older law already prohibited cannabis cultivation and possession in its surviving revised text, while treating medicinal extracts and tinctures through licensing and pharmacy rules.[@2] The 2001 statute placed its cannabis offences alongside that existing system.[@1]

## A national enforcement law followed

In 2008, parliament passed a [new drug-control law](/history/sierra-leone-drugs-control-act-2008/) after a cocaine-laden aircraft landed in the country and exposed weaknesses in enforcement.[@4] It created a national agency and expressly repealed the colonial Dangerous Drugs Act.[@3]

The 2008 law also said it would prevail where another enactment conflicted with it.[@3] In 2024, the Pharmacy Board relied on the 2001 cultivation-consent power for [medical production guidelines](/history/sierra-leone-medical-cultivation-guidelines-2024/) focused on export.[@5] In [Sierra Leone](/country/sl/), the pharmacy law became a basis for licensed production as well as criminal restrictions.[@1,5]
