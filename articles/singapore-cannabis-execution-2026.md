Singapore executed Omar bin Yacob Bamadhaj on 16 April 2026 for importing just over
one kilogram of cannabis. The quantity was more than twice the threshold at which
importation can be punished by death.

## The border search

Omar entered Singapore from Malaysia by car through Woodlands Checkpoint on 12 July
2018. During a routine check, an auxiliary police officer found his bag. It
contained three bundles wrapped in aluminium foil, cling wrap and newspaper.

The Health Sciences Authority analysed the bundles and found at least 1,009.1 g of
cannabis. Under Singapore's Misuse of Drugs Act, importing more than 500 g of
cannabis is punishable with death.

## Trial and appeal

At trial, Omar said he did not know the bundles contained cannabis. He also alleged
that officers had threatened him while taking his statements.

The High Court found material inconsistencies in his evidence and did not consider
his account reliable. It accepted corroborating accounts from officers, supported
by CCTV footage, and found that he knew the nature of the bundles and intentionally
brought them into Singapore.

The court convicted him of importation on 24 February 2021. The Court of Appeal
upheld both conviction and sentence on 12 October that year, finding no basis to
interfere with the High Court's assessment.

## Further applications

Four post-appeal applications were dismissed or otherwise resolved. After being
notified of the execution date, Omar sought permission to begin another review,
again raising an allegation that an officer had threatened to slap him.

The Court of Appeal said the allegation had already been examined at trial and on
appeal. It found no new or sufficient material and summarily dismissed the
application. His petitions to the president for clemency were unsuccessful.

## A continuing policy

The execution followed the [2023 execution of Tangaraju Suppiah](/history/singapore-tangaraju-execution-2023/)
in another case involving slightly more than one kilogram of cannabis. Together,
the cases show that Singapore's capital threshold is not merely a maximum written
in the statute: it continues to be used.
