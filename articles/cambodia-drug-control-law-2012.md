Cambodia's new drug law set a basic two-to-five-year prison term for unlawful possession, transport and trafficking, while ordinary unlawful cultivation carried six months to two years.[@1] Cannabis, its resin and its oil were included in the most restricted table.[@1]

## Personal possession entered a broader offence

The [2005 rules](/history/cambodia-personal-use-penalties-2005/) had separately punished growing, buying or storing drugs for personal consumption with seven days to one month in prison.[@2] The new law defined unlawful keeping to include possession, concealment and storage, within an offence also covering transport and trafficking.[@1]

Higher penalties applied to specified circumstances and quantities.[@1] Unlawful cultivation had its own rules, with the penalty rising to two to five years when growing was for distribution, manufacture or trade.[@1]

Permission for lawful drug activity was restricted to research, clinical, scientific or industrial purposes.[@1]

## Treatment had prompted public criticism

While the bill was being prepared in 2011, critics [warned about compulsory treatment](/history/cambodia-draft-treatment-warning-2011/) lasting up to two years.[@3] They pointed to allegations of abuse inside detention centres and challenged a draft definition of addiction that could include people simply under the influence.[@3]

The final law, promulgated on 2 January 2012, set out both voluntary and compulsory procedures.[@1] It said treatment should in principle require consent and allowed people receiving voluntary treatment to stop it.[@1]

## Compulsory treatment had conditions and appeal rights

The law linked compulsory treatment to severe dependence and immediate serious risk, or inability to express willingness to accept voluntary care.[@1] A prosecutor receiving a request from relatives or authorities had to seek a specialist medical examination.[@1]

The person could appeal a prosecutor's compulsory-treatment order to the appeals prosecution office, which had to decide within seventy-two hours of receiving the case.[@1] State-centre treatment and rehabilitation could last six months to two years.[@1]

The consumption rules also retained discretion for limited quantities used in non-addictive ancestral practice.[@1]

## The later campaign tested the safeguards

Cambodia launched an [anti-drug campaign in January 2017](/history/cambodia-drug-campaign-2017/) and repeatedly extended it.[@4] Amnesty International later documented allegations of arbitrary detention, abuse and inadequate care across the broader campaign.[@4]

Its [2020 investigation](/history/cambodia-drug-campaign-abuses-report-2020/) called for voluntary services and an end to punishing personal drug use.[@4] The dispute in [Cambodia](/country/kh/) now concerned how the law's treatment promises and procedural rights worked for people caught in enforcement.[@1,4]
