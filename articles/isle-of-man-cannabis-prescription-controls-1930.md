From 1 January 1930, cannabis extracts and tinctures on the Isle of Man remained within a system of authorised supply and medical prescriptions. Patients could lawfully possess medicine supplied under the rules, while sellers faced controls over manufacture, dispensing, storage and records. A companion order dealt separately with Indian hemp and resin.[@1,2]

## Two orders replaced earlier controls

Lieutenant Governor Sir Claude Hamilton Archer Hill made both orders in December 1929. They used powers under the island’s pharmacy and dangerous-drugs legislation. The consolidation replaced dangerous-drugs regulations from 1923 and 1926; the companion order replaced the Coca Leaves and Indian Hemp Regulations of 1928.[@1,2]

The two orders divided cannabis products between different sets of rules. Indian hemp, its resin and preparations based on resin came under the existing raw-opium regulations, with extracts and tinctures expressly excepted. Traders had to use separate registers, or separate parts of a register, for Indian hemp and coca leaves. Extracts and tinctures instead appeared in the dangerous-drugs consolidation.[@1,2]

## Patients needed lawful supply

The consolidation prohibited possession without authority. Someone receiving a drug on a lawful medical prescription, or directly from a doctor dispensing their own medicines, was treated as authorised to possess it. A patient already receiving treatment and drugs from another doctor had to disclose that fact before obtaining a further supply.[@1]

Prescriptions had to be written, signed and dated, identify the patient and specify the quantity. Dispensers had to check that a prescription was genuine and mark it with the dispensing date. Ordinarily it authorised one supply; a prescriber could expressly permit a second or third dispensing after specified intervals.[@1]

## Sellers had to secure their stock

Shops lawfully retailing poisons were authorised to make cannabis extracts and tinctures in their ordinary retail business. Their drug stocks had to be kept in a locked receptacle accessible only to the authorised person or a pharmacist assistant.[@1]

Suppliers also had to record quantities obtained and supplied, with a separate register or register section for Indian-hemp extracts, tinctures and preparations. Registers and other required documents generally had to be retained for two years. The rules made the supply chain traceable as well as restricting who could handle the drugs.[@1]

## Possession later brought prison

The island’s courts subsequently developed a stern approach to unlawful possession. Legal historian Peter W. Edge records that in [Mandryk in 1978](/history/isle-of-man-mandryk-cannabis-prison-1978/), Deemster Luft upheld a month in prison for cannabis possession and defended deterrent sentences.[@3] The [Isle of Man profile](/country/im/) follows the later medical reforms and today’s rules.
