In Soviet Russia's 1974 drug-law rewrite, keeping drugs for oneself could
bring up to three years in prison or one year of corrective labour. A plan
to sell was unnecessary for that offence. Specified repeat offences carried
up to five years.[@1]

## A Union decree, then a Russian amendment

The Russian republic issued its amendments on 15 July, expressly following
the Union decree of 25 April on intensifying the fight against drug
addiction. The amendments rewrote the republic's criminal code and changed
health legislation as well.[@1] The [July entry](/history/russia-drug-code-amendments-1974/)
records that implementation separately.

The new possession provision covered unlawful manufacture, acquisition,
keeping, transport and dispatch without an intention to supply. Supply
and activities undertaken with that intention had their own, heavier
penalties: up to ten years, or six to fifteen years with confiscation in
aggravated cases. Aggravating factors included repetition, a group's prior
agreement and large quantities.[@1]

## Cannabis growing had its own offence

The cultivation provision named opium poppy and Indian, southern Manchurian
and southern Chuy hemp, alongside other prohibited drug-bearing crops.
Growing them carried up to five years; specified repeat offences raised
the band to three to eight years.[@1] This continued the
[earlier cultivation controls](/history/russia-rsfsr-article-225-1960/)
through a rewritten provision.

The same decree created or restated offences covering theft of drugs,
inducing drug consumption and providing premises for consumption. Keeping
or organising such premises could bring five to ten years, with possible
confiscation.[@1] The regime reached people who helped supply a drug or
provided somewhere to use it as well as those who held it.

## Compulsory treatment also meant confinement

The health-law amendments required drug-dependent people to undergo
treatment. A district or city people's court could send those evading it
to a treatment-and-labour institution for six months to two years.[@1]

Human Rights Watch's later account describes these institutions as run by
the same ministry as prisons, with a regime resembling penal colonies.
Commitment proceedings were simplified and decisions could not be
appealed.[@2]

## The challenge that came later

The [1990 constitutional-supervision decision](/history/soviet-union-use-ruling-1990/)
challenged coercive treatment and criminal liability for drug use. Human
Rights Watch describes its insistence that ill people who had committed
no offence should receive voluntary treatment in medical institutions.[@2]
Possession law followed its own course, traced in the
[1997 criminal-code article](/history/russia-criminal-code-1997/)
and the [Russia profile](/country/ru/).
