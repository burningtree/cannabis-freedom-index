On 11 August 2004 Bhutan's Penal Code took effect with the country's first statutory drug offences. Possessing or using cannabis without a doctor's prescription became a petty misdemeanour, carrying one month to under a year in prison. Growing, making or trading in it became a fourth-degree felony, carrying three to under five years.[@1]

## A notification without penalties

Drug control had rested on a [1988 government notification](/history/bhutan-1988-narcotics-notification/), which gave the Royal Bhutan Police responsibility for enforcement but set no penalties.[@2] Police made arrests anyway: the [first recorded drug arrest, for cannabis](/history/bhutan-first-arrest-1989/), was in Gelephu on 29 June 1989, and by 2005 officers had made 817 drug arrests, 603 of them in Thimphu.[@2,5] The Penal Code repealed the notification and replaced it with a penalty scale.[@1]

## Possession, supply and the intent to sell

The code did not name cannabis. It covered "any narcotic drug or psychotropic substance", and it repealed the 1988 notification on narcotic drugs.[@1,2] Section 496 made unauthorised cultivation, production or manufacture a fourth-degree felony. Section 498 did the same for importing, exporting, selling, purchasing, hoarding, storing or transporting. Section 500 made possession or use without a registered doctor's prescription a petty misdemeanour, and section 501 raised it to a misdemeanour — one to under three years — if the court thought the amount showed an intention to sell.[@1]

That last clause put weight on the quantity. A person found with more than they could explain could be treated as a dealer without evidence of a sale. The supply offence was broad too: carrying cannabis from one place to another, or storing it, counted as an illegal transaction.[@1]

## The 2005 act took over

The code's drug sections lasted just over a year. The [Narcotic Drugs, Psychotropic Substances and Substance Abuse Act 2005](/history/bhutan-narcotics-act-2005/), in force on 29 September 2005, replaced them with a dedicated cannabis offence. Cultivation and domestication became offences, harvesting was prohibited except for fibre and animal feed, and landowners had to destroy cannabis found growing on their land.[@3]

The [2015 narcotics act](/history/bhutan-narcotics-act-2015/) replaced that law and set the 50-gram, 7-gram and five-plant thresholds still used today. The [2017 amendment](/history/bhutan-narcotics-amendment-2017/) added a treatment route for people holding up to twice the threshold who test positive, have no trafficking record and are not shown to be trafficking.[@4] The 2004 code's basic arrangement — criminal possession and heavier penalties for supply — survived both, though the amounts and penalties are now more finely graded. The [country profile](/country/bt/) sets out what that means for someone caught today.
