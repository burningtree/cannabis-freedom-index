In 1914, British authorities in the East Africa Protectorate brought cannabis
under an opium ordinance. The territory that became Kenya was regulating the
plant before international drug treaties imposed cannabis controls.

Neil Carrier's historical account identifies two overlapping forces behind the
measure: the emerging international opium framework and colonial officials'
earlier concerns about cannabis within the protectorate.

## An opium law reaches cannabis

The ordinance followed the
[1912 Hague Opium Convention](/history/hague-opium-convention-1912/).
That treaty did not itself cover cannabis. Including the plant in the
protectorate's legislation therefore went beyond simply transcribing an
international cannabis obligation.

Carrier describes the 1914 restrictions as applying to *Cannabis indica*. His
example complicates an account in which African cannabis prohibition arrived
only after the [1925 Geneva convention](/history/geneva-opium-convention-1925/).
In this case, colonial legislation had already brought the plant into the
drug-control system more than a decade earlier.

## Officials had already asked for restrictions

The concern preceded The Hague. Officials in western Kenya and on the coast
reported worries about cannabis production, imports and consumption.

In [February 1908](/history/kenya-commissioner-asks-for-ban-1908/), John Ainsworth,
the provincial commissioner at Kisumu, wrote to the Crown Advocate in Mombasa
seeking a law against cultivation. He associated cannabis with harm and crime.
These were an official's arguments for prohibition, rather than independently
established findings about what cannabis caused.

The sequence separates advocacy from enactment. Ainsworth's letter documents a
request; the ordinance records a later legal restriction. The letter alone does
not prove that its author determined every provision of the resulting law.

## A plant with an existing history

Carrier places the Kenyan measure within a much longer eastern and southern
African history of cannabis use, exchange and medicine. Officials were
regulating a substance already present in regional life, rather than a plant
introduced by the new treaty system.

That context helps explain why the ordinance mattered beyond its title. An
opium framework could be extended to practices involving a different plant,
making colonial drug administration a means of changing their legal status.

## What the record establishes

Carrier cites notices in the protectorate's official gazette for 1913 and 1914.
This article follows his account; those gazette notices have not been
independently inspected here. It does not reconstruct offence wording, licence
exceptions or penalty scales from the chapter's brief description.

For [Kenya's timeline](/country/ke/), the event marks the early legal restriction.
Its place in the record should be distinguished from evidence about how often
people were arrested or how consistently the colonial authorities enforced it.
