South Africa's 1922 Customs and Excise Duties Amendment Act turned earlier provincial restrictions into national cannabis prohibition. The law criminalised *dagga* across the Union and began a national enforcement regime that lasted, in changing forms, for almost a century.

## From provincial controls to a national ban

Cannabis had long been cultivated, traded and consumed in southern Africa. Colonial governments had already imposed racially targeted controls: Natal restricted cannabis for Indian workers, and the Cape later prohibited it more broadly. Those measures did not yet amount to one rule covering the Union.

Act No. 35 of 1922 changed that position. Academic histories identify it as the first national prohibition, classifying dagga with other “habit-forming drugs.” Cultivation, possession, use and sale became matters for criminal enforcement, and police began recording cannabis arrests and seizures.

## Enforcement and race

The new law did not fall evenly. University of Johannesburg research describes the penalisation of predominantly African users whose communities had a much longer history of cannabis use. Later policing targeted both personal possession and the cultivation and supply networks that continued despite the ban.

That racial impact persisted through later governments. Historian Thembisa Waetjen describes disproportionate imprisonment of Black South Africans as a continuing feature of cannabis criminalisation, rather than an incidental consequence confined to one period.

## From domestic law to international control

South Africa also helped push cannabis onto the international agenda. Prime Minister Jan Smuts's government subsequently asked the League of Nations to treat cannabis as a habit-forming drug. The request is recorded separately in the [1923 history entry](/history/south-africa-league-request-1923/).

The 1922 provisions were moved into the Medical, Dental and Pharmacy Act in 1928. Prohibition then became progressively harsher, culminating in the [mandatory penalties of 1971](/history/south-africa-mandatory-minimums-1971/). Private possession, use and cultivation were not constitutionally protected until [2018](/history/south-africa-constitutional-court-2018/).

## What moved in the index

The national ban cut [South Africa's](/country/za/) possession, cultivation and consumption scores from 6 to 1. Enforcement fell from 6 to 2, sharing from 5 to 1 and access from 5 to 0. The broad fall reflects a national prohibition reaching every scored part of personal cannabis freedom.
