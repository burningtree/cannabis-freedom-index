For more than three decades before the 2022 fines, Israel's cannabis law and its police practice pointed in different directions. The statute allowed prison for personal possession. The attorney general's office told police not to use it against first-time users of small amounts.[@1]

## The directive

In 1985 the attorney general issued guidance telling police not to arrest or prosecute first-time users caught with small quantities for personal use. The policy was reported as renewed in 2003.[@1] It did not change the Dangerous Drugs Ordinance: section 7(c) still allowed up to three years in prison for possession for personal consumption, and up to twenty years for possession not for personal use.[@3]

The directive was an enforcement policy, not a legal exemption. A person could still be arrested, charged and convicted; the guidance told police and prosecutors to exercise restraint. The [1985 entry](/history/israel-attorney-general-directive-1985/) records it.

## A widening gap

The gap grew in the 2010s. Ministers and Knesset members proposed decriminalisation, and in 2017 an inter-ministerial committee recommended a formal administrative fine scheme.[@2] That scheme arrived as a temporary order in 2018 and became permanent in the 2022 regulations, which set a 500-shekel fine for possession or private use, 1,000 shekels for public use, and no criminal record.[@1]

The 2022 rules also extended the administrative route to minors in 2023 and excluded soldiers, reservists in certain police or prison units, and prisoners. They did not legalise supply: section 13 still treats any supply, paid or free, as dealing.[@3]

## The limit of the model

The 1985 directive showed that a police force could decline to enforce a law without parliament changing it. The 2022 regulations went further, putting the fine on the statute book. But the fine scheme was then barely used: police issued no administrative cannabis fines between 2022 and 2025.[@1]

That outcome makes the 1985 directive the beginning of a long Israeli pattern: the law on the books is harsh, the practice is milder, and the two are reconciled by discretion rather than by a new statute.
