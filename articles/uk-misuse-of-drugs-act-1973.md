The Misuse of Drugs Act 1971 came into force on 1 July 1973 and is still the law that governs cannabis in the United Kingdom. For cannabis it was, narrowly, a relaxation: the maximum sentence for possession was halved, from ten years to five.

## The report behind it

In 1967 the Home Office's Advisory Committee on Drug Dependence set a sub-committee, chaired by Baroness Wootton, to study cannabis and LSD. That July an advertisement in The Times declared the dangers of cannabis exaggerated and the law socially damaging, and the sub-committee decided to report on cannabis first.

The Wootton Report, dated 1968 and published in January 1969, concluded that long-term use in moderate doses had no harmful effects and that cannabis was less dangerous than opiates, amphetamines, barbiturates and alcohol. Its main proposal was that possession of a small amount "should not normally be regarded as a serious crime to be punished by imprisonment".

The Home Secretary, James Callaghan, told Parliament the committee had been "over-influenced" by the legalisation lobby. A year later he introduced the consolidating bill that became the 1971 Act.

## What the Act did

The Act consolidated the earlier drug laws into a single scheme.

- Controlled drugs are sorted into three classes, A, B and C, and penalties for possession and supply are graded by class.
- Cannabis and cannabis resin were placed in Class B.
- A drug can be moved between classes by order, after the government has consulted a new statutory body, the Advisory Council on the Misuse of Drugs.
- The Home Secretary licenses lawful production and supply.

The halved maximum for possession is the one Wootton recommendation that reached the statute book. Possession stayed a crime that could be punished with prison, and the Act kept the police power of stop and search introduced in [1967](/history/uk-stop-and-search-1967/).

## Why the index moved

The index moved the [United Kingdom](/country/gb/) from 11 to 13 with the [1971 Act](/history/uk-misuse-of-drugs-act-1973/). Only enforcement changed, from 2 to 3, for the lower ceiling on possession. The class system it created is what made the later moves possible: down to Class C in [2004](/history/uk-class-c-2004/) and back to Class B in [2009](/history/uk-class-b-2009/).
