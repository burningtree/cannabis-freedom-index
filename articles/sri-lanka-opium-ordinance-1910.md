Ceylon's Opium Ordinance of 1910 addressed cannabis as well as the drug in its
short title. Its longer title expressly named possession and sale of bhang and
ganja, while dedicated sections regulated those substances separately from the
ordinance's opium arrangements.

The measure passed the Legislative Council on 6 May and received the governor's
assent on 2 June 1910. Publication in the gazette followed on 17 June.

## A longer history of licensed shops

C. G. Uragoda's history places the reform against decades of licensed opium
retailing. The [1867 ordinance](/history/sri-lanka-licensing-1867/) had established
a licensing framework associated with opium and bhang. Revenue from opium shops
went to government and local authorities, while critics pressed for restrictions.

Uragoda describes opposition involving Buddhist reformers and Legislative
Council member S. C. Obeyesekere, followed by questions in the British Parliament.
A committee subsequently recommended closing opium shops and making importation,
distribution and sale a government monopoly. This supplied a political context
for changing the system, rather than treating the 1910 law as an isolated event.

## Cannabis provisions inside an opium law

Section 24 covered possessing, transferring, giving and selling bhang or ganja.
The definitions extended those terms to substances containing them and extracts
or preparations of Indian hemp. The law therefore reached beyond a commercial
sale at a licensed shop.

The same section provided criminal penalties, search and seizure powers, and
court-ordered destruction. Its treatment of gifts and transfers is particularly
relevant to distinguishing restrictions on supply from restrictions confined to
retail trade.

## An exception tied to another licensing system

Section 25 preserved specified activities by people licensed to sell poisons
under the 1901 Poisons Ordinance, provided they complied with its regulations.
The cannabis restriction consequently operated alongside a conditional licensed
route. Section 26 placed the burden of establishing an applicable exception on
the person relying on it.

These provisions describe a prohibition with exceptions. Neither a blanket
permission for medical use nor an exception-free ban captures their structure.

## Assent was not commencement

Section 1 required the governor to appoint commencement by proclamation and
allowed different dates for different provisions. The verified assent date
therefore does not establish when every cannabis provision became operative.

The NDDCB's later chronology distinguishes this measure from the
[1929 consolidation](/history/sri-lanka-dangerous-drugs-enactment-1929/),
[1935 amendment](/history/sri-lanka-dangerous-drugs-ordinance-1935/) and
[1936 commencement](/history/sri-lanka-dangerous-drugs-commencement-1936/).
For [Sri Lanka's timeline](/country/lk/), that sequence shows why several dates
are needed to describe the development of cannabis controls.
