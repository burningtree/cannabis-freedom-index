In the early 1970s psychiatrists from the University of Athens built the
first epidemiological portrait of Greece's hashish population from police
files. Searching the narcotics archives of Athens, Piraeus and the Athens
suburbs for 1958 to 1973, they counted 3,696 drug users, of whom 3,128 were
men who used only hashish.[@1]

## Fifteen years of police files

The team — a psychiatrist and a social worker — spent two to three hours a
day for five months in the headquarters of the three narcotic units, whose
archives cover all of Greece except Patras and Corfu. Record-keeping differed
between units, and one abuser could hold records in all three; the
researchers compared and cross-referenced 5,589 usable records into one card
per person, discarding 1,893 duplicates.[@1]

Of the 3,696, besides the 3,128 hashish-only men there were 107 women who
used only hashish, 330 heroin addicts, 52 users of other drugs such as
amphetamines and barbiturates, and 186 foreigners arrested in Greece. Women
were left out of the analysis because the team's earlier clinical sample had
been male-only, as were people living permanently abroad. The team sorted
the men into five socioeconomic bands and nine regions and set the result
against the 1971 census.[@1]

## A working-class habit with a long past

The study confirmed what the history suggested: from the 1870s hashish had
belonged to port workers, sailors, porters and cart drivers, and to the
rebetiko musicians whose music carried it across the harbours. The
[1890 ban](/history/greece-hashish-ban-1890/) never closed the tekedes, the
Piraeus and Athens hashish cafés; use flourished in the 1920s as soldiers
returned from Asia Minor and a million and a half refugees arrived, and
persisted despite the [1920 cultivation ban](/history/greece-cultivation-ban-1920/).[@1]

From 1932 to 1970 the laws grew steadily harsher — the [1932 stocks
law](/history/greece-stocks-law-1932/) ordered hidden resin destroyed — but
they were not strictly enforced until the civil war ended in 1950, after
which cultivation and use gradually fell. By the early 1970s the tariff ran
from one to ten years in prison, fines of 50,000 to 10 million drachmas,
lifelong loss of the driving licence, confiscation of property, dishonourable
discharge and a ban on foreign travel — though an addict confirmed by the
medical service counted as sick and earned a lighter sentence. The authors
noted use falling among workers but rising among middle- and upper-class
teenagers and students.[@1]

## What came next

Four decades later the tariff collapsed: the [2013 law](/history/greece-law-4139-2013/)
cut personal-use possession and growing to at most five months, normally
suspended and kept off the record, and medical use followed in 2017. The
[country profile](/country/gr/) follows the later history.[@1]
