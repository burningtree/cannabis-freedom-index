On 8 August 2004 Iraq's interim government brought back the death penalty for drug trafficking, but only where the trade was meant to fund or help an armed attempt to overthrow the government.[@1,2] The order ended a suspension that had lasted since June 2003 and put drug cases back inside a capital system that also covered murder, kidnapping and attacks on the state.[@1,2]

## A suspension the new government wanted to end

The occupation authority had [suspended capital punishment in June 2003](/history/iraq-death-penalty-suspension-2003/), replacing death with life imprisonment where death had been the only penalty.[@3] Its order said the former regime had used criminal law to repress people, and kept capital punishment off the statute book during the occupation.[@3] Ministers about to join the new government had already argued that the present circumstances required capital punishment.[@1] Vice President Ghazi al-Yawir announced the decision in July; European Union foreign ministers objected the same day.[@1]

## Drug trafficking returned, with a political condition

Prime Minister Ayad Allawi's interim government issued the order on 8 August 2004, as an exception to the occupation order.[@1,2] It restored death for several crimes in the 1969 penal code, including premeditated murder, kidnapping and offences against internal security.[@1] For drugs, it reached the trafficking offences in Article 14 of the 1965 narcotics law only when the offender acted "with the aim of funding or abetting" the acts against the state listed in Article 190 of the penal code — attempts to overthrow the government by force.[@1,2]

That condition limited the restored death penalty to a political subset of trafficking. A dealer with no political purpose still faced imprisonment, not execution.[@2,3] The order also required the prime minister's approval and the presidential council's confirmation before an execution, and it reduced death sentences that had already become final to life imprisonment.[@1,2]

## The political link did not last

The 2017 narcotics law replaced the 1965 statute and dropped the Article 190 condition.[@4] It made death or life imprisonment the penalty for importing, exporting, producing or cultivating drugs with intent to traffic, and life or temporary imprisonment for other trafficking.[@4] The [2017 replacement](/history/iraq-narcotics-law-2017/) therefore left the death penalty available for ordinary commercial trafficking, not only for trade tied to an armed group.[@4]

The sentencing figures show the result. Harm Reduction International recorded 144 or more drug-related death sentences in 2024 and 40 or more in 2025, about a fifth of the latter for cannabis-related offences; Iraq executed people in 2025, but none for drugs.[@5] Drug offences drove 68 per cent of Iraq's death sentences in 2024 and 42 per cent in 2025, long after the 2004 order's political test had gone.[@5] The [2024](/history/iraq-drug-death-sentences-2024/) and [2025](/history/iraq-drug-death-sentences-2025/) counts made Iraq one of the places where trafficking still carried a realistic threat of execution.[@5]

The [Iraq profile](/country/iq/) sets out the present law: one to three years and a fine for personal use, and death or life imprisonment for trafficking.[@4]
