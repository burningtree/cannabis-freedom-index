Herer's discussion of school urine testing raises a freedom question beyond possession:
whether access to an activity can depend on submitting to a drug test.

On 26 June 1995, the Supreme Court upheld Vernonia's testing policy for student athletes.
The school's laboratory screened for marijuana, cocaine and amphetamines. A confirmed
positive could lead to an assistance programme or suspension from athletics.

The decision's scope was narrower than all extracurricular activities. The Court warned
against assuming that suspicionless testing would be constitutional in other contexts;
Justice Ginsburg's concurrence emphasised voluntary participation in interscholastic
athletics.

We record the ruling as unscored context on the [US timeline](/country/us/). The index's
six segments measure adult personal cannabis freedom, so this school policy alone does
not justify changing them.

A later ruling, [Earls in 2002](/history/us-earls-drug-testing-2002/), upheld testing
beyond athletics. That later extension should not be attributed to the 1995 holding.
