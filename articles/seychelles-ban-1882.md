In 1882 the Seychelles, then a dependency of Mauritius, copied the Mauritian cannabis law into its own legal system word for word.[@2] The timing was exquisite: just as the islands froze the ban in place, the parent colony was deciding it was dead.[@1]

## A copy of Port Louis law

The copied law was Mauritius's Ordinance No. 2 of 1840, which had banned cannabis amid fears of its use in robberies.[@1] An 1848 law had tightened it further, restricting possession of "gandia" and other poisons such as stramonium.[@1] The setting was mass migration: some 455,000 Indian indentured labourers had brought their ganja techniques and vocabulary to Mauritius, and the laws were meant to hold them down.[@1]

The original ordinance pages were not independently inspected for this article; the ordinance number, the robbery rationale and the later repeal rest on historian Jamie Banks's scholarly account.[@1]

## The parent colony changes its mind

By the 1880s the Mauritian prosecutor general, Etienne Pellereau, considered the prohibition dead.[@1] A vote to repeal it failed in 1882, the same year Seychelles adopted it.[@1,2] Pellereau called the ban "manifestly inoperative" and proved it by sending a messenger into Port Louis, who returned within fifteen minutes with a handful of the drug, which was passed round the members of the committee.[@1]

Ordinance No. 25 of 1883 followed, legalising the import, sale and possession of cannabis in Mauritius.[@1] Mauritius reopened the cannabis trade from 1883 until 1887, and it was unclear whether those changes in the law applied to the Seychelles at all.[@2] The dependency's statute book still carried the copied ban while the parent colony's trade ran openly.[@2]

## What came next

Seychelles eventually built its own drug law on top of the inheritance: a [dangerous drugs ordinance in 1935](/history/seychelles-dangerous-drugs-ordinance-1935/), tightened to cover [cannabis extract preparations in 1949](/history/seychelles-cannabis-extract-preparations-1949/), then the misuse-of-drugs acts of 1990 and 2016.[@2] The 2016 act passed by unanimous vote to replace the 1990 law, giving investigators more powers while putting new emphasis on treatment, education and reintegration of users.[@2] Medical cannabis rules followed in [2020](/history/seychelles-medical-2020/), and the 1882 copy survives in [Seychelles's](/country/sc/) history as the islands' first cannabis ban.
