Transnistria narrowed its criminal growing offence and gave courts alternatives to prison for personal possession in 2012. Cultivation had to reach a legally defined large scale to fall within the growing crime. For the ordinary criminal possession offence, judges could now choose a fine or work penalty alongside the existing prison option.[@1]

## A previous penalty could turn another small grow into a crime

Before the reform, the code punished sowing or growing prohibited plants when it happened within a year after an administrative penalty. That ordinary cultivation offence allowed a fine, compulsory work or up to two years in prison. The aggravated tier covered group activity, repeated conduct and a large grow, with three to eight years.[@2]

The amendment replaced the ordinary offence with cultivation on a large scale. It also removed the repeated-conduct aggravation and changed the quantity wording in the aggravated tier to an especially large amount. The legally defined size of the grow became central to the criminal offence.[@1]

For a home grower, this changed the route into a criminal case. The earlier administrative penalty was replaced by a quantity condition in the growing offence. Growing below the criminal threshold could still be punished through administrative law.[@1,3]

## Possession gained a fine or work option

The pre-reform ordinary personal-possession offence applied to a legally defined large amount and listed imprisonment for up to three years as its punishment. Repeated conduct or an especially large amount carried three to ten years.[@2]

The new text kept the ordinary three-year maximum but added a fine of up to 1,000 statutory calculation units, compulsory work and corrective labour. Corrective labour is a work-based criminal penalty. Judges therefore had express non-prison choices in the ordinary possession case.[@1]

The same reform removed repetition from the wording of the higher possession tier. The especially-large-amount offence retained its three-to-ten-year range.[@1,2]

## Supply penalties fell too

Ordinary supply had carried seven to fifteen years in the earlier code. The reform replaced it with four to eight years; its aggravated tiers provided five to twelve or ten to twenty years. Cannabis remained subject to the criminal drug rules, with lower supply ranges and different personal-use penalties.[@1,2]

The president signed the amendment on 8 October, but its final clause expressly gave it effect from 1 October 2012. That earlier date anchors this change in the timeline.[@1]

The later [2014 administrative code](/history/transnistria-administrative-drug-code-2014/) set out warnings or fines for growing below the criminal offence and possible administrative arrest for small possession. The [Transnistria profile](/country/xt/) reflects both routes: administrative punishment below the criminal thresholds, and fines, work penalties or prison within the criminal code.[@3]
