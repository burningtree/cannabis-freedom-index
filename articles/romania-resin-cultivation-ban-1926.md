In 1926 Romania prohibited growing cannabis for resin.[@1] The ban was not home-grown policy so much as a move in a traffickers' chess game being played across the Balkans, with Egypt's demand moving the pieces.[@1,2]

## Egypt's demand moves the pieces

Egypt had criminalised hashish around 1880, and ending cultivation there was the easy part: Nile-bound fields were simple to find and burn.[@2] Consumption survived on smuggling, and by the 1920s Lebanese, Bulgarian, Romanian and Yugoslav farmers were all labouring to meet the Egyptian demand.[@2] The main exporter at the time was Greece, just across the Mediterranean — until Greek politics led it to prohibit production and burn the cannabis fields, as Egypt had done forty years earlier.[@2] Greek farmers lost their income and Egypt lost its most abundant import channel at a stroke.[@2]

Traffickers answered by planting resin cannabis further around Eastern Europe, and each stop ended in prohibition: the [full Balkan sequence](/history/romania-narcotics-law-1928/) runs from Greece through Bulgaria to Romania's 1926 ban and on to Yugoslavia, with the region's fields shut by the late-1920s closures.[@1] Athens even needed a second, more drastic law in 1932 to destroy leftover resin stocks.[@1]

## A different road from Lebanon

Lebanon entered the trade only after Greece's prohibition, when whole valleys turned to cannabis and Palestine became the corridor to Egypt.[@2] But where Romania banned the crop outright in 1926, the French administration in Lebanon never mustered Greek-style resolve: it claimed the problem was Egypt's, its ties to powerful landowning families deterred confrontation, and field-burnings stayed rare.[@2] Two mandate-free and mandate-bound territories, two opposite enforcements of the same Egyptian demand.[@2]

## Locked in by law

The 1926 ban covered the crop; [a 1928 narcotics law](/history/romania-narcotics-law-1928/) locked hashish and its preparations into Romania's drug controls alongside other narcotics.[@3,1] The pair closed the fields the traffickers had opened after Greece's 1920 ban.[@1,3]

Romania kept prohibition as its posture for the rest of the century. A limited medical cannabis law passed in [2013](/history/romania-medical-2013/), allowing only low-THC derivatives — a narrow door that barely opened in practice — while the 1926 ban remains the recorded start of [Romania's](/country/ro/) cannabis prohibition.[@3]
