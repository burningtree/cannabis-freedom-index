In July 1994, the Philippine Supreme Court reduced Martin Simon's life sentence for selling marijuana to an indeterminate prison term of six months to six years.[@1] His case established how courts should apply the new law's lower sentences to small quantities, including offences committed before the amendment.[@1]

## A small sale had brought a life sentence

Simon was prosecuted after a police buy-bust operation in Guagua, Pampanga, in October 1988.[@1] Officers recovered four tea bags containing a total of 3.8 grams of marijuana.[@1] The Court treated the appeal as concerning the sale of two bags; the other two had been seized separately from his possession.[@1]

The trial court imposed life imprisonment and a ₱20,000 fine.[@1] The Supreme Court upheld his conviction, rejecting his challenge to the prosecution's evidence, but a change in the law required it to reconsider his punishment.[@1]

## Restoring death also lowered small-quantity sentences

The [1993 amendment](/history/philippines-death-penalty-1993/) restored capital punishment for specified drug offences involving at least 750 grams of marijuana.[@2] Below that quantity, it prescribed a lower range depending on weight.[@2]

The text nevertheless used the same severe prison penalty, reclusion perpetua, both at the top of the lower range and at the bottom of the capital range.[@1,2] The Court resolved that overlap in the defendant's favour, holding that the lower range should stop at reclusion temporal, a fixed-term prison penalty.[@1]

## The Court divided smaller amounts into three bands

The ruling allocated marijuana below 250 grams to prision correccional, a penalty lasting six months and one day to six years.[@1] The next band covered 250–499 grams, and the highest noncapital band covered 500–749 grams.[@1] Sentencing circumstances and the rules for indeterminate sentences still affected an individual's final term.[@1]

For Simon, the resulting sentence ran from a six-month minimum to a six-year maximum.[@1] The Court also explained that the amended law attached a fine to the reclusion-perpetua-to-death band, rather than these lower bands.[@1]

## Earlier convictions could benefit

The Court applied the favourable amendment retrospectively.[@1] It said people whose convictions were already final could seek relief through habeas corpus, a court procedure for challenging unlawful detention.[@1]

That route mattered in [David Cruz's case in 2000](/history/philippines-cruz-release-2000/): the Court ordered his release after he had served longer than the reduced maximum for 2.70 grams.[@3] The [2002 replacement drug law](/history/philippines-drugs-act-2002/) later imposed new possession bands and restored life-to-death exposure for unauthorised sales regardless of quantity.[@4]
