Malaysia's drug law is a colonial ordinance that was never replaced. The official text still carries its origin on the first page: "First enacted: 1952 (Ordinance No. 30 of 1952)". It came into operation in Peninsular Malaysia on 1 November 1952 and was extended to Sabah and Sarawak on 1 June 1978. It was revised in 1980 as Act 234, the Dangerous Drugs Act 1952.

## What the ordinance was for

The long title describes a regulating law: "further and better provision for the regulating of the importation, exportation, manufacture, sale, and use of opium and of certain other dangerous drugs". Cannabis sits in Part II beside raw opium, coca leaves and poppy straw. Import and export need the Minister's authorisation (sections 4 and 5), and section 6 punishes anyone who holds cannabis, or the seeds of the plant, without it.

## How it hardened

The footnotes of the official text record each increase. Parliament amended the Act in 1972, 1973, twice in 1975, in 1976, three times in 1977, then in 1978, 1980 and 1983, and went on afterwards.

| Provision | Earlier | Now |
|---|---|---|
| Possession fine (section 6) | Up to 10,000 ringgit, until 1986 | Up to 20,000 ringgit, five years, or both |
| Whipping for trafficking (section 39B) | At least six strokes until 1986, then fifteen until 2017 | At least twelve strokes |
| Growing (section 6B) | — | Imprisonment for life and at least six strokes |

Three later additions set the Act apart. Section 37 presumes that a person found with 200 grams or more of cannabis is trafficking. Section 39A raises the penalty for possession by weight, starting at 20 grams of cannabis. Section 39B punishes trafficking with death or imprisonment for life; that wording comes from Act 846, which replaced the earlier subsection and deleted two others.

## Why the index moved

The 1952 ordinance did not make cannabis illegal in Malaya, since earlier colonial laws had already done that, so five of the six scores stay where they were. The index lowers enforcement from 2 to 1 at this point: cannabis now sat in one consolidated statute with opium, licensed by the Minister and punished by the courts, and every later tightening, from the trafficking presumption to caning and the death penalty, was written into this text.
