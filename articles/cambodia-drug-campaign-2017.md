Cambodia launched a six-month anti-drug campaign in January 2017 and repeatedly extended it.[@1] By March 2020, at least 55,770 people had been arrested on suspicion of using or selling drugs, as enforcement fed both criminal courts and administrative detention centres.[@1]

## A short campaign became a lasting policy

The campaign took place as methamphetamine supply expanded across South-East Asia, bringing falling prices and wider availability.[@1] Amnesty International described governments responding with heavy policing, prosecutions and compulsory detention in the name of rehabilitation.[@1]

Cambodia already had a [2012 drug law](/history/cambodia-drug-control-law-2012/) that criminalised unauthorised possession and supply of cannabis and other controlled drugs.[@2] It also provided treatment routes, medical assessments and appeal rights for compulsory treatment.[@2]

The campaign brought that legal system into contact with many more people, with repeated extensions carrying enforcement beyond its original six-month period.[@1]

## People faced two detention systems

Amnesty International's investigation described two routes: prosecution through the criminal justice system and administrative detention in drug-related centres.[@1] Interviewees reported arrests based on association or location, pressure to pay for release, and ill-treatment during detention.[@1]

The organisation found that poor and marginalised people were especially exposed, with money affecting their ability to avoid or challenge detention.[@1] It also documented allegations of abuse in facilities presented as places of recovery.[@1]

The arrest total concerned the whole drug campaign.[@1] The report placed its findings within a regional synthetic-drug market, so these figures describe broad drug enforcement rather than a count of cannabis arrests.[@1]

## Treatment promises met conditions in custody

The drug law obliged the state to support treatment, rehabilitation and harm reduction, with public treatment costs borne by the state.[@2] Voluntary treatment required informed consent, and the law gave people the right to stop it.[@2]

Amnesty's interviews instead described detention without proper safeguards, inadequate healthcare and punishment presented as treatment.[@1] Those accounts challenged the gap between the statutory promises and people's experiences in custody.[@1]

## Investigators called for a different response

In its [May 2020 report](/history/cambodia-drug-campaign-abuses-report-2020/), Amnesty urged an end to compulsory drug detention and punishment for personal use, possession and cultivation.[@1] It called for voluntary, evidence-based care and health services.[@1]

For [Cambodia](/country/kh/), the investigation turned the campaign's arrest totals into questions about who was detained, how they were treated and whether services helped them recover.[@1]
