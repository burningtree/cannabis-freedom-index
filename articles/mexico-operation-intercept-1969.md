On 21 September 1969 the United States began Operation Intercept at its border with Mexico. Intensive searches were intended to interrupt drug traffic, but the immediate effects also reached ordinary travellers and relations between the two governments.

Carmen Boullosa and Mike Wallace place the operation in the rapid expansion of cannabis consumption and the Nixon administration’s pressure on Mexico over cultivation and trafficking. Contemporary diplomatic records show how quickly the border campaign became a foreign-policy problem.

## Searches provoke a protest

A memorandum sent by National Security Council staff member Viron Vaky on 30 September recorded the start date and the growing reaction. It noted that the Mexican consul general in El Paso had been searched and that tourist traffic had fallen sharply.

Mexico’s foreign minister complained to Secretary of State William Rogers on 26 September about the lack of consultation. The Mexican ambassador lodged a further protest on 29 September. President Gustavo Díaz Ordaz also publicly criticised the operation during a luncheon for visiting astronauts. This was a dispute between governments as well as an enforcement operation.

## From Intercept to Cooperation

An October 20 memorandum from Acting Secretary of State Elliot Richardson and Attorney General John Mitchell described the replacement arrangement, Operation Cooperation. The United States would modify procedures to facilitate travel; Mexico would intensify enforcement; and further talks would discuss cooperation.

The same memorandum proposed assistance against both opium and cannabis cultivation. Its scope therefore extended beyond marijuana, and it should not be read as a new Mexican cannabis prohibition.

## The diplomatic aftermath

Ambassador Robert McBride’s year-end letter to Rogers described the damage to bilateral relations. He reported that talks held in Mexico City on 27–30 October established a joint working group on narcotics, marijuana and dangerous drugs. The group worked through November and much of December on recommendations for further cooperation.

McBride believed relations were improving but expected resentment over Intercept to persist. His account documents diplomatic repair and plans for enforcement. It does not establish that border searches permanently reduced the supply of cannabis or that every later eradication measure followed directly from this one operation.
