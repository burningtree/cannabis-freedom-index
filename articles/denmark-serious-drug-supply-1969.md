In the summer of 1969 Denmark built the two-track drug law it still uses. Law No. 276 of 18 June,
in force on 1 July, inserted section 191 into the Penal Code: up to six years' imprisonment for
unlawfully transferring drugs to many people, for substantial payment, or in otherwise
particularly aggravating circumstances — with possession punishable only where intent to transfer
on those terms could be shown.[@1]

## Serious commercial activity only

The parliamentary committee that cleared the bill drew a line the Danish courts have honoured
ever since. Its guidance reserved section 191's cannabis application for serious commercial
activity: this was not a six-year maximum for every personal-possession case.[@2] The same
committee went further, recommending more research into cannabis and the effects of legal controls
on users, and an inquiry into whether personal-use conduct should be removed from the drug law's
offences altogether.[@2] Those were recommendations, not law — but they set the terms of the next
decades' argument.[@2]

## Aimed at suppliers, not users

The bargain was explicit. Section 191 was approved on condition that enforcement would
distinguish cannabis from other drugs and users from sellers: born of 1960s political and public
anxiety about young people's hash smoking, it targeted suppliers, not the smokers
themselves.[@3] A fortnight after the law took effect, the chief public prosecutor's circular
made the other half of the settlement concrete — possession of a small amount of cannabis for
personal use should be met with a warning, and warnings stayed the standard response until
2004.[@3]

The review clauses the 1969 package carried were repealed in 1971,[@4] but the structure survived:
ordinary cases under the Euphoriants Act, serious ones under section 191 — the same two-track
law that still anchors [Denmark's](/country/dk/) supply regime today.[@3]
