Spain's 1983 penal-code reform made a long line of court decisions explicit: merely possessing a drug for one's own consumption was not a crime. Criminal liability centred on trafficking and conduct intended to promote or facilitate illegal consumption by others.

## The courts got there first

Spanish Supreme Court decisions had excluded personal possession from the trafficking offence well before Parliament acted. A 1984 circular from the public prosecutor traces the approach to decisions from the 1960s and says dozens of judgments after 1971 consistently treated possession for later personal use, without trafficking, as outside the offence.

In practice, therefore, the 1983 legislation confirmed an established judicial rule. Its importance was to put that distinction directly into the wording and structure of Article 344.

## Possession and intent

Organic Law 8/1983 rewrote Article 344. Possession intended for trafficking was punishable; possession whose purpose was the holder's own consumption was not. Where no sale had occurred, courts could infer intent from the quantity, packaging, location and equipment found with the drugs.

The reform also distinguished substances by the seriousness of the harm attributed to them. The prosecutor's circular records that the Supreme Court consistently placed cannabis derivatives in the category not causing grave harm, while still regarding trafficking in them as an offence.

## Not a legal market

The reform did not authorise commercial sales. Supplying another person, whether for payment or free, could amount to promoting or facilitating consumption. Nor did the absence of a possession crime create licensed shops or a regulated production chain.

Public-space penalties came later. The [1992 public-security law](/history/spain-corcuera-law-1992/) made public possession and consumption administrative offences, preserving the distinction between private personal conduct and conduct exposed to the public.

## What moved in the index

The reform raised [Spain's](/country/es/) possession score from 1 to 4, cultivation from 1 to 3, enforcement from 2 to 5, sharing from 1 to 2 and consumption from 1 to 4. Access remained 0. Those gains describe non-criminal private conduct and the personal-use doctrine; the low sharing and access scores preserve the continuing prohibition on an ordinary legal supply market.

It was decriminalisation, not commercial legalisation.
