Jersey gave Centeniers a power to fine people for possessing Class B or C drugs at a Parish Hall enquiry when the person accepts that decision.[@1,2] The change took effect on 18 October 2024 and added an option between a written caution and sending a case to court.[@1,3]

## Parish Hall already offered cautions

The [earlier caution system](/history/jersey-caution-guidelines-2015/) let eligible small personal cases be dealt with through a written caution.[@3] Current Attorney General directions cover up to fifteen grams of herbal cannabis or resin and exclude evidence of dealing or onward distribution.[@3] A second caution can be considered where at least a year has passed and the Centenier considers the circumstances justify it.[@3]

The directions require individual assessment and retain discretion; the quantity ceiling does not promise the same outcome in every case.[@3] Cannabis oil is treated separately, with zero tolerance in the table, while oil in confectionery is referred to the Attorney General case by case.[@3]

## An accepted fine can keep the case out of court

The new article allows a level-one fine where the person accepts the Centenier's decision.[@2] For an eligible third Class B offence, the current directions specify £200, admission of the offence and no evidence of dealing or onward distribution.[@3] The directions also address personal importation and permit discretion for short-stay visitors where attendance at the Alcohol and Drugs Service would be impractical.[@3]

A Centenier must issue a receipt and tell the Chief Officer of Police about the fine.[@2] The statutory power therefore supplies a recorded local disposal for an offence that remains within the drug law.[@2]

## Police records still retain the outcome

The government's [2025 reform proposal](/history/jersey-reform-debate-deferred-2026/) explains that Parish Hall cautions and fines do not appear on the person's criminal record.[@4] It also says they remain in police records indefinitely because the rehabilitation law does not address those disposals.[@4] A record held by police and a criminal-record entry therefore have different consequences.[@4]

Court prosecution remains possible, with ordinary Class B possession carrying up to five years or a fine, or both.[@2] The [Jersey profile](/country/je/) records that criminal ceiling alongside the guidance and Parish Hall outcomes that often shape a small personal case.[@2,3]
