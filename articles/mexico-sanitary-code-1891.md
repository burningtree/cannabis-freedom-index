In 1891 Porfirian Mexico put cannabis in the pharmacy. The first of three national sanitary codes — followed by further codes in 1894 and 1902 — recognised the medical use of herbs and narcotics but reserved them to the country's new modern medical elite: pharmacies could keep hemp seeds and various cannabis products in stock, and marijuana went on the list of plants that gatherers could sell only to pharmacists.[@1]

## Doses by the gram

The codes were precise. Pharmacies could dispense two grams of "haschich" or "tincture of haschich", half a gram of cannabis alcohol extract, and a third of a gram of "cannabinone" — but only with a prescription signed by a licensed physician.[@1] Despite the moral prejudice against indigenous psychoactive plants, the useful colonial crop had thus become a controlled medicinal substance: recognised as a source of useful medicines, yet available only through doctors and pharmacists.[@1]

## The alliance behind it

The codes sealed an alliance between the state and the medical profession, each vouching for the other's competence and authority in the Porfirian drive for order and progress.[@1] Mexico had regulated before at local level — in 1871 Guanajuato had already prohibited selling marihuana except in pharmacies on a physician's prescription, citing the "serious evils" of the grass — but the sanitary codes made the pharmacy monopoly national.[@1]

## From medicine to vice

The monopoly did not hold. In 1904 Zacatecas banned selling marihuana except as a prescribed medicine, Nuevo León tied gatherers to permitted pharmacies in 1905, and Mexico City moved to treat the plant as one to be banned in 1909.[@1] Then the 1920 decree dropped marijuana from the regulated medicines altogether while keeping opium, morphine, codeine and heroin under control, fining unpermitted sales and possession with fifteen days' jail for those who could not pay.[@1] The 1926 code finished the reversal, listing "marihuana in any of its forms" among the "enervating drugs" and confining users in the federal drug-addicts hospital, a pavilion inside Mexico City's La Castañeda asylum — and the [1920 ban](/history/mexico-ban-1920/) sealed what the 1891 code had begun by bringing under control.[@1] The [country page](/country/mx/) follows the arc from pharmacy shelves to prohibition.
