Ethiopia put cannabis imports and sales behind permits, pharmacy licences
and medical prescriptions in September 1942. Someone breaking those rules
could face up to two years in prison, a fine of up to 2,000 Ethiopian
dollars, or both.

## Importers needed approval from two offices

The short Dangerous Drugs Proclamation named cannabis indica alongside
opium, coca, datura and heroin. Its import restriction also covered the
listed plants' alkaloids and derivatives, extending the control beyond
the raw plants themselves.

An importer needed a permit issued by the Minister of Commerce and
Industry. That decision depended on advice from the Director of Medical
Services. The arrangement therefore put both commercial administration
and medical oversight between an importer and a lawful shipment.

## A shop licence alone did not authorise a sale

The law limited offers for sale to a pharmacy or drug store licensed
under Ethiopian law. An ordinary trader could not offer the named drugs
through a general shop.

Even an authorised pharmacy needed a prescription before selling them.
The prescription had to come from a medical practitioner who was both
registered and licensed. The importer, the premises and the practitioner
thus each had a separate role in the legal route to supply.

For cannabis, this tied a lawful retail transaction to a medical order.
The drug's express inclusion matters: the restriction can be read directly
from the list, without having to infer whether a general reference to
narcotics covered the plant.

## The same penalty covered each breach

The proclamation attached one penalty to contraventions of its rules.
A court could impose imprisonment, a fine, or both together. The maximum
prison term was two years, and the maximum fine was 2,000 dollars.

That meant the import permit, the licensed selling place and the
prescription were backed by criminal punishment. Each was a condition
that the people bringing in or selling the drug had to satisfy.

## Later laws reached further along the supply chain

Ethiopia's [replacement penal code](/history/ethiopia-penal-code-1957/)
took effect in May 1958. It covered unauthorised production, transport,
acquisition, storage and supply of narcotic substances, and knowingly
providing premises for drug use.

The [1999 drug law](/history/ethiopia-drug-control-law-1999/) returned to
special licences, controlled prescriptions and locked storage. Those later
rules continued the close supervision of importers, prescribers and
dispensers already visible in [Ethiopia's history](/country/et/) in 1942.
