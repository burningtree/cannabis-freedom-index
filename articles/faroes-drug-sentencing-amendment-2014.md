People selling drugs at concerts, festivals, clubs and other youth events in the Faroe Islands faced a tougher sentencing rule from December 2014.[@1,3] Courts had to treat those settings as a significant aggravating factor, including when drugs were handed over free for somebody else to sell.[@1]

## Police and customs wanted gaps closed

The Faroese health ministry explained that Danish drug law had changed while the rules applied in the islands had fallen behind.[@2] Police and customs had asked for an update because some newer intoxicating substances were outside the existing controls, preventing prosecution when those substances were seized.[@2]

Drug legislation remained a Danish responsibility, so bringing the changes into Faroese law required a separate royal order.[@2] The earlier legal scheme rested on an [order issued in 1956](/history/faroes-euphoriant-substances-order-1956/), with amendments in 1969 and 1988.[@3]

## Selling at youth events counted more heavily

The amendment singled out sales in restaurants and nightclubs, at concerts and music festivals, and at other gatherings attended mainly by children or young people.[@1] It also covered an unpaid handover intended to lead to a later sale.[@1]

Courts were instructed to consider the harmfulness of the substance when setting sentences and fines.[@1] A separate aggravating rule concerned repeated sales of particularly dangerous or harmful drugs.[@1]

These instructions operated within a law covering different controlled substances.[@1] The consolidated Faroese order provides a basic maximum of two years in prison or a fine for breaches of its rules.[@3]

## Officials could control groups of substances

The update also allowed the authorities to regulate groups of drugs, extending a power previously expressed in terms of individual substances.[@1] The health ministry linked that change to the appearance of new drugs and the difficulty of keeping the islands' controls aligned with Denmark's.[@2]

The order was published locally on 19 December 2014 and took effect the next day.[@3] Its changes therefore reached the Faroese system through the islands' own publication process.[@1,3]

## Medical access became a separate debate

A [cannabis campaigning party](/history/faroes-cannabis-party-formed-2018/) emerged four years later, and a [medical trial scheme](/history/faroes-medical-cannabis-trial-2019/) opened in April 2019.[@5,6]

In the [Faroe Islands](/country/fo/), police still said in their 2025 report that even small personal-use quantities were prosecuted when discovered, while their strategic attention centred on organisers and larger middlemen.[@4]
