Algeria's replacement drug law of December 2004 repealed the 1985 drug provisions, including their capital clause.[@1,2] It retained severe supply penalties and imposed life imprisonment for unlawful cannabis cultivation, while allowing treatment-linked non-prosecution for qualifying users.[@1,2]

## Personal use could mean prison or a fine

Illicit consumption or possession for personal consumption carried two months to two years in prison and a fine of 5,000–50,000 dinars, or either penalty alone.[@1,2] Compared with the previous health law, the maximum use sentence rose from one year to two years.[@1,2] The offence covered narcotics and psychotropic substances generally.[@1,2]

Giving or offering drugs to another person for that person's consumption had its own penalty of two to ten years.[@1] Wider production and supply offences carried ten to twenty years, with life imprisonment for specified organised activity.[@1] The law treated illicit cannabis cultivation as a separate offence carrying life.[@1]

## The earlier capital clause is repealed

The [1985 health law](/history/algeria-health-law-drug-framework-1985/) had allowed capital punishment for specified trafficking and facilitation offences affecting the Algerian people's moral health.[@1,2] The replacement law expressly repealed the articles containing those drug offences and the capital clause.[@1,2] Its supply and cultivation provisions instead used prison terms extending to life.[@1,2]

## Treatment remains a route away from prosecution

People who completed prescribed detoxification, or met the law's detoxification or medical-supervision conditions, could avoid prosecution.[@1] Courts could also order treatment when a charged person needed it and could withhold the ordinary use penalty under the treatment provisions.[@1] Avoiding an ordered course could bring the use penalties back into play.[@1]

Medical and scientific activities had a separate route: the listed controlled-drug operations required authorisation from the health minister.[@1] The law therefore distinguished authorised work from the illicit use, growing and supply offences.[@1]

## Later rules change the practical choices

The [2007 treatment decree](/history/algeria-treatment-diversion-procedure-2007/) specified assessment, doctors' reports and completion certificates for non-prosecution decisions.[@3] It also required confiscation of seized substances and plants.[@3]

In [2023](/history/algeria-law-23-05-2023/), lawmakers added a lower cultivation penalty when the crop was for personal consumption: two to five years and a fine.[@4] That amendment changed the blanket life-sentence rule for cultivation, while retaining life for the general offence.[@4] These later distinctions became part of the rules facing people who grow or use cannabis in [Algeria](/country/dz/).[@4]
