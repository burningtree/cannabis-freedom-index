Kenya's High Court refused to exempt Rastafari religious cannabis use from the country's drug prohibitions on 15 July 2026. The Rastafari Society of Kenya and two fellow petitioners had sought protection for private worship and designated places of worship. Possessing, using and growing the sacrament remained subject to the existing law.[@1]

## Worship under prohibition

The petitioners described cannabis as a sacred herb used in meditation and communal reasoning, the religious discussions through which participants seek understanding and connection with Jah. Their witnesses described smoking, incense and other forms of use in private settings and tabernacles, with adults-only community controls. They argued that prohibition forced believers to choose between religious observance and obeying the law.[@1]

Their case sought a defined religious exemption. They relied on constitutional protections for religion, privacy, dignity, equality and association, and pointed to South African decisions protecting private adult use. The Attorney General and the national drug agency opposed the claim, arguing that an exemption would complicate enforcement and create opportunities for diversion.[@1]

## The court wanted the licensing route tried first

Justice Bahati Mwamuye said the petitioners had not exhausted the legal and administrative remedies available under the drug law. They had not first sought a licence or exemption, or shown that applying would be futile. He considered the constitutional petition premature on that ground.[@1]

The judgment also examined the substance of their rights arguments. It recognised Rastafari as a religion but found the evidence inconsistent on whether cannabis was indispensable to its practice. The judge held that the petitioners had not established the claimed violations and accepted public-health, safety and enforcement justifications for the restrictions. These are the court's conclusions about the evidence and law in this case; the [Rastafari sacrament](/culture/traditions/rastafari-sacrament/) has its own meanings for practitioners.[@1]

## An invitation to debate

In his closing observations, Mwamuye described cannabis use as widespread and questioned the allocation of scarce enforcement resources to small quantities. He called for a frank national conversation about policy, while leaving changes to the institutions responsible for making it.[@1]

The [ruling's history entry](/history/kenya-high-court-rastafari-petition-2026/) therefore records an unsuccessful challenge and a judicial contribution to that debate. For Rastafari worshippers, the immediate outcome was continued exposure to the prohibitions described in [Kenya's profile](/country/ke/).[@1]
