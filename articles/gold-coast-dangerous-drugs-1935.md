The Gold Coast's 1935 Dangerous Drugs Ordinance belongs to the history of
cannabis prohibition in what became Ghana. It also belongs to a broader story
of colonial drug regulation, international agreements and replacement legislation.
Describing it simply as a first cannabis ban leaves those distinctions unresolved.

The colonial government's annual report identifies the measure as Ordinance
No. 29 of 1935. That contemporary account provides a firmer legislative identity
than the later shorthand Dangerous Drugs Act.

## An international framework

The report says the ordinance implemented the 1931 international convention
supplementing the Second Opium Convention. Its explanation focuses on extending
controls to classes of opium derivatives and the manufacture of coca-leaf
derivatives.

This places the measure within a system covering several drugs. It does not
support interpreting the legislation as a response solely to cannabis, or as
proof of a particular level of local cannabis use.

## Replacement and territorial reach

The same account states that the ordinance repealed existing laws in this area
and applied to the whole Gold Coast. Both points matter: replacement legislation
presupposes an earlier legal framework, while wider territorial application can
change the reach of controls without inventing every offence afresh.

The report discusses the measure within a period of changes to courts and the
territorial organisation of legislation. Its description therefore gives the
1935 event an administrative dimension alongside its drug-control purpose.

An exact commencement day is not established by this account. Nor does its
short summary reproduce the cannabis provisions of the ordinance itself.

## Cannabis in the later historical account

Richard Quansah Amissah's 2022 review associates cannabis-possession prohibition
with the 1935 legislation, citing earlier scholarship. It describes a framework
regulating trade, production and use of opium and other dangerous drugs.

That account supports including cannabis in the event's history. It should still
be distinguished from inspection of the original statutory text, especially when
making claims about precise exemptions, penalties or the first restriction.

## A legal history that continued after independence

The Supreme Court later compared the colonial ordinance with the
[Pharmacy and Drugs Act of 1961](/history/ghana-pharmacy-drugs-act-1961/).
Its [Hassan judgment](/history/ghana-hassan-possession-acquittal-1962/)
shows why wording and replacement dates could determine a possession case.

Those later records belong in their own timeline entries. Together with the
annual report, they give [Ghana's history](/country/gh/) a more precise sequence
than an uninterrupted, undifferentiated ban beginning in 1935.
