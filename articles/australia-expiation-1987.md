On 30 April 1987 South Australia began dealing with simple cannabis offences by
expiation notice rather than by criminal charge. An adult caught with up to 100
grams of cannabis, 20 grams of resin or 10 plants could pay a fine of $50 to $150
within 60 days and avoid a conviction.[@1]

## The first Australian scheme

The policy came out of the 1979 South Australian Royal Commission into the
Non-Medical Use of Drugs, which recommended that minor use should not be a
criminal offence. South Australia was the first Australian jurisdiction to put
that recommendation into practice. The scheme kept the conduct unlawful — the
notice was an alternative to prosecution, not a legal right to possess — but it
took ordinary users out of the courts.[@1]

## What followed

The Australian Capital Territory copied the model in 1992 and the Northern
Territory in 1996. Other states chose cautions, diversion or infringement
notices rather than a full expiation scheme. By the 2000s every jurisdiction had
some summary route for small amounts, but the details and the amounts varied
widely.[@1]

South Australia's current scheme is narrower than the 1987 version. It covers up
to 100 grams of cannabis and one non-hydroponic plant, with fees of $250 below 25
grams and $400 from 25 to 100 grams.[@2] Paying the fee expiates the offence; a
person who does not pay can still be prosecuted.

## Why it matters

The 1987 reform is the point where an Australian state first treated small
possession as a regulatory matter rather than a criminal one. It set the pattern
that the ACT later extended and that the other states modified, and it remains
the reference point for the country's patchwork of cautions and fines.[@1]

The date is given as 30 April 1987 because that is the commencement day recorded
for the scheme, but the UNODC report says only that it was operational in 1987.
South Australia's primary source for the exact day has not been located.[@1]
