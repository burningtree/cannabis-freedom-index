On 1 January 2006 Serbia got a Criminal Code that punished drug users less and rewarded those who talked. Adopted on 29 September 2005, the Code moved drug offences into Article 246 with more lenient penalties than the 2003 scheme — and added two ways out: remission for holding narcotics for personal use, and remission for naming the supplier.[@3,1]

## What changed

The offence forms stayed, but the penalties softened. The 2003 scheme had been harsh: at least five years for the basic production offence, at least seven for the aggravated form, and six months to five years for unauthorised possession.[@1] The 2006 Code kept the possession offence in the same words — holding a smaller quantity of a narcotic for personal use — while adding the remission clauses.[@1] A holder for personal use, or an offender in any of the first three paragraphs who disclosed where they got the drugs, could be remitted from punishment.[@1,2]

## Why it mattered

The reform expressed a general turn toward weakening criminal-law repression of users.[@1] Courts could now remit punishment for a personal-use holder, and prosecutors later gained the separate tool of deferring small-possession cases — a structure that still defines [Serbia's](/country/rs/) cannabis law: a fine or up to three years for small amounts, with waiver or deferral, and three to ten years for large stocks even when held for personal use.

The settlement did not last untouched. A 2009 amendment deleted the summary possession form and created a separate small-possession offence, which first produced wandering court practice and wrong decisions before the Supreme Court of Cassation intervened.[@1] In 2019 the screws tightened again: growing rose to two to eight years, and large possession to three to ten.[@4] But the 2006 remission logic — the user who holds for themselves, and the offender who names their source — survived every rewrite.
