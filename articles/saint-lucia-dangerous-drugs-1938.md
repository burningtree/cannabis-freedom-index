Saint Lucia banned cannabis in 1938. Ordinance No. 20 of that year, published in the 1957 revised ordinances as Chapter 55 and in force from 1 May 1939, gave the colony a full dangerous-drugs code — and its Part IV is headed simply "Indian Hemp".[@1]

## A botanical definition

The ordinance defines its terms with unusual care. "Indian Hemp" means and includes the plant known as Cannabis Sativa L., growing or otherwise, and the dried fruiting or flowering tops of that plant from which the resin has not been extracted, by whatever name those tops are called — plus the resins obtained from Cannabis Sativa and every preparation of which those resins form the base.[@1] The definition sits among the standard Geneva-convention machinery: corresponding laws, import certificates, export authorisations, diversion certificates, and the Hague, Geneva No. 1 (1925) and Geneva No. 2 (1931) conventions named in full.[@1]

## Section 10: the outright ban

Part IV does the work in two strokes. Section 9 bars importing or exporting Indian hemp, and section 10 goes further: anyone who imports, exports, cultivates, produces, sells, has in possession, or otherwise deals with any Indian hemp is guilty of an offence against the ordinance.[@1] The only proviso is transit — hemp merely passing through the colony under the Part VI transit terms commits no offence.[@1] Cocaine, morphine and the rest wait for Part V; hemp gets a part of its own, and the stricter treatment.[@1]

## Ten years on indictment

Conviction carried weight: every offence against the ordinance drew, on indictment, a fine of up to nine hundred and sixty dollars or up to ten years' imprisonment with or without hard labour, or both — six months or two hundred and forty dollars on summary conviction — with forfeiture of everything involved and court-ordered destruction.[@1] The [country page](/country/lc/) follows the law from this ordinance to decriminalisation.
