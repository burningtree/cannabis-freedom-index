On 22 October 1938 the front page of a Mexico City newspaper announced that the
doctors of the Academy of Medicine had smoked marijuana. They had, without knowing it.
The man who gave it to them was one of the country's leading psychiatrists.

## The belief

In Mexico in the 1930s marijuana was "the weed of madness". Three puffs were said to be
enough to unhinge the strongest mind. Families believed it, patients believed it and
doctors believed it, and the law had treated users as degenerates since
[1920](/history/mexico-ban-1920/).

## The psychiatrist

Leopoldo Salazar Viniegra had more than a decade of clinical experience at the national
psychiatric hospital. In all of it, he told the Academy, he had not seen one case of
psychosis that could definitely be blamed on marijuana. Wherever a family or a doctor
had made that diagnosis, careful observation found something else: alcohol, syphilis, an
illness already there.

A historian who went back to the hospital's files found the same. Very few records
mention marijuana at all, and those that do usually record heavy drinking alongside it.

## The experiments

With a medical student, Jorge Segura Millán, Salazar spent a year testing the idea.
They burned cannabis in boxes containing dogs. And they rolled cigarettes that looked
like the commercial kind, half tobacco and half marijuana, and gave them to people who
did not know: novices, psychiatric patients, and, at one meeting of the neurological
society, the doctors sitting nearby.

The subjects showed red eyes and dry mouths. Many noticed nothing else. Salazar's
explanation was that the physical effects resemble those of strong emotion, which makes
a smoker highly open to suggestion. People who expected madness could produce something
like it.

By today's standards the method was indefensible. A nephew of his once found a pack of
the cigarettes and shared them with children as young as nine.

## The scandal

Salazar ended his thirty-four-page paper with a cry of "Poor slandered and lovely
marijuana bush!" and handed round his cigarettes. The press named it one of the year's
great scandals, and suggested he had lost his own mind.

His marijuana work never became policy. His other proposal, state-supplied morphine for
addicts, became law in [1940](/history/mexico-brief-legalisation-1940/) and was
[suspended](/history/mexico-regulation-suspended-1940/) within months.
