In February 2012, barely a month after taking office, Guatemala's president Otto Pérez Molina
did what no sitting head of state had done: he said the war on drugs had failed and opened the
debate on decriminalising drugs.[@1][@2] A former general who had campaigned on an iron fist,
he argued that the policies for controlling the drug market had collapsed and that violence
tied to trafficking groups was Central America's greatest problem.[@1]

## Partial or complete

Pérez Molina put the whole chain on the table. He told the Washington Post the proposal could
mean partial decriminalisation or complete decriminalisation covering production, transit and
consumption alike.[@2] His government paper, "For a comprehensive policy against the drug
problem in the Americas", carried the argument to the region, and as host of the OAS Assembly
he forced leaders who preferred silence to answer.[@3]

## Applause abroad, silence at home

Colombia's Santos had moved first that January, and Pérez Molina's echo turned one plea into
a regional debate: by 2013 the hemisphere was discussing health models instead of only
interdiction.[@3] But the presidents of El Salvador, Nicaragua and
Panama refused decriminalisation, and inside Guatemala Congress never wrote a single reform
bill.[@1] Pérez Molina himself conditioned success on an international agreement first — which
meant, in practice, waiting.[@1]

## The commission that outlived the idea

In October 2013 the government announced a National Commission for Drug Policy Reform, made of
independent experts; Pérez Molina inaugurated it in January 2014 to review the failed rules and
recommend changes.[@1] It studied cannabis and even poppy legalisation for medicines, then left
no law behind.[@1]
