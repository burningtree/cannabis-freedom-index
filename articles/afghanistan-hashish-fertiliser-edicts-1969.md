According to historian James Bradford, Afghanistan's king issued an edict in 1969
encouraging farmers to use fertiliser to increase hashish production. A further
order followed in 1970.

## An old export

Hashish and opium were established Afghan exports by the 1920s. The
[penal code of 1924–25](/history/afghanistan-penal-code-1924/) forbade producing and
using them at home, with lashes for smokers, while cannabis grown for export was exempt
and taxed. The state disapproved of the habit and lived off the trade.

## The travellers

In the 1960s a new kind of buyer arrived. Western travellers on the overland route to
India found Afghan hashish cheap and plentiful, and some started carrying it home. In
1971 alone almost sixty thousand tourists came to Kabul, many of them for the hash.

They widened the markets available to Afghan producers. The older regional trade
included opium exports to Iran; buyers from Europe and America now created further
demand for hashish, increasingly in large quantities.

## The edicts

By the end of the decade demand was large enough that King Zahir Shah issued an official
edict, in 1969 and again in 1970, encouraging farmers to use fertilisers to increase
hashish production.

Plantations spread around Herat, Kandahar and Mazar-e-Sharif, and cannabis lined the
main roads out of Kabul. For rural families it was more than extra income: the trade
gave many farmers their first access to tractors and other machinery.

## The professionals

Organised smugglers followed the tourists. One American group bought so much between
1970 and 1973 that farmers had to change their methods to supply it, and asked for hash
oil, which was stronger and easier to hide. An American narcotics agent estimated their
return at 80,000 dollars for every 50 kilos sold in New York or Montreal.

## The reversal

The United States posted a narcotics agent to Kabul in late 1970. The
[1972 enforcement drive](/history/afghanistan-smuggling-crackdown-1972/) included new
anti-smuggling units, while a late-year government promised stronger measures.
Reported smuggler arrests rose from 72 in 1971 to 584 in 1973. The
[1973 cultivation ban](/history/afghanistan-ban-1973/) marked a further restriction.

conflict between income from the trade and international pressure to suppress it.
