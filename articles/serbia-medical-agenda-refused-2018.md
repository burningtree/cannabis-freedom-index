Serbia’s parliament declined to put Goran Čabradi’s medical-cannabis bill on its agenda on 26 March 2018.[@1] The Green Party MP argued that seriously ill people were buying cannabis oil on an unregulated market and risking prosecution.[@1] His motion received ten votes in favour, while 149 MPs did not vote.[@1]

## Patients were at the centre of the argument

Čabradi had [submitted the bill in June 2017](/history/serbia-medical-bill-2017/).[@1] Introducing his agenda motion, he described people with serious illnesses who believed cannabis helped them and wanted the state to stop treating their access to oil as a criminal matter.[@1]

He cited an estimate of 30,000–50,000 affected citizens and argued that a legal medical route would reduce their reliance on the grey market.[@1] Those numbers were part of his parliamentary case for reform, rather than a patient registry or a finding established by the vote.[@1] He asked MPs to let the proposal reach debate so that the legislature could address those patients’ situation.[@1]

## The ministry had taken a narrower approach

A separate government amendment process had included a [public consultation beginning in December 2016](/history/serbia-cannabis-consultation-2016/), with meetings in Belgrade, Novi Sad and Kragujevac.[@2] The later consultation report said many comments concerned industrial hemp and medical cannabis.[@2]

Officials accepted objections to lowering the existing hemp THC ceiling because growers already used varieties permitted by the law.[@2] They rejected proposals to legalise medical cannabis oil, citing their expert assessment of clinical evidence and the existence of registered cannabinoid medicines.[@2] The report therefore recorded different outcomes for agricultural hemp and the oil sought by patient campaigners.[@2]

## The agenda vote left the bill without a hearing

The parliamentary record shows no votes against the scheduling motion and no abstentions, but only ten votes in favour were insufficient to add the item.[@1] The chair announced that the Assembly had not accepted the proposal.[@1] Čabradi’s medical-access arguments consequently did not lead to consideration of his bill during that sitting.[@1]

The controlled-substances framework retained restricted routes for research and exceptional therapeutic use, alongside specific authorisations for controlled medicines.[@3] Research possession and clinical trials each required ministerial consent, while high-THC cannabis cultivation remained prohibited.[@3] The [Serbia profile](/country/rs/) describes those controls together with the criminal penalties affecting people who possess or grow cannabis outside the authorised routes.[@3]
