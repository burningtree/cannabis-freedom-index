In 1984 Nigeria's second military government answered drugs with the firing squad. The
rulers issued Decree 20, which made the death penalty available for drug trafficking — and
applied it retroactively. Three convicted traffickers, Bartholomew Owoh, Lawal Akanni
Ojuolape and Bernard Ogedengbe, were publicly executed on 10 April 1985 for offences
committed before the decree existed, drawing condemnation at home and abroad.[@1,4]

The decree was aimed at hard drugs, not cannabis: until 1984 little was known in Nigeria
about trafficking in anything except locally produced cannabis, Indian hemp.[@1] But the
same year's amendments to the Indian Hemp Decree raised cannabis penalties too — the 1984
amendment set imprisonment of at least 21 years for planting or cultivating, and at least
four years without a fine option for smoking or knowingly possessing.[@3]

The death penalty did not hold. It was used sparingly, aimed at cocaine, and removed the
next year; in July 1986 the regime abolished the death sentence under Decree 20 for drug
trafficking and illegal ship bunkering, commuting the machinery of the decree into long
prison terms.[@2] In 1989 Decree 48 created the NDLEA, the drug agency that enforces the
long sentences to this day.[@4]

Cannabis kept spreading regardless. Use became popular among young Nigerians between 1980
and 1990, the very years of the harshest laws, and Nigeria grew into one of the largest
cannabis-using populations in the world — a standing rebuttal, Nigerian papers still argue,
to the idea that the harshest penalty deters the trade.[@4]
