On 27 October 1934 Moscow banned the sowing of opium poppy and Indian hemp across the entire Soviet Union. The decree of Kalinin and Molotov allowed only crops whose harvest went exclusively to the medical and scientific needs of the USSR: each year the agriculture commissariat set the sowing area by district, republic land organs issued the permits, and unlicensed sowing meant criminal prosecution with mandatory confiscation of the crop.[@1] The union republics were ordered to write matching offences into their own criminal codes, and the ban took effect from 1935.[@1]

## Hemp as a state crop

In Kyrgyzstan the ban coexisted for decades with official cultivation: through the 1950s and 1960s the republic grew hemp and poppy at state level.[@2] Every autumn, when the hemp harvest began, crowds arrived from other parts of the USSR — checks showed many were addicts and speculators, and the republic's party chief later recalled 310 kilograms of hashish seized from them in just three years.[@2] Workers at the Frunze tobacco factory conspired with the speculators to lace cigarettes with hemp narcotics and sell them at inflated prices, hashish smoking spread among young people including senior schoolchildren, and crimes committed under hashish intoxication rose year after year.[@2]

## Ending the Chuy crop

From 1962 the Kyrgyz party leadership pressed Moscow to stop growing southern hemp in the region; the question stalled until the chemical industry learned to make full substitutes for hemp-and-jute goods.[@2] Once the substitute technology arrived in early 1964, the USSR Council of Ministers acted: its 3 June 1964 decision banned the sowing of southern Chuy hemp anywhere in the Kyrgyz SSR from 1965.[@2] The same decision ordered non-narcotic hemp varieties, banned hemp on household plots nationwide, and set the republics to liquidating wild hemp stands — while the Chuy experimental station was converted from bast crops to forage and the hemp seed station abolished.[@2]

## From the decree to today

The 1934 decree's machinery — quotas, permits, criminal liability — governed Central Asian cannabis for the rest of the Soviet era, with the [1965 Chuy ban](/history/kyrgyzstan-chuy-hemp-ban-1965/) as its local climax; the same union-wide ban applied in [Uzbekistan](/history/uzbekistan-soviet-ban-1934/) and the other republics.[@1,2] [Kyrgyzstan](/country/kg/) inherited both the fields and the prohibitions.
