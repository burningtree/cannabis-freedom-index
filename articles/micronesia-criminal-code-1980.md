The territorial code published in 1980 gave small marijuana possession a
fine-only penalty: no more than $50 for an ounce or less. Giving someone that
amount without payment received the same treatment. Larger holdings could bring
prison, and selling marijuana had a separate, heavier penalty.[@1]

## A territorial law with a longer history

The code applied across the Mariana Islands other than Guam, the Marshall
Islands and the Caroline Islands.[@2] Its controlled-substances chapter credits
the Congress of Micronesia's Public Law 5-110, the scheduled drug law covered in
the [1974 entry](/history/micronesia-controlled-substances-act-1974/).[@1]

That chapter differed from the older law used against
[Mariano Bermudes](/history/cnmi-bermudes-marijuana-charges-1974/). In July 1974,
the territorial High Court had dismissed his possession and sale charges after
finding that health officials had been given insufficiently guided power to
decide which drugs were prohibited.[@3]

## The amount divided three possession bands

The published code prohibited knowing or intentional possession outside its
authorised routes. Marijuana had three quantity bands:[@1]

| Amount | Maximum possession penalty |
|---|---|
| One ounce or less | $50 fine |
| More than one ounce, less than 2.2 pounds | Three months, $500, or both |
| 2.2 pounds or more | One year, $1,000, or both |

At 2.2 pounds or more, the law also presumed trafficking unless the defendant
rebutted that presumption. Ordinary marijuana manufacture or delivery could bring
five years, a $5,000 fine, or both. The unpaid-gift exception covered no more than
one ounce.[@1]

## Probation could end without a conviction

An eligible first-time possession defendant could consent to probation while the
court deferred further proceedings without entering a judgment of guilt.
Completing the conditions required dismissal; breaking them could bring a
judgment and ordinary sentencing. This route could be used only once.[@1]

After dismissal, the person could apply to clear the public official records of
the arrest and case. The court retained a nonpublic record to check eligibility
if the person later sought the same relief.[@1]

## Written rules and local enforcement diverged

Innocente Oneisom's 1991 report, based on fieldwork in 1985, described Chuuk
using the former territorial rules. It found conflicting police records:
monthly reports listed 56 cases and 40 arrests in 1984, while daily reports
listed 36 cases. Those were local administrative records with different totals.[@4]

The [1978 enforcement entry](/history/micronesia-chuuk-police-cannabis-1978/)
traces that earlier record. The [Micronesia profile](/country/fm/) follows later
changes, including the separate laws of its states.
