For seven years cannabis shops in Ticino sold flowers as wardrobe fresheners, bath additives and potpourri. In 2003 Operation Indoor closed the shops and seized an industry that had grown in the space between the wording of Swiss law and its obvious purpose.

## The scented-bag loophole

The first Ticino hemp shops opened in 1996. Swiss narcotics law then prohibited hemp when it was intended for use as a drug. Sellers therefore labelled cannabis flowers for non-intoxicating purposes, most famously as scented bags for wardrobes.

The distinction was formal rather than convincing. Shops limited sales to adults while insisting that the products were decorative or aromatic. Authorities, customers and sellers understood the trade, but enforcement remained light enough for it to expand.

## A regional boom

Ticino's climate and struggling farm sector helped cannabis become what SWI called the region's “green gold”. Farms changed crops, greenhouses and indoor rooms appeared, and production developed the paperwork of a conventional business, including invoices and value-added tax.

Hemp tourists filled hotels and campsites, leading journalists to call Ticino a new Amsterdam. The rapid expansion also brought complaints about nuisance and put pressure on law enforcement. By 2000 public prosecutor Antonio Perugini was challenging the pretence behind adult-only wardrobe products.

## Operation Indoor

The crackdown began in March 2003. Over six months police seized 198,000 plants, four tonnes of processed cannabis and CHF4 million in cash. Shops closed and 130 people were jailed.

The episode acquired a final twist on 26 October. Eight people broke into the military depot at Polveriera with blowtorches and chainsaws and removed three vanloads of confiscated hemp. Police traced the rented vehicles and found the cannabis in an underground tunnel in the Verzasca Valley.

## National law, cantonal enforcement

Operation Indoor ended a distinctive market in one canton; it did not enact a new national cannabis law. The episode shows how intent-based prohibitions can produce a temporary quasi-legal market when officials tolerate labels that conceal the product's expected use.

## Why the index did not move

Because the [2003 operation](/history/switzerland-operation-indoor-2003/) was an enforcement change in Ticino rather than a nationwide statutory change, the [Swiss](/country/ch/) index series records it for context only. None of the six national scores moves.
