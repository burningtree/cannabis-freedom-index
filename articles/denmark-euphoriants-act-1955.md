Denmark's Euphoriant Substances Act made unauthorised possession part of a broader
drug-control framework in 1955. The law gave authorities a basis for prosecuting
cannabis offences, but the history of that power also depended on decisions about
when to use it.

## Why possession entered the law

The parliamentary record identifies the measure as Law No. 169 of 24 May 1955.
It followed a committee appointed in 1950, whose 1953 report proposed changes to
existing opium legislation.

Officials were concerned about drug use and access to substances through medical
prescriptions, including false prescriptions. The law expanded controls and
introduced offences involving unauthorised purchase, receipt and possession.
These concerns extended beyond cannabis: the framework also addressed other
drugs, including amphetamine preparations.

The parliamentary summary records unanimous approval at the third reading. The
measure emerged from an existing system of drug regulation rather than creating
every Danish restriction on intoxicants from nothing.

## Permission and prohibition

The act empowered the minister to designate controlled substances. It provided
different arrangements for especially dangerous substances and substances limited
to medical or scientific purposes, including authorised pharmaceutical channels.

The general maximum prison sentence rose from six months to two years. That
ceiling describes statutory power; it does not establish the usual punishment
for a cannabis user in 1955.

## The later distinction between users and suppliers

The Transnational Institute's historical report places cannabis offences under
the 1955 act alongside a later Penal Code provision aimed at organised supply
and larger quantities. It describes a long period in which enforcement treated
users more leniently than suppliers.

The [1969 warning policy](/history/denmark-warnings-1969/) is a separate event
in that history. Its importance lies in the difference between an offence
remaining on the books and prosecutors choosing a limited response to it.
Possession could remain illegal while attracting a warning rather than an
ordinary prosecution.

## A framework with changing consequences

The report traces a subsequent turn towards punishment in the early 2000s.
The [2004 change](/history/denmark-fines-2004/) replaced the earlier approach
with fines for personal possession, while later measures increased the cost of
repeat offences.

Those developments have their own entries because they changed the operation of
the system established earlier. They should not be projected backwards as the
uniform enforcement practice of 1955.

For [Denmark's timeline](/country/dk/), the act marks a significant legal power
over possession. Understanding its effect requires following both that power
and the later policies governing its exercise.
