Yugoslavia’s 1950 drug law prohibited growing Indian hemp except in specified scientific institutions’ experimental fields.[@1] Bosnia and Herzegovina was covered by the federal law, which put drug cultivation, manufacture and trade under state control as part of the planned economy.[@1]

## Cannabis entered a planned supply chain

The law listed Indian hemp among the controlled raw materials and separately included its extracts, tinctures and resins.[@1] State control extended from growing and manufacture to the movement of the resulting drugs.[@1]

Production was restricted to medical and scientific purposes and assigned to designated public enterprises.[@1] Annual quantities were tied to the state economic plan, so the law regulated both the purpose of production and how much could be produced.[@1]

## Scientific fields were the growing exception

The cannabis-growing ban allowed specified scientific institutions to cultivate in their own experimental fields.[@1] The government’s public-health authority would prescribe the conditions and quantities.[@1]

This exception was narrower than the law’s arrangements for opium poppies, which could be grown in designated areas by several kinds of agricultural undertaking and household, with the harvest delivered to an authorised purchaser.[@1] Ordinary cannabis growers had no comparable agricultural route in that statute.[@1]

## Trading and border movements were controlled

Wholesale distribution of medicines ran through designated republican enterprises, while retail supply of narcotic drugs ran through pharmacies, dispensaries and veterinary-medicine outlets.[@1] An authorised state enterprise handled imports and exports, with permission required for each consignment.[@1]

Imports served medical and scientific requirements, and exporters needed confirmation of a valid import certificate from the receiving country.[@1] Enterprises and dispensing outlets also had to keep special registers recording receipts and issues.[@1]

## Unlawful activity brought labour or prison

Unauthorised cultivation, production or preparation for gain, and unlawful drug trading, could bring correctional labour or up to two years in prison, with a higher forced-labour imprisonment range for specified aggravated conduct.[@1] Unlawful imports or exports carried correctional labour or up to three years, rising to five for the aggravated cases.[@1]

The later [Bosnian state law](/history/bosnia-narcotics-law-commencement-2006/) provided a permitted hemp crop for specified fibre and seed purposes.[@2] After [medical rescheduling in December 2025](/history/bosnia-medical-use-approved-2025/), officials were still preparing prescribing rules in September 2026, continuing the long distinction between controlled medical supply and ordinary adult access described in the [country profile](/country/ba/).[@3]
