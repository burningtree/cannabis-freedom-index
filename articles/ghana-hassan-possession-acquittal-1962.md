On 30 November 1962 Ghana's Supreme Court quashed an Indian-hemp
possession conviction and entered an acquittal, holding that the 1961
Act punished only hemp prepared for smoking or smoking residue — not
mere possession of leaves.[@1]

## Eight hundred grammes of leaves

Fatayi Hassan had been convicted in the Accra Circuit Court for
possessing 800 grammes of Indian hemp on 20 January 1961, charged under
sections 47(1) and 57 of the Pharmacy and Drugs Act, 1961.[@1] The
prosecution proved "green leaves" and "dried leaves" that tested as
Indian hemp — nothing more.[@1]

Section 47(1) read: no person shall possess without lawful excuse any
opium or Indian hemp "which is prepared for smoking or any residue from
the smoking" of either drug.[@1] Under that wording, the court said,
possession of Indian hemp simpliciter was no offence at all.[@1]

## The old law had been stricter

Before 13 June 1961, when the Act came into force, mere possession of
Indian hemp in any form had been an offence under section 12 of the
Dangerous Drugs Ordinance.[@1] Parliament's new text narrowed the ban
without saying so plainly — and Hassan, who appeared in person against
a Senior State Attorney, walked through the gap.[@1]

## A defective charge, not a licence

The court arrested the judgment on its own motion: the charge disclosed
no offence on its face, so the conviction could not stand.[@1] The
appeal was allowed, the conviction quashed, and a verdict of acquittal
entered, with the appellant discharged.[@1]

The decision concerned the charge and evidence before the court, not a
general right. Later law returned to broader bans: the [1961
Act](/history/ghana-pharmacy-drugs-act-1961/) sits in a chain running
through [PNDCL 236 of 1990](/history/ghana-pndcl-236-1990/) to the [2020 Narcotics Control Commission
Act](/history/ghana-narcotics-commission-act-2020/), under which holding
cannabis for personal use is again an offence — now punished with a
fine rather than prison.[@1]
