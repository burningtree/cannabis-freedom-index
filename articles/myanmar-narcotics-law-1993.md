Myanmar's replacement drug law of January 1993 kept cannabis possession
and growing punishable with five to ten years in prison and a possible
fine. It also treated possession of at least 75 grams of cannabis or
cannabis essence as possession for sale, bringing a heavier prison range.

## The military government replaced the earlier drug law

The State Law and Order Restoration Council enacted the law on
27 January 1993. It repealed the [1974 drug law](/history/myanmar-narcotics-law-1974/),
while allowing earlier rules and orders to continue where they were
consistent with the replacement.

One stated objective was implementing the UN convention against drug
trafficking, which [Myanmar had joined in 1991](/history/myanmar-1988-convention-1991/).
The law combined criminal offences with treatment, rehabilitation,
financial investigations and control over property linked to drugs.

## Keeping cannabis could become possession for sale

The definition of a narcotic drug expressly included the cannabis plant
and substances derived or extracted from it. The ordinary offence covered
growing, possession, transport and transfer, with five to ten years in
prison and a possible fine.

At 75 grams, the law deemed cannabis, cannabis essence or their combined
weight to be held for sale. The same presumption applied to transporting
or transferring that quantity. Possession for sale carried at least ten
years, with no upper limit stated in the penalty clause.

Production, distribution, sale, import and export carried at least fifteen
years, again without a stated upper limit, or death. The quantity rule
therefore mattered to which offence the holder faced. It gave no legal
allowance for keeping a smaller amount.

## Treatment registration had a prison penalty

A drug user had to register at a prescribed place or recognised medical
centre for treatment. Failing to register, or failing to follow the
health ministry's treatment directions, carried three to five years
in prison.

The law also directed ministries to provide rehabilitation, livelihood
training and aftercare. Its treatment route operated alongside the
offences for possessing and supplying the substance.

There were exceptions for authorised research, production and medical
work, and for permitted use, possession or transport under a registered
practitioner's direction. These depended on ministry consent and the
conditions set for the activity.

## The registration offence changed twenty-five years later

The [2018 amendment](/history/myanmar-drug-law-amended-2018/) removed
the registration prison offence and introduced a process involving
assessment, treatment orders and community service. It also raised the
cannabis quantity presumed to be for sale to 100 grams. The separate
possession offence remained, keeping prison central to
[Myanmar's cannabis controls](/country/mm/).
