Until November 1985 ganja was sold in licensed shops in much of India. India had
defended that system at the United Nations for a quarter of a century, and gave it
up in a single act of parliament.

## A licensed drug

British India taxed cannabis from [1798](/history/india-bhang-tax-1798/) and, on the
advice of the [Hemp Drugs Commission](/history/india-hemp-drugs-commission-1894/),
put it under licence in [1896](/history/india-act-xii-1896/): growing in approved
places, sale through licensed vendors, duty paid on the way. Independent India kept
the arrangement and left it to the states.

At the [1961 conference](/history/un-single-convention-1961/) India fought the
proposed world ban. It won an exception for bhang, by keeping leaves out of the
treaty's definition of cannabis, and it claimed the transitional period that let
traditional use of ganja and charas continue for 25 years.

## The deadline

That period was due to end in [1989](/history/single-convention-phase-out-1989/).
American pressure for a comprehensive drug law grew in the 1980s, and Rajiv
Gandhi's government acted before the deadline. The bill was introduced in the Lok
Sabha on 23 August 1985, received assent on 16 September and came into force on
14 November.

## What the Act bans

The Narcotic Drugs and Psychotropic Substances Act prohibits producing, selling and
possessing two things: charas, the resin, and ganja, the flowering tops. Leaves and
seeds are outside the definition unless they come with the tops, so bhang stayed
legal and the states regulate it. Some, such as Assam and Maharashtra, ban it;
most sell it through authorised dealers.

Penalties follow quantity.

| | Ganja | Charas | Penalty |
|---|---|---|---|
| Small quantity | Up to 1 kg | Up to 100 g | Up to 1 year |
| In between | | | Up to 10 years |
| Commercial quantity | 20 kg or more | 1 kg or more | 10 to 20 years |

Consumption is an offence in itself, with up to six months in prison.

## The criticism

Members of parliament objected at the time that the Act treated soft and hard drugs
alike; the government answered that soft drugs were a gateway. Critics since have
called it draconian, pointing to bail rules that amount to a denial of bail for
larger quantities and to a presumption of guilty intent. The Times of India wrote
that it created a drug problem where none had existed.

Use did not end. A government survey in 2019 counted about 31 million current
users, 2.83 per cent of Indians aged 10 to 75.

Changes have been small: the mandatory death sentence for repeat traffickers was
removed in [2014](/history/india-ndps-amendment-2014/), and in
[2021](/history/india-decriminalisation-consensus-2021/) ministries discussed ending
prison for personal use, without result.
