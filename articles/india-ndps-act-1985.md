Until November 1985 ganja was sold in licensed shops in much of India. India had
defended that system at the United Nations for a quarter of a century, and gave it
up in a single act of parliament.[@1]

## A licensed drug

British India licensed cannabis through provincial excise. The
[1896 excise act](/history/india-act-xii-1896/) covered the North-Western Provinces and Oudh,
the Punjab, the Central Provinces, Burma, Coorg, and Ajmere and Merwara. Bengal and the
other provinces kept their own systems. Independent India left growing, selling and
possession to the states.[@2]

At the [1961 conference](/history/un-single-convention-1961/) India fought the
proposed world ban. It won an exception for bhang, by keeping leaves out of the
treaty's definition of cannabis, and it claimed the transitional period that let
traditional use of ganja and charas continue for 25 years.[@3]

## The deadline

That period was due to end in [1989](/history/single-convention-phase-out-1989/).
American pressure for a comprehensive drug law grew in the 1980s, and Rajiv
Gandhi's government acted before the deadline. The bill was introduced in the Lok
Sabha on 23 August 1985, received assent on 16 September and came into force on
14 November.[@1]

## What the Act bans

The Narcotic Drugs and Psychotropic Substances Act prohibits producing, selling and
possessing two things: charas, the resin, and ganja, the flowering tops. Leaves and
seeds are outside the definition unless they come with the tops. That federal
exclusion does not decide whether a state allows leaf-only bhang.
State laws govern its possession and sale.[@6,2]

The familiar three-band sentencing structure came with the
[2001 reform](/history/india-ndps-amendment-2001/). Its small-quantity maximum was
six months, a fine, or both; the [2014 amendment](/history/india-ndps-amendment-2014/)
later raised that maximum to one year.[@4,5] Cannabis consumption remained a separate
offence, carrying up to six months, a fine, or both under the 2001 wording.[@4]

## The criticism

Members of parliament objected at the time that the Act treated soft and hard drugs
alike; the government answered that soft drugs were a gateway. Critics since have
called it draconian, pointing to bail rules that amount to a denial of bail for
larger quantities and to a presumption of guilty intent. The Times of India wrote
that it created a drug problem where none had existed.[@1]

Use did not end. A government survey in 2019 counted about 31 million current
users, 2.83 per cent of Indians aged 10 to 75.[@2]

Mandatory death for specified repeat drug offences became discretionary
in [2014](/history/india-ndps-amendment-2014/),[@5] and in
[2021](/history/india-decriminalisation-consensus-2021/) ministries discussed ending
prison for personal use, without changing the law.[@1]

In the index the 1985 ban takes possession from 5 to 0, enforcement from 5 to 2
and access from 5 to 2. The [2001 quantity bands](/history/india-ndps-amendment-2001/)
then raise possession from 0 to 1, which is where [India](/country/in/) remains.
