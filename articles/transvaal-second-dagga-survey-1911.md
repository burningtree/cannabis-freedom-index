A second Transvaal inquiry in 1911 found that most responding officers opposed a total dagga prohibition, even as a newspaper campaign portrayed cannabis smokers as a danger to white households.[@1] The inquiry included town magistrates as well as the mines studied three years earlier.[@1]

## A newspaper warned of violence and sexual danger

The Sunday Times published an alarmist account linking cannabis with insanity, criminality and threats to white women.[@1] It described African servants and white people adopting African smoking practices through the racist fears of the period.[@1]

The article called for prohibition, but an official in Johannesburg's Native Affairs administration reported that he could not find miners incapacitated in the way the newspaper described.[@1]

Historian Thembisa Waetjen uses the contrast to show how claims circulating in public could differ from observations reported by officials working around cannabis users.[@1]

## The earlier survey had already challenged a ban

The [1908 inquiry](/history/transvaal-mine-dagga-survey-1908/) had found managers describing moderate use and worrying that restrictions would deter workers from coming to the mines.[@1] In 1911, some respondents again defended workers' customs and reported little evidence of the newspaper's alleged effects.[@1]

One official described miners' belief that coughing after smoking helped relieve the dust they inhaled underground.[@1] These were reports of workers' practices and officials' beliefs within an unhealthy labour system.[@1]

## There were disagreements about youth and local rules

The replies did not present a single view of cannabis.[@1] Some officials thought use was increasing; others reported very little smoking in their districts.[@1]

Suggestions included age restrictions and controls over sales or growing in civilian areas.[@1] A similar dispute in [Zululand in 1909](/history/zululand-youth-women-dagga-proposal-1909/) had connected smoking with arguments over children, women and chiefly authority.[@1]

Other officials feared another restriction would add to African people's grievances after war, taxation and growing colonial interference.[@1] Most responding officers still did not support a blanket ban.[@1]

## National policy eventually took another course

An [unpresented national draft in 1916](/history/south-africa-habit-forming-drugs-bill-1916/) proposed banning use, possession, trafficking and unlicensed cultivation.[@1] A national cannabis prohibition followed in 1922.[@1]

The inquiries remain part of [South Africa's history](/country/za/) because they record opposition from within the administration itself, alongside workers' customs and the racial anxieties used to argue for suppression.[@1]
