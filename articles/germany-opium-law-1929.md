On 10 December 1929 President Hindenburg and the interior minister Carl Severing signed the Law on the
Trade in Narcotics, known as the Opium Law. Its first section added a new name to the drugs that
[Germany](/country/de/) controlled: *Indischer Hanf*, Indian hemp. It took effect on 1 January 1930.

## From pharmacy shelf to narcotic

Cannabis had long been a medicine in Germany. Imperial ordinances of
[1872](/history/germany-pharmacy-only-1872/) and [1890](/history/germany-medicines-ordinance-1890/)
let only pharmacies sell Indian hemp. They regulated a medicine; they did not ban possession or use.

Germany's first drug law came from defeat. The historian James Mills notes that Germany had been a
leading maker of morphine, heroin and cocaine, and its industry went to the Hague conference of 1912 to
protect that trade. In Article 295 of the Treaty of Versailles Germany agreed to bring the Hague
convention into force. The resulting [law of 1920](/history/germany-opium-law-1920/) covered opium,
morphine, cocaine and heroin. It did not mention cannabis, because the Hague convention had not
controlled it. Only the second Geneva convention of 1925 did, and the 1929 law was Germany's way of
carrying it out.

## What the law said

| Section | Rule |
|---|---|
| § 1 | Indian hemp, its extract and its tincture are controlled drugs |
| § 3 | Importing, growing, making, trading, buying or supplying them needs a permit; pharmacies dispensing on prescription, and their patients, are exempt |
| § 4 | Other purchases need a voucher from the Opium Office of the Reich Health Office |
| § 9 | Hashish and other cannabis resin are banned outright |
| § 10 | Up to three years in prison or a fine; convicted foreigners may be expelled |

The law did not make using cannabis, or holding it, an offence in itself; the crimes were importing,
making, trading and acquiring it without a permit. A doctor could still prescribe it, and a pharmacy
could still sell it.

## A long life

The law outlasted the republic that passed it. Ned Richardson-Little writes that in Weimar Germany
hashish was popular with some left-wing intellectuals, among them Walter Benjamin. Under the Nazis
cannabis stayed too exotic to become a social issue. After the war the Soviet occupation authority
[reinstated the 1929 law](/history/germany-soviet-zone-opium-law-1945/) in the east, where it stayed the
only drug law until the [1973 Narcotic Drugs Act](/history/germany-gdr-narcotic-drugs-act-1973/). In the
west it lasted until the [Narcotics Law of 1971](/history/germany-narcotics-law-1971/), which raised the
maximum sentence from three years to ten.

In 2024 Germany's [Cannabis Act](/history/germany-cannabis-act-2024/) let adults possess cannabis and
grow it at home.
