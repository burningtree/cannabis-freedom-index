Malta’s cannabis regulator allowed licensed associations to produce and distribute resin in May 2025, giving members who preferred hashish a regulated supply route.[@1] ARUC said those users had previously depended on the illicit market and presented the change as part of its harm-reduction approach.[@1] Production had to use the associations’ quality-controlled flower and solventless extraction.[@1]

## Resin joined the association supply system

The permission extended the system created by [Malta’s 2021 reform](/history/malta-legalisation-2021/), which allowed licensed non-profit associations to cultivate for their members.[@3] Membership and distribution operate within that statutory system, alongside the separate household growing allowance.[@3]

ARUC’s initial announcement counted one gram of resin as three grams of dried flower because of its higher potency.[@1] The daily seven-gram and monthly fifty-gram distribution allowances applied to the combined flower-and-resin equivalent, so a member’s different products were counted together.[@1]

## Processing required additional approval

The [January 2026 directive](/history/malta-processing-directive-2026/) set out a specific approval process for associations that wanted to make resin or hash.[@2] Holding a cultivation and distribution permit did not automatically include processing permission.[@2] Applicants had to describe the product, responsible person, equipment, staff, method, premises layout, strains and expected output.[@2]

The authority could refuse an application where the proposed process or facilities did not meet its requirements.[@2] An association therefore needed both the ordinary operating permission and approval for this additional work before supplying its processed products.[@2]

## Finished products had to be solid and tested

Only solventless methods were allowed, with purified water the permitted liquid exception.[@2] The directive excluded distillates, oils and tinctures and required a solid finished product.[@2] It also required testing of the processed batch before release, together with records of output, waste and member supplies.[@2]

The 2026 equivalents depended on THC concentration: a gram counted as one gram of flower up to 33%, two grams at 34–66%, and three grams above 66%.[@2] Associations had to account for mixed supplies within the same daily and monthly limits.[@2]

## Associations also faced inventory controls

The [February 2026 regulations](/history/malta-association-stock-limits-2026/) placed category-based caps on distribution-site stocks and required prior written authorisation for transport.[@4] Grow-site storage was also limited, with surplus destruction requirements.[@4] The [Malta profile](/country/mt/) brings these operating rules together with the personal possession, cultivation and consumption limits that members meet outside an association.[@3,4]
