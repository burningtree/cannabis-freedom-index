Peru's Decree-Law 19505, dated 22 August 1972, changed an important feature of
the earlier drug law: the explicit treatment of clandestine consumers as
trafficking offenders. Its preamble distinguished people profiting from the trade
from consumers, whom it described as victims needing care and recovery.

The resulting rules combined a narrower trafficking definition with a separate
judicial process for consumers. The change should be understood through both
parts, rather than equated with unrestricted personal use.

## What the earlier law said

[Decree-Law 11005 of 1949](/history/peru-decree-law-11005-1949/) had included
purchase and use among its trafficking activities. Article 12 separately treated
clandestine individual consumers as authors or accomplices to the offences
covered by the decree.

The original scheme allowed mitigation when consumers identified people involved
in manufacture, importation, sale or delivery. It therefore connected a consumer's
position to the penal framework and to cooperation against suppliers, rather
than creating a distinct response based solely on treatment.

## Two textual changes mattered

The 1972 decree rewrote Article 2, omitting purchase and use from its list while
retaining sale, storage, distribution, transport and other commercial activities.
It also preserved the provision concerning cannabis cultivation, production and
harvest for obtaining narcotics.

Article 4 replaced the old Article 12 with a rule defining accomplices through
collaboration in the listed criminal acts. The former express classification of
clandestine consumers disappeared. These changes show why the 1972 measure
belongs as a separate event in the legal history.

## A separate process could still be coercive

Article 8 provided different procedures for minors and adults identified as
individual consumers. For adults it involved a civil judge, questioning and
contact with relatives, followed by institutional confinement or other measures
the judge considered appropriate.

The law thus changed the legal route without guaranteeing freedom from state
intervention. Replacing a trafficking classification with a treatment-oriented
judicial process could still involve loss of liberty. The statute alone does
not establish how often those powers were used.

## Reform within a continuing prohibition framework

The decree also addressed young people involved in trafficking and treated the
use of minors as an aggravating circumstance. Its changes operated within an
existing drug-control system whose cultivation and supply offences continued.

[Peru's later 1978 legislation](/history/peru-decree-law-22095-1978/) and
[1981 personal-dose reform](/history/peru-personal-dose-dependence-reform-1981/)
are further milestones. The 1972 text establishes an earlier distinction between
consumers and traffickers, while leaving broader questions of lawful possession,
medical access and enforcement to their own evidence.
