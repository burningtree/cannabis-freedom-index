Cameroon's drug law of 7 August 1997 set a separate penalty for people possessing, buying or growing a small supply for themselves. Courts could impose months in prison, a fine alone, or both, and could waive punishment after a solemn promise not to reoffend.

## International commitments shaped the law

Cameroon had joined the main UN drug conventions and created a national anti-drug committee. Explaining the law to the government's cannabis committee in 2017, justice ministry magistrate Bikele Noah Lambert said it was intended to meet those international commitments and strengthen the response to drug production and trafficking.

He described cannabis and cannabis resin as high-risk drugs in the first controlled list. Earlier [French colonial rules](/history/french-cameroons-poisonous-substances-1926/) had already controlled hashish supplies and punished group use. The national law combined strict controls on production and supply with a distinct route for small personal quantities.

## Small personal supplies had their own penalty

For high-risk plants or substances, including cannabis oil, the personal-use range was two months to one year in prison, a fine of 25,000 to 1.25 million CFA francs, or both. Cannabis derivatives other than oil had a shorter range: one to six months, a fine of 25,000 to 500,000 francs, or both.

The rule covered possession, purchase and cultivation. It required a quantity small enough to be considered intended for the person's own consumption, without setting a gram or plant limit. Judges could waive punishment if the person made a recorded promise in court not to repeat the offence.

## Growing and supply remained punishable

The law prohibited cannabis cultivation and required landowners, farmers and other occupants to destroy plantations appearing on their land. Outside the small personal-use rule, cultivation and the main trafficking offences carried ten to twenty years in prison, a fine, or both.

Offering or giving a high-risk drug directly to someone for that person's consumption had a separate two-to-five-year range, again with a fine-only option. Helping others use drugs, including by providing premises, carried five to ten years, a fine, or both.

## Treatment could replace or accompany punishment

Courts could require treatment or suitable care for people with drug dependence convicted of specified offences. It could replace punishment or be added to it. Evading that requirement carried two to five years, a fine, or both.

Twenty years later, the government was preparing [higher fines and restrictions on sentencing leniency](/history/cameroon-draft-harsher-law-2017/). The contrast between long supply penalties and discretion for personal quantities remained central to [Cameroon's](/country/cm/) drug law.
