On 19 April 1932 Puerto Rico made marijuana a crime from the ground up: Act 12 punished planting, importation, purchase and sale.[@1] Anyone convicted faced between one month and a year in jail.[@2]

## The island acted through its own legislature

The law was an insular statute, passed by Puerto Rico's legislature and printed in both English and Spanish official texts — the Spanish title punishes "la siembra, importación, compra y venta de la marijuana".[@1] It covered the whole chain at once — growing the plant, bringing it in, buying it and selling it — rather than starting with possession alone.[@1,2]

Two years later the legislature was already reworking it: Act No. 51 of 13 May 1934 amended the 1932 act's title and text.[@1] Its most concrete change was jurisdictional, giving the district courts exclusive jurisdiction over marijuana cases.[@1]

## The 1934 statute fell in step with a US model law

The 1934 reworking moved the island in step with the Uniform State Narcotic Drug Act then being taken up across US states.[@2] The [re-enacted ban](/history/puerto-rico-cannabis-reenactment-1934/) widened the net beyond planting and dealing to possession and use, including marijuana cigarettes in any form.[@1]

By 1935 the legislature was amending the re-enacted section again. The rewritten rule listed manufacture, transportation, possession, use, purchase, sale, transfer and gift of any marijuana cigarette or cigar, whatever its shape.[@1] The ban kept broadening from the field to the finished cigarette.

## The misdemeanour era ended in 1959

The early regime punished cannabis with jail time measured in months. A [1959 narcotics law](/history/puerto-rico-narcotics-law-1959/) replaced that with prison of no less than five years and no more than twenty, plus a possible fine of up to twenty thousand dollars.[@3] A second offence drew the same five-to-twenty-year range with the same possible fine.[@3]

From the 1932 planting ban to the felony era, the island's cannabis law kept getting tougher for nearly three decades, and the felony regime proved durable: a 2013 decriminalisation bill failed, with polls showing seventy per cent against and twenty-six per cent in favour.[@2] More than eighty years after Act 12, a governor's executive order finally opened a legal medical route in [Puerto Rico](/country/pr/).[@2]
