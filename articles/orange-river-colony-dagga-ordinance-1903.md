The Orange River Colony made giving and receiving cannabis offences in 1903, including sales, gifts and exchanges.[@1] Known locally as dagga, cannabis came under civilian restrictions after Africans had been prosecuted and fined under a military proclamation left over from the South African War.[@1]

## Wartime controls entered civilian law

Britain's victory in the war left officials governing the former Boer republics and deciding how older controls would operate in peacetime.[@1] Historian Thembisa Waetjen traces the Orange River measure to confusion over a military proclamation still being used against African people holding dagga.[@1]

The colony adopted those provisions in its Dagga Prohibition Ordinance.[@1] Its wording covered both sides of a transfer: supplying cannabis and obtaining it from somebody else.[@1]

For users who acquired cannabis through gifts, barter or purchase, those ordinary transactions now fell within the prohibition.[@1] A payment was not needed for the supply restriction to apply.[@1]

## Neighbouring officials wanted controls too

In the Transvaal, officials also argued for restrictions, attributing intoxication, lost usefulness and violence to African dagga smokers.[@1] But their proposals encountered another part of colonial administration concerned with keeping the gold mines supplied with labour.[@1]

A [1908 labour-bureau survey](/history/transvaal-mine-dagga-survey-1908/) asked mine inspectors and other officers what they had actually observed.[@1] Many respondents described moderate use and saw little reason for prohibition, while some feared new restrictions would discourage men from coming to work.[@1]

The neighbouring territories therefore produced different responses to the same plant.[@1]

## Colonial rules followed different interests

The [Cape Colony's 1905 policy](/history/cape-colony-dagga-poison-listing-1905/) took another route by placing cannabis on a poison list and reserving sales to chemists.[@1] Growers opposed the commercial power that gave those buyers, while medical campaigners continued to seek stronger controls.[@1]

Waetjen places these disputes within a divided political landscape before the colonies joined the Union of South Africa in 1910.[@1] Cannabis policy involved labour recruitment, professional authority and racial government as well as claims about intoxication.[@1]

## A national ban came later

The [national prohibition of 1922](/history/south-africa-national-ban-1922/) eventually brought cannabis within a Union-wide drug-control system.[@1] Officials still argued about enforcement, leading to a [compromise in remote African areas](/history/south-africa-enforcement-compromise-1922/).[@1]

The Orange River ordinance was one step in [South Africa's history](/country/za/): an early restriction whose neighbours' disagreements showed that a uniform national policy had yet to be made.[@1]
