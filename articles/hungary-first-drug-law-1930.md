In 1930 Hungary wrote its first drug rules, and they were not Hungarian rules at all: Act XXXVII simply declared the 1925 Geneva Opium Convention part of Hungarian law.[@1] From then on, Indian hemp counted as a controlled plant, and its resin could cross the border only on paper, certificate by certificate.[@1,2]

## There had been nothing before

Until the early 1930s, drug use and drug dealing had never appeared in Hungarian legislation at all.[@3] When parliament finally acted, it confined itself to transposing the international conventions, and Act XXXVII of 1930 was the vehicle.[@3]

The act's first section does the whole job in one sentence: the Geneva convention "is incorporated among the laws of the country."[@1] The second section then prints the convention's official Hungarian translation, definitions and all — a roll of some thirty signatories from Albania to Uruguay, pledging to cut manufacture and supervise the trade more strictly.[@1]

## Hemp entered under its trade names

The incorporated text defines Indian hemp as the dried flowering or fruiting tops of the female Cannabis sativa plant from which the resin has not been extracted — "under whatever name it appears in trade."[@1] That catch-all wording meant hashish, esrar, chiras and djamba all fell under the same rule.[@1,2]

The convention's hemp regime worked through paperwork. For resin and the ordinary resin-based preparations, every import needed a special certificate from the importing country's government, stating the approved purposes and pledging that the goods would not be re-exported.[@2] Plain Indian hemp could be imported only where it was required exclusively for medical or scientific purposes.[@2] Every permit went into numbered national registers, and the League's commentary walked governments through the machinery of export authorisations, bonded warehouses and free ports.[@2]

## The convention framework lasted decades

Hungary kept legislating by transposition for another generation. A 1961 law placed narcotics abuse among crimes against public safety and order, with the list of drugs set by a 1957 health ministry decree.[@3] The 1978 criminal code reorganised the offences, introducing a small-quantity concept while still blurring the line between consumers and dealers.[@3]

Hungary later joined the Single Convention system in [1964](/history/hungary-single-convention-1964/), but the 1930 incorporation had already fixed the pattern: international texts, domesticated whole, as [Hungary](/country/hu/) drug law.
