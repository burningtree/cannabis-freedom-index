Robert Randall turned a prosecution for growing cannabis into the first successful medical-necessity defence of its kind in the United States. His case then helped create a federal programme that supplied a small number of patients with government-grown cannabis.

## Glaucoma and a raid

Randall had glaucoma, a progressive disease that threatened his sight. Conventional treatment had not provided lasting relief. After finding that cannabis reduced his symptoms, he grew plants to maintain a supply. Police raided his home in August 1975.

Faced with a cultivation charge, Randall argued that using cannabis was necessary to preserve his vision. On 24 November 1976, federal judge James Washington accepted the necessity defence and ruled in his favour.

## From acquittal to federal supply

Randall also sued the Food and Drug Administration, Drug Enforcement Administration and other federal agencies for access. The settlement became the legal basis for a compassionate Investigational New Drug programme established in 1978.

Under the programme, approved patients received cannabis grown for the federal government at the University of Mississippi and administered through the National Institute on Drug Abuse. Eligibility initially covered conditions such as glaucoma and later expanded to include people with AIDS.

## A route for very few

The programme never became general medical legalisation. Wikipedia records 43 approvals, but says 28 people whose doctors completed the paperwork never received cannabis. At most fifteen patients were supplied at one time.

The federal government stopped admitting new patients in 1992. Four people were still receiving its cannabis in 2011. The programme therefore preserved an extraordinary contradiction: federal law kept cannabis in Schedule I while federal agencies grew and distributed it to selected patients.

Closing the programme during the AIDS epidemic also mattered politically. Patients and advocates seeking relief from anorexia and wasting increasingly built medical-cannabis campaigns outside this tightly controlled federal route.

## What moved in the index

The [1976 ruling](/history/us-randall-ruling-1976/) opened an exceptionally narrow lawful access route. The [United States](/country/us/) access score rises from 0 to 1; possession, cultivation, enforcement, sharing and consumption stay unchanged because the defence and programme applied only to individually approved patients. Broader state medical access did not arrive until [California's 1996 initiative](/history/us-california-medical-1996/).
