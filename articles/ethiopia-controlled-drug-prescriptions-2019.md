Ethiopia's replacement medicine law required pharmacies dispensing controlled
medicines to check an original prescription issued within the previous
fifteen days. Each prescription could cover only one narcotic or
psychotropic medicine, and had to identify the prescriber and the institution.

## A wider medicine law replaced the earlier controls

The law took effect on 28 February 2019. Its stated purposes included
protecting people from unsafe medicines and preventing the illegal
distribution and use of narcotic drugs, psychotropic substances and
chemicals used to make them.

It replaced the [2010 health-law controls](/history/ethiopia-drug-permits-health-law-2010/)
in the matters it covered. Those earlier rules had already required special
permits, special prescription paper and secure storage. The replacement
set out a detailed sequence of checks for the person writing a prescription
and the person dispensing it.

Cannabis fell within the law's treaty-based definition of narcotic drugs,
following Ethiopia's [accession to the Single Convention](/history/ethiopia-single-convention-1965/).
The licensed medicine trade remained subject to these general controls.

## A prescription had a short life

A licensed medical professional could prescribe controlled medicines only
in the health institution where they were authorised to work. They could
not prescribe the medicines for themselves.

Narcotic and psychotropic medicines needed their respective special
prescription papers. The prescriber had to keep a copy after giving the
original to the patient. If a mistake was made, the erroneous sheet had
to remain intact in the prescription folder. The folder was to be returned
to the national or regional regulator as appropriate.

The prescription lost its validity after fifteen days. Putting more than
one controlled medicine on the same paper was prohibited.

## The dispenser had to check the paper

An authorised health institution or medicine retailer had to confirm
the prescriber's name and the institution's stamp, the original document,
and writing or printing free of errors. It also had to check the correct
type of prescription paper, a readable serial number and the date.

Together, these requirements made the prescription's identity, age and
contents part of the dispensing decision. Holding a written order was
only one step in obtaining the medicine through an authorised supplier.

## Permits and locked storage continued

Manufacturing, importing or exporting controlled medicines required a
special permit. An import or export permit applied to a particular
shipment and lasted ninety days. Stocks and their invoices, registers and
prescriptions had to stay in a locked cupboard or special room, with the
key held by the authorised pharmacy professional.

The law kept [Ethiopia's controlled-drug supply](/country/et/) under
supervision from the shipment's permit to the prescription presented at
the dispensing counter.
