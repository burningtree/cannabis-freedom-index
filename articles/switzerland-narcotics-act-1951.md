Switzerland's new narcotics law brought cannabis under federal controls and
extended the drug ban to hashish. Approved in 1951,
the law took effect on 1 June 1952. The government wanted to act before
supplies arriving through neighbouring countries made cannabis a larger
domestic issue.

## Officials feared a trade arriving from abroad

The [government's proposal](/history/switzerland-cannabis-controls-proposal-1951/)
followed the [1925 Geneva Convention](/history/geneva-opium-convention-1925/),
which had brought Indian hemp into international drug controls. In its
explanation to parliament, the government said Swiss authorities had already
been supervising the trade provisionally.

Officials had delayed changing the law because cannabis had little importance
in Switzerland. After the war, however, they feared hashish or marijuana
could reach neighbouring states through foreign troops and then enter
Switzerland. Adding cannabis to the narcotics list was presented as a
precaution against that possibility.

## Lawmakers argued over medicinal uses

The government wanted to extend an existing prohibition on prepared smoking
opium to hashish. It argued that hashish was unnecessary for treatment, a
judgment that shaped the proposed ban.

During the parliamentary debate, the National Council sought a narrower rule:
prohibiting supply to the public while allowing importation and manufacture.
The recorded explanation pointed to preparations used against corns on the
feet. Medicinal use therefore featured in the debate over how far the
prohibition should reach.

The Federal Commission for Drug Issues later described the 1951 change as
extending the earlier opium prohibition to hashish. The distinction
between controlled medicines and prohibited drug supplies became a continuing
issue in Swiss cannabis policy.

## Existing stocks had to be dealt with

The new law also addressed supplies of drugs that had become prohibited.
Under cantonal supervision, existing stocks could be converted into a substance
the law allowed. Where that was impossible, they were to be destroyed.

For holders of those stocks, the change therefore reached material acquired
before the prohibition. The rule for converting or destroying stocks became part of the
legal machinery for dealing with newly banned substances.

## Consumption became a separate offence later

Switzerland added a specific offence of consuming drugs through the
[1975 revision](/history/switzerland-use-punishable-1975/).
That later step extended criminal law directly to use.

Decades later, [the 2022 medical reform](/history/switzerland-medical-2022/)
removed the special federal permission doctors had needed to prescribe
cannabis medicines. The place of cannabis in treatment remained central to
changes in [Swiss cannabis law](/country/ch/) long after the original debate.
