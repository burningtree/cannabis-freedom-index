Cannabis became a matter of international law because of a speech. It was given in
Geneva in late 1924 by a delegate whose subject was not on the agenda.

## The conference

The Second Opium Conference met to limit the production of opium, morphine and cocaine.
On its second day Mohamed El Guindy, of the newly independent Egypt, asked that hashish
be included. Britain objected that the matter had not been notified. El Guindy submitted
a formal proposal regardless.

## The case he made

He granted that hashish taken "occasionally and in small doses perhaps does not offer
much danger". The habitual user was another matter: he showed symptoms "very similar to
those of hysteria", his "intellectual faculties gradually weaken and the whole organism
decays". Hashish, he said, caused between 30 and 60 per cent of the insanity in Egypt. A
memorandum from his delegation raised the figure to about 70 per cent.

If cannabis were left off the list, he warned, it would take the place of opium and
cocaine and "become a terrible menace to the whole world".

## The evidence

The figures probably came from John Warnock, who ran Egypt's lunacy department from
1895 to 1923 and generalised from the patients he saw. The Cairo asylum's own report
for 1920–21 attributed 19 of its 715 admissions to hashish, fewer than the 48 it
attributed to alcohol.

Nobody at the conference had the means to check. Most delegates had no experience of
cannabis, and one account has them raising their hands for a ban "without knowing what
cannabis actually was".

## Why Egypt wanted it

Egypt had [banned hashish](/history/egypt-import-ban-1879/) for half a century and could
not stop it coming in from Greece, Turkey and the Levant. An international rule would
oblige the suppliers to act. Turkey, Greece, South Africa and Brazil supported the
proposal; [South Africa](/history/south-africa-league-request-1923/) had made a similar
request the year before.

## The result

A sub-committee recommended complete prohibition, with only Britain, India and the
Netherlands against. The conference would not go that far, and its drafters cut the
text back over El Guindy's "somewhat uncompromising insistence". The
[convention of February 1925](/history/geneva-opium-convention-1925/) banned exports to
countries that prohibited cannabis and required import certificates elsewhere.

Fourteen years later Egypt's own police chief
[told the League](/history/league-russell-anslinger-exchange-1938/) that hashish caused
no particular crime. By then the principle was fixed.
