For twenty years the Dutch number was thirty. Since the 1976 revision of the Opium Act, possession
of up to 30 grams was a misdemeanour the prosecution service left alone, and coffeeshops sold in
whatever quantities the market would bear.[@1] Then, in the mid-1990s, the government decided the
shops had grown too many, too big and too visible — and cut the number to five.[@1][@2]

## The Haarlem rehearsal

The tightening was rehearsed in Haarlem. In 1994 the city became the national test model: surprise
inspections, a 1,500-gram stock allowance, and a set of shop rules issued a week later that read
like a football referee's book — break one three times and you closed.[@2] The rules got the name
AHOJ-G: no Advertising, no Hard drugs, no Overlast (nuisance), no Jeugd (youth sales), no Grote
hoeveelheden (large quantities).[@2] In March 1995 Haarlem ordered its shops to build their stocks
down from 1,500 to 1,000 grams, and to the intended 500 grams by June.[@2] The age limit had already
moved from 16 to 18 in 1994.[@2]

## Five grams, nationwide

In 1995 the Dutch government tightened the regulations on cannabis shops across the country, and
from 1996 the new limits applied: personal possession tolerated only up to 5 grams instead of 30,
shops capped at 500 grams on the premises, tougher enforcement of growing and trade, and
municipalities given the say over whether shops could exist at all.[@1] Shops that broke the AHOJ-G
rules could be shut down.[@1] The number of coffeeshops, about 1,450 at the 1993 peak, fell to
around 1,275 in 1996 and about 900 by 2002 — while smaller towns, out of realism or a new mayor,
quietly started allowing a few of their own.[@2]

The 1996 lines still define [the Netherlands](/country/nl/): up to 5 grams not prosecuted, shops
capped at 500 grams of stock, and the back door — where the cannabis comes from — still a
crime.[@3]
