Spain’s cannabis social clubs grew from a legal distinction between personal consumption and supplying others. Transform’s account dates the first club to 2001. Members sought to organise their own supply collectively, using the private-use exception and the courts’ narrow tolerance of shared consumption as their starting point.[@1]

## Members tried to organise their own supply

The model was a private, non-profit association rather than an open shop. Existing members vouched for new members, and the association planned cultivation around their expected consumption. Fees covered production and operating costs, with revenue returned to the organisation.[@1]

Voluntary rules aimed to keep cannabis within that membership. Transform described daily allowances averaging three grams and a preference for consumption soon after distribution. These were practices adopted by clubs, rather than a three-gram entitlement written into Spanish criminal law.[@1,2]

## Personal consumption left a narrow opening

The criminal offence centred on trafficking and conduct that promoted or facilitated illegal consumption. Possession exclusively for personal use lay outside it, but collective growing raised the question of when an association became a supplier to other people.[@2]

Regional and municipal attempts to regulate associations did not remove the national criminal-law problem. Arana’s review described different approaches in Navarra, the Basque Country and Catalonia, alongside constitutional litigation over the limits of regional powers.[@2]

## The Ebers case exposed the boundary

The Bilbao association Ebers had 290 members, a gardener and a system for preparing and distributing cannabis against members’ consumption forecasts. Its rules forbade reselling cannabis and leaving the premises while smoking. Those safeguards did not prevent prosecution.[@3]

The Supreme Court’s 2015 judgment treated organised, continuing distribution to a membership open to new entrants as exceeding the shared-consumption exception. The Constitutional Court later annulled the convictions because the defendants had not been heard on a decisive question about their awareness of unlawfulness. That procedural decision did not create a national club licence.[@3]

## A model spread without a settled supply right

By 2018, Transform estimated roughly 400 clubs or similar associations, especially in Catalonia and the Basque Country. The model relied on members’ own rules and regional federations, leaving substantial variation between associations.[@1]

The [cultural atlas](/culture/traditions/cannabis-social-clubs/) follows its spread beyond Spain. The [Spain profile](/country/es/) keeps private personal use separate from public-possession fines and the criminal exposure of organised supply: operating as an association does not itself resolve the latter.[@1,2,3]
