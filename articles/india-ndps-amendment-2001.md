In October 2001, India introduced lighter sentences for small cannabis quantities.
The new maximum was six months in prison, a fine of up to 10,000 rupees, or both.
The heaviest quantity band still carried ten to twenty years plus a fine.[@1]

## A response to users waiting in jail

The [1985 law](/history/india-ndps-act-1985/) had made severe penalties central
to national drug control. In its later account of the reform, the Supreme Court
described people dependent on drugs spending long periods in custody as trials
dragged on and strict bail conditions kept release out of reach. Parliament
wanted less serious offences punished less severely while retaining deterrent
sentences for major trafficking.[@4]

The President [assented in May](/history/india-ndps-amendment-assent-2001/),
but the reform began on 2 October. The Narcotics Control Bureau's contemporary
report records that commencement and explains the new three-band structure.[@2]

## The amount changed the sentence

For unlawful possession, sale and the other cannabis dealings covered by the
amended provision, small quantities brought the six-month-or-fine alternative.
Intermediate quantities carried up to ten years plus a fine of up to 100,000
rupees. Commercial quantities carried ten to twenty years and a fine normally
between 100,000 and 200,000 rupees; a court could exceed that fine with recorded
reasons.[@1]

The [19 October notification](/history/india-cannabis-quantity-cutoffs-2001/)
supplied separate cutoffs for flower and resin:[@3]

| Product | Small-quantity cutoff | Commercial-quantity cutoff |
|---|---|---|
| Ganja | 1 kg | 20 kg |
| Cannabis resin | 100 g | 1 kg |

The definitions used less than the small cutoff and greater than the commercial
cutoff. These were criminal sentencing categories. Unlawful cultivation had a
separate maximum of ten years and a fine.[@1]

## Bail and treatment changed too

The strict bail test was narrowed to commercial quantities and separately named
serious offences, including financing illicit traffic and harbouring offenders.
The amendment also widened immunity for drug-dependent people who voluntarily
sought and completed recognised treatment: it covered consumption and
small-quantity offences. Leaving treatment unfinished could cost that
protection.[@1]

## Some prisoners missed the benefit

Lighter punishment applied to investigations and trials pending when the reform
began, but the law excluded appeals already pending that day. In
[Basheer in 2004](/history/india-basheer-pending-appeals-2004/), the Supreme Court
upheld that dividing line. People whose trials had concluded and whose appeals
were pending on 2 October 2001 remained subject to the earlier law.[@4]

The [2014 amendment](/history/india-ndps-amendment-2014/) later raised the
small-quantity cannabis maximum to one year, retaining the fine alternative.[@5]
The [India profile](/country/in/) follows the subsequent rules.
