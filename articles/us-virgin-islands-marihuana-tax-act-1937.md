Five days before the mainland's Marihuana Tax Act took effect, Franklin Roosevelt extended it to the Virgin Islands. His Executive Order 7715, signed on 26 September 1937, ordered that from 1 October no one in the territory could import, grow, make or deal in marihuana without registering and paying the federal tax — ending the legal trade there just as on the mainland.[@1]

## A mirror of the mainland

Congress had approved the Tax Act on 2 August 1937 as Public No. 238 of the 75th Congress, and its section 15 singled out the Virgin Islands: the president was authorised and directed to carry the Act's intent into the territory by providing for registration and the special and transfer taxes.[@1] The order built a miniature copy of the mainland machinery: the islands' Commissioner of Finance would do the work of the Internal Revenue collectors, and the Governor would stand in for the Commissioner of Narcotics.[@1] From 1 October 1937, importing, manufacturing, producing, compounding, selling, dealing, dispensing, prescribing, administering, giving away — or even acquiring — marihuana without complying was unlawful.[@1]

## Two-cent forms and Treasury stamps

The details were fiscal. Traders registered with the Commissioner of Finance on the governor's forms and paid the Act's special taxes, yearly each July.[@1] The order forms they needed cost two cents for the original and one copy; the stamps proving transfer-tax payment came from the Treasury in Washington on requisition.[@1] Anyone the mainland law excused from registering or paying owed nothing in the islands either.[@1] The governor could issue further regulations, following the Treasury's own as far as island conditions allowed, and he and his men could inspect and act to enforce the Act.[@1]

## The tax machinery stays

The order's framework outlived Roosevelt. In 1951 Harry Truman handed the president's Virgin Islands cannabis-tax powers — registration, special and transfer taxes, and medical-purpose exemptions — to the Secretary of the Interior, to be exercised in consultation with the Treasury.[@2] The tax-based control of the 1937 order thus passed intact into the territory's later drug law.[@2] The [territory page](/country/vi/) traces the road from tax stamps to the prison terms that followed.
