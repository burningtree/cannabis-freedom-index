In the 1960s Westerners pilgrimaged to Nepal for its cannabis and the region's hippie trail, and in 1961 the kingdom laid the foundations for that tourism, taxing and licensing drug sales from Kathmandu stores.[@1] The Eden Hashish Center, the Cabin and the Central Hashish Store lined a skinny lane called Jhochhen Tole — Freak Street — where licensed agents sold openly and everyone from farmers to the government took a cut.[@1,2] The plant was no stranger: an early-1800s commentator had already noted its local use.[@2]

## A couple of bucks bought everything

A couple of dollars covered food, lodging and some of the best pot on the planet.[@1] Each outlet curated a psychedelic menu: fresh buds, brownies, opium-laced joints called Chinese Crackers, and charas packed as conker-sized temple balls of hand-rolled resin.[@1]

Nepalese charas was recognised as among the best available, and the country became a gateway for every ganja lover.[@2] Demand from outside markets pushed hashish production up and opened smuggling routes through India to the wider world.[@2]

## The licences died in 1973

In 1973 Nepal cancelled the licences of all cannabis shops, dealers and farmers, under pressure from the United States and the international community.[@2] Personal cultivation and use were left alone, and the commerce continued illicitly — but the treasury lost some $100,000 in revenues, and later in the decade the government tried crop substitution instead.[@2]

King Birendra had outlawed the trade, with a complete ban of cannabis soon after.[@1] The [1976 Narcotics Act](/history/nepal-narcotics-act-1976/) of 22 September 1976 then prohibited cultivation, production, preparation, purchase, sale, trafficking, storage and consumption outright, classifying cannabis as a narcotic drug — while leaving one door open: the government could still license growing and selling for medicine or scientific research, or sale on a doctor's recommendation.[@2]

The licensed shops lasted barely a dozen years, but Freak Street's fame outlived them — the lane by Basantapur Durbar is still called hippie street today, though the trade went underground.[@2] Their rise and fall — taxation and fame, then American pressure and the [1973 closures](/history/nepal-shops-closed-1973/) — still shapes how [Nepal](/country/np/) debates the plant today.
