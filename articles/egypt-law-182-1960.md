On 5 June 1960 Egypt passed Law 182, the narcotics law that still governs cannabis
today. Its explanatory note ranks punishments by "decreasing progression" of the
danger the offender poses to society.[@1]

## A ladder of danger

Article 33 put the most dangerous first — importers, exporters and commercial
manufacturers — under life at hard labour.[@1] Article 34 covered the next rank down:
dealers, growers of the listed Table V plants, custodians who diverted drugs from
authorised purposes, and keepers of consumption dens — life or term hard
labour.[@1] Article 35, for offering drugs free or facilitating use, set the lighter
term of hard labour.[@1] Recidivists, officials and controllers faced
aggravation.[@1]

## The user at the bottom

Personal use got detention plus a fine of 500 to 3,000 Egyptian pounds, with at
least six months where the penal code's mitigation applied — meant, the note says, to
intimidate the tempted and reclaim them.[@1] But the same article let courts send
habitual users to a sanatorium instead, and barred prosecution entirely for anyone
who walked in voluntarily for treatment — unless twice confined before, or released
within five years, on the ground that treatment had failed them.[@1]

## Why then

The law capped a century of failed bans — 1868, 1874, 1877, 1879, 1884 — that had
produced smuggling instead of abstinence.[@2] Egypt had pressed for cannabis control
at Geneva in the 1920s and got it into the 1925 convention; the 1960 law wrote the
international regime into domestic iron.[@2] Amended in 1989, it grew harsher still:
death or life for dealing and importing, a year minimum for possession.

[Egypt's profile](/country/eg/) reflects the law the republic built and never
dismantled: [the 1960 statute](/history/egypt-law-182-1960/) over the [1884 growing
ban](/history/egypt-cultivation-ban-1884/), with hashish still smoked in the cafés.
