Drug suppliers in the northern Somali Republic faced ten years in prison and a fine under a decree issued in May 1984.[@1] The same decree imposed five years for hosting a drug-consumption venue and three years for entering it to consume.[@1]

## The decree followed earlier drug controls

The north, now administered as Somaliland, had already come under a [penal code](/history/somaliland-penal-code-1964/) that prescribed one to three years for unauthorised drug supply and possession for sale.[@2] Its consumption-venue penalties were lower: six months to two years for allowing the premises to be used, and up to six months for attending to consume.[@2]

A [1970 law](/history/somaliland-narcotics-law-1970/) added health-ministry licences, possession offences, controlled cultivation and prescription dispensing.[@3] Its accompanying list expressly included cannabis, hashish and Indian hemp.[@6]

The 1984 decree's preamble called for stronger punishment and action against intoxicating drugs.[@1]

## Supply and consumption venues faced fixed terms

The ten-year offence covered unlawful manufacture, production, import, dealing and supplying or obtaining drugs for another person.[@1] It also imposed a fine of 50,000 Somali shillings.[@1]

The penalty increased where drugs were delivered to someone under eighteen or to people within the decree's specified mental-impairment or dependency categories.[@1]

Hosting a public or private place where people gathered to consume drugs brought five years and a 25,000-shilling fine.[@1] Entering it to consume brought three years and a 20,000-shilling fine.[@1] The venue offences distinguished those allowing the gathering from those attending it.[@1]

## Drug cases went to security courts

The decree assigned drug prosecutions to the National Security Court and its regional branches.[@1] Pending cases before ordinary courts were to be transferred, while earlier offences remained subject to the penalties applicable before the decree.[@1]

It took effect on the president's signature and was then submitted for conversion into statute.[@1] Parliament's June conversion law retained the decree and provided its own commencement period after publication.[@4]

## Somaliland later established a constitutional test

The [2001 constitution](/history/somaliland-constitutional-legal-continuity-2001/) retained existing laws only where compatible with sharia, individual rights and fundamental freedoms.[@5] It also required crimes and punishments to be established by law and guaranteed an accused person's presumption of innocence.[@5]

Those constitutional conditions became part of the legal order in [Somaliland](/country/xs/) alongside its inherited criminal legislation.[@5]
