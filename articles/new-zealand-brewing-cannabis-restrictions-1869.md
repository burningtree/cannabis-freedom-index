New Zealand expressly prohibited cannabis in brewing under its Distillation Act of 1868. The relevant provisions came into operation on **1 January 1869**, according to a contemporary Customs notice. They regulated particular transactions and uses well before the country’s later general drug-control legislation.

## Cannabis in the brewing provisions

The *New Zealand Gazette* of 11 November 1868 reproduced the provisions for brewers and spirit merchants. Its notice, dated 3 November, announced when the rules would take effect.

Section 131 named “cannabis indicus” alongside opium, tobacco, nux vomica and other substances. A public brewer could not use these substances, or their specified preparations, in beer, ale, porter or the worts from which the drink was made. The section also extended to other deleterious or poisonous matter.

On a first conviction, the brewer faced a penalty of **£200**. A second or subsequent offence was a misdemeanour carrying a fine up to **£500** and imprisonment for up to **two years**, with or without hard labour. The affected beer and worts were forfeited and could be seized by authorized officers.

## Possession and supply

Section 132 restricted possession of the listed substances by brewers and beer retailers. It preserved an exception for medicinal or domestic purposes, with the burden of proving the purpose placed on the brewer or retailer. Breach carried a **£50** penalty, and the substances could be seized and forfeited.

The supply rule was differently worded. Section 133 penalized anyone knowingly selling, disposing of, sending or delivering the listed drugs or preparations to a brewer or beer retailer except for a medicinal purpose. It did not repeat the domestic-purpose exception. The supplier bore the burden of proving the medicinal purpose and faced a **£50** penalty.

Section 134 also imposed a **£50** penalty for knowingly selling or disposing of adulterated drinks, or having such drinks found on one’s premises. Its wording included spirits as well as ale, beer and porter.

## What this restriction establishes

The gazetted text is direct evidence of cannabis-specific trade restrictions effective in 1869. It does not establish a general prohibition on cannabis possession by everyone, or a ban on every medicinal use. The exceptions and the identity of the regulated traders are essential to understanding its reach.

The source does not show how frequently cannabis was used in brewing or whether these particular provisions produced prosecutions. Those questions require separate enforcement evidence. The entry records the commencement of the documented rules, rather than assuming that the statute’s 1868 title supplies their operative date.
