In March 1885, an agreement made Egypt’s customs regime applicable to United States citizens, vessels and commerce on the basis already accepted for Greek subjects. The convention printed with it explicitly singled out hashish for Egyptian control and set out search procedures.

## A precise commencement clause

The agreement was signed at Cairo on 16 November 1884 by N. D. Comanos for the United States and Nubar Pasha for Egypt. Its final clause made it operative immediately upon the United States Senate’s consent.

The official Statutes at Large records that consent on 18 March 1885. Presidential ratification and proclamation followed on 7 May. This entry therefore uses 18 March, rather than the signature or proclamation date. The proclamation itself repeats the agreement’s unusual commencement mechanism.

## What the United States accepted

The operative paragraph consented to applying the Egyptian customs regulations already applicable under the [Greek–Egyptian convention of 1884](/history/egypt-greek-customs-hashish-convention-1884/) to American citizens, shipping, commerce and navigation.

The agreement also secured for American commerce rights, privileges and immunities granted to other foreign powers. Its significance for cannabis lies in the customs regime it accepted, rather than in a separate American statute naming cannabis as a domestic offence.

## The hashish exception

The annexed convention’s article V excluded hashish, together with several other named commodities, from its general trade stipulations. It reserved Egypt’s control over those goods and applied the relevant regime to Greek subjects on the same terms as Egyptian subjects. The American agreement extended the customs framework to US citizens and commerce.

Article V also allowed immediate warehouse or dwelling searches. A duplicate search order was to be communicated to the relevant consular officer, who could attend, but notification could not delay the search. The text thus combined foreign commercial rights with recognition of Egyptian enforcement powers.

## The limits of the evidence

These provisions support a history of international arrangements affecting cannabis enforcement before the twentieth-century drug conventions. They do not establish a cannabis ban throughout the United States, nor do they show how frequently American premises or ships were actually searched.

The convention separately described general maritime customs powers, including a vessel-size threshold and a coastal distance limit. Those provisions should not be turned into an unlimited cannabis-search authority. Likewise, its general tariff provisions cannot be applied to hashish without accounting for article V’s exception.

The agreement and annex were read in the official US publication. This event is unscored context in [Egypt’s history](/country/eg/), recording the extension of enforcement arrangements to another country’s commerce.
