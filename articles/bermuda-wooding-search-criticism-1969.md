Young people in Bermuda complained that marijuana policing made ordinary gatherings look suspicious. The commission investigating the 1968 disturbances agreed that officers were interpreting their search powers too widely. Its report, signed on 24 January 1969, called for reasonable grounds tied to the individual being searched.[@1]

## Marijuana policing gained new powers

Bermuda had formed a [specialist narcotics squad in 1961](/history/bermuda-narcotics-squad-1961/). The commission later reported no more than twenty-two drug arrests in its first five years, although police intelligence suggested a more extensive marijuana trade.[@1]

The [1966 amendment](/history/bermuda-warrantless-drug-search-powers-1966/) allowed officers to search people without a warrant when they reasonably suspected unlawful drug possession. It also covered vehicles, vessels and aircraft suspected of carrying illicit drugs, and allowed seizure of drugs and their containers. Officers bore the burden of showing that their conduct was reasonable.[@2]

## Most searches found no drugs

The commission examined records for the sixteen weeks from the beginning of 1968 to 25 April, the first day of the disturbances. Of 103 people searched, eleven had quantities warranting prosecution. Seven had minute traces for which police sought no sanction, and eighty-five had nothing.[@1]

Four of the eleven successful searches took place at the airport. Among the other ninety-nine searches, forty-nine were in or near Court Street and five elsewhere in Pembroke. The commission stressed the public humiliation involved: people were searched in front of others, sometimes their girlfriends.[@1]

## A reputation could not justify a search

Young witnesses described Court Street as a traditional meeting place where they talked, socialised and showed off their clothes. They resented being watched as if everyone gathered there dealt in or smoked marijuana.[@1]

The commission said an officer needed reasonable grounds to suspect that a particular person possessed drugs at that moment. Knowing someone's reputation, associates or appearance was insufficient. Information from an informer needed to be assessed for its content and reliability. The commission attributed the problem to an excessively broad reading of the law.[@1]

## The commission supported prohibition

The report recommended keeping the drug law, arguing that proper administration would remove much of the grievance. It preferred tighter observance of the existing search limits to changing the cannabis prohibition.[@1]

Bermuda's later framework continued to [exclude cannabis from ordinary professional prescribing permissions in 1973](/history/bermuda-cannabis-prescribing-restrictions-1973/), while allowing ministerial licences.[@3] The [Bermuda profile](/country/bm/) follows the subsequent medical and possession reforms.
