Long before Kenya banned cannabis, a colonial officer wrote down who smoked it and how. C.
W. Hobley's 1902 survey, titled for eastern Uganda, covered peoples of what is now western
Kenya and recorded bhang — Indian hemp — grown for home consumption alongside tobacco.[@1]

The customs differed by people. Among the Wanga and their neighbours it was always smoked
in a hubble-bubble or water-pipe made from a gourd; men and unmarried women smoked it, but
a married woman might not smoke it again.[@1] Among the Luo it was smoked separately in a
water pipe by men only, with young fighting men barred from it.[@1] The Nandi, by contrast,
neither grew nor smoked it.[@1]

Hobley's account fixes three facts about the plant's deep history in the region: it was
grown, not bought; it was smoked in water-pipes, not eaten; and its use was governed by
age, sex and status — a married woman's abstinence, a warrior's bar. The Swahili word the
survey records, bhang, survives today as [bangi](/culture/language/bangi/).

Prohibition followed within years of the survey: the commissioner asked for a ban in 1908
and Kenya banned the plant in 1914, criminalising customs Hobley had found unremarkable.
