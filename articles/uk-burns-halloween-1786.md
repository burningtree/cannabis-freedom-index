Robert Burns's first collection, *Poems, chiefly in the Scottish dialect*, appeared in Kilmarnock in July 1786. Among its poems was *Halloween*, which brought a Scottish hemp-seed courtship charm into print. Cannabis supplied the crop and the imagery; no drug was consumed in the episode.

Publication gives the custom a secure place in the literary record. It does not establish when the practice began or how widely it was observed.

## A book in Scots

The University of Glasgow's account of the Kilmarnock edition identifies John Wilson as its printer and records a print run of 612 copies. Its digitisation preserves a copy held in the university library's Special Collections.

The collection made local language and rural experience available to a readership beyond the immediate communities it described. In *Halloween*, the dialogue and action are accompanied by explanatory notes for readers unfamiliar with the customs. Those notes are particularly valuable evidence for what hemp meant within the scene.

## Sowing a future relationship

Burns describes a person scattering hemp seed and making an appeal to a future sweetheart. The promised figure would appear behind the sower, gathering the crop. The act of sowing and the imagined act of harvesting gave a relationship that had not yet formed a visible shape.

The agricultural action is central. This is not an account of smoking, eating resin or inhaling fumes. Reading the hoped-for apparition as evidence of cannabis intoxication would import a mechanism that the poem and note do not describe.

## Fear becomes comedy

Jamie Fleck, the character who tries the charm, initially dismisses frightening stories. Outside in the darkness, his confidence fails. A pig provides the comic explanation for his alarm.

The episode records belief without simply endorsing it. Burns lets the reader see the attraction of discovering a future partner and the ease with which expectation becomes fright. His poem is crafted literature, not a survey recording every household's practice or a neutral transcript of one ritual.

## Hemp before the drug-law narrative

The publication belongs to a history of cannabis as a familiar crop in seasonal folklore. Britain's later [cannabis ban](/history/uk-ban-1928/) concerns a different use and political setting; the poem itself changed no drug law.

The [tradition entry on hemp-seed divination](/culture/traditions/halloween-hemp-seed-divination/) explains the custom more fully. Burns's contribution was to preserve its language, action and humour in a dated work. That evidence widens cannabis history beyond the question of who consumed an intoxicant, to include what people imagined through a plant they knew.
