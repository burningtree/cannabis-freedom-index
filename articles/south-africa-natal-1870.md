Natal’s 1870 cannabis provision targeted Indian immigrants within a colonial labour system. Its legal significance needs a narrower description than the familiar claim that Natal simply banned cannabis that year. The evidence reviewed here supports a power to make restrictions; it does not establish when a prohibitory rule actually took effect.

## A power and its implementation

Craig Paterson’s historical research examines the Indian Immigrants Commission report published in 1887. The commissioners asked the Governor in Council to exercise powers under section 70 of Law No. 2 of 1870. The contemplated restrictions covered cannabis use and possession, supply to Indian immigrants, destruction of the substance and penalties connected with cultivation for smoking.

That request matters because it distinguishes the statutory authority from a rule made under it. A legislature may give an executive power to prohibit conduct without itself bringing every contemplated prohibition into force. The original section and the subordinate notices must be read together to establish the operative law.

## Uneven evidence

Thembisa Waetjen’s chapter in *Cannabis: Global Histories* describes the 1870 provision more directly as a prohibition. It also stresses that administration of cannabis use among indentured workers was largely medical, including hospital treatment, and that the later commission produced no additional legal controls.

Paterson’s account makes the implementation problem particularly visible: an [1884 proposal](/history/natal-prohibition-proposal-1884/) failed to produce a rule, and the [1887 commission](/history/natal-law-tightened-1887/) still sought action under the older authority. These findings caution against treating the year of the enabling legislation as a verified commencement date for an enforced ban.

## A restriction defined by status

The measure concerned Indian immigrants rather than everyone living in Natal. It belongs to the history of colonial labour administration and unequal legal treatment. It cannot establish a national South African prohibition: the later national state did not yet exist, and the legal position of other colonial populations requires separate evidence.

This article relies on the historians’ accounts and Paterson’s reproduced commission passages. The original 1870 statute and any implementing notices were not inspected. Until those documents are retrieved, the defensible event is authorization of targeted restrictions, with commencement and enforcement unresolved.
