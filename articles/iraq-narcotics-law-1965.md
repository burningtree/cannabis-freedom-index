Iraq's 1965 drug law kept cannabis growing and unauthorised possession prohibited, but allowed a supervised route for crops grown exclusively for fibre and seeds.[@1] It brought older cultivation and drug controls into one law, with prison terms, fines and crop destruction behind the restrictions.[@1]

## Older bans were brought into one law

The [1933 cultivation ban](/history/iraq-hemp-cultivation-law-1933/) had required cannabis plants to be uprooted at the grower's expense.[@2] A first offence meant a fine of up to ten dinars; repeat offending could bring up to three months in prison, a larger fine, or both.[@2] Anyone who knew of planted crops had to report them to the authorities.[@2]

A [drug law followed in 1938](/history/iraq-dangerous-drugs-law-1938/).[@6] The government's explanation for replacing both laws in 1965 pointed to gaps found during their use and to the need to match the newer international rules.[@6] Iraq had [joined the Single Convention](/history/iraq-single-convention-1962/) in 1962.[@6] That treaty supplied the new law's definitions and control system.[@6]

## Fibre and seed crops needed special permission

The new cultivation ban covered cannabis alongside poppy, coca and khat.[@1] However, cannabis grown exclusively for industrial fibre and seeds could be authorised by the agriculture ministry and had to remain under its direct supervision.[@1] Authorities were required to uproot and destroy illegal or wild plants under agricultural specialists' supervision.[@1]

For someone growing cannabis, the crop's purpose and the permission mattered.[@1] The fibre-and-seed exception was expressly written into the cultivation rule.[@1]

## Possession followed a controlled medical supply chain

Possession was reserved to authorised professionals, institutions and other approved holders, or people obtaining drugs from a licensed pharmacist on a registered doctor's prescription.[@1] Manufacturers needed licences, and imports and exports required permission.[@1]

The general penalty for breaking the law was a fine of 100–1,000 dinars, six months to five years in prison, or both.[@1] Illegally held drugs were confiscated.[@1] Lesser penalties applied to specified record-keeping and prescription offences and failures to report cannabis or poppy crops.[@1]

## Penalties hardened and the crop exception disappeared

The [1967 amendment](/history/iraq-drug-penalties-1967/) replaced that general penalty with separate rules.[@3] Personal possession or growing brought one to five years in prison and a fine, while suppliers faced hard labour.[@3] Giving drugs away was included among the supply offences.[@3]

In [1979](/history/iraq-drug-treatment-hemp-reform-1979/), Iraq removed the industrial-crop exception altogether.[@4] The law continued to shape [Iraq's cannabis controls](/country/iq/) through repeated amendments until its [replacement in 2017](/history/iraq-narcotics-law-2017/).[@5]
