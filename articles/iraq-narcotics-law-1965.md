Iraq's 1965 drug law kept cannabis growing and unauthorised possession prohibited,
but allowed a supervised route for crops grown exclusively for fibre and seeds.
It brought older cultivation and drug controls into one law, with prison terms,
fines and crop destruction behind the restrictions.

## Older bans were brought into one law

The [1933 cultivation ban](/history/iraq-hemp-cultivation-law-1933/) had required
cannabis plants to be uprooted at the grower's expense. A first offence meant
a fine of up to ten dinars; repeat offending could bring up to three months
in prison, a larger fine, or both. Anyone who knew of planted crops had to
report them to the authorities.

A [drug law followed in 1938](/history/iraq-dangerous-drugs-law-1938/).
The government's explanation for replacing both laws in 1965 pointed to gaps
found during their use and to the need to match the newer international rules.
Iraq had [joined the Single Convention](/history/iraq-single-convention-1962/)
in 1962. That treaty supplied the new law's definitions and control system.

## Fibre and seed crops needed special permission

The new cultivation ban covered cannabis alongside poppy, coca and khat.
However, cannabis grown exclusively for industrial fibre and seeds could be
authorised by the agriculture ministry and had to remain under its direct
supervision. Authorities were required to uproot and destroy illegal or wild
plants under agricultural specialists' supervision.

For someone growing cannabis, the crop's purpose and the permission mattered.
The fibre-and-seed exception was expressly written into the cultivation rule.

## Possession followed a controlled medical supply chain

Possession was reserved to authorised professionals, institutions and other
approved holders, or people obtaining drugs from a licensed pharmacist on a
registered doctor's prescription. Manufacturers needed licences, and imports
and exports required permission.

The general penalty for breaking the law was a fine of 100–1,000 dinars,
six months to five years in prison, or both. Illegally held drugs were
confiscated. Lesser penalties applied to specified record-keeping and
prescription offences and failures to report cannabis or poppy crops.

## Penalties hardened and the crop exception disappeared

The [1967 amendment](/history/iraq-drug-penalties-1967/) replaced that general
penalty with separate rules. Personal possession or growing brought one to
five years in prison and a fine, while suppliers faced hard labour. Giving
drugs away was included among the supply offences.

In [1979](/history/iraq-drug-treatment-hemp-reform-1979/), Iraq removed the
industrial-crop exception altogether. The law continued to shape
[Iraq's cannabis controls](/country/iq/) through repeated amendments until
its [replacement in 2017](/history/iraq-narcotics-law-2017/).
