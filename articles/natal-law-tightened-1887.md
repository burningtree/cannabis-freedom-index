Natal’s Indian Immigrants Commission recommended cannabis prohibition in its 1887 report. That recommendation has sometimes been compressed into a claim that an existing ban was tightened. The sources examined here support a proposed exercise of executive power, rather than a verified new rule.

## What the commission sought

The inquiry ran from 1885 to 1887 and considered conditions affecting Indian immigrants in Natal. Cannabis occupied a distinct section of its report. Craig Paterson’s thesis reproduces its request that the Governor in Council use section 70 of the [1870 immigration law](/history/south-africa-natal-1870/) to restrict cannabis use, possession and supply among the population covered by that law.

The commission also recalled an earlier request for prohibition by the Medical Officer of Pietermaritzburg Circle. In [1884](/history/natal-prohibition-proposal-1884/), disagreement in the Executive Council had prevented a rule from being issued. The commissioners’ renewed request therefore identifies a continuing campaign for implementation.

## Medical testimony and labour discipline

Thembisa Waetjen places the inquiry within the administration of indentured labour. Doctors associated cannabis with illness, alleged insanity and inability to work; workers regarded as suffering from overuse could be treated in hospital. Those allegations reveal the arguments used by colonial authorities. They do not independently establish the medical explanations advanced at the time.

Paterson also questions how faithfully the commission represented its witnesses. His examination finds that several doctors did not discuss cannabis and that one explicitly opposed prohibition. The report’s presentation of medical agreement should therefore be distinguished from the range of testimony it collected. A [separate entry](/history/natal-wragg-commission-1887/) covers that testimony.

## The missing implementing rule

Paterson concludes that no rule appears to have followed the commission’s recommendation. Waetjen similarly states that no additional controls beyond the 1870 legislation were implemented. These accounts support removing the claim of an enacted tightening in 1887.

The original commission report and government notices were not retrieved in this pass. This event is consequently recorded as a documented recommendation, verified through historical research, rather than a definitive finding that no local restriction could have existed. Establishing a new offence, its commencement and its actual enforcement requires an identifiable legal instrument. None is supplied by the sources reviewed here.
