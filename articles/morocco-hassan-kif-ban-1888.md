In 1888 Sultan Hassan I banned both tobacco and cannabis in Morocco. He acted
by executive order rather than legislation, after seeking the guidance of a
Muslim cleric, and ordered commercial stocks to be burned in public. Farming
in the kif-growing Rif mountains was allowed to continue.[@1]

## Monopolies that paid for the state

Morocco's tobacco monopoly dated to 1844, when the sultan needed money for a
war against France, overturning two centuries of religious and political
disapproval of tobacco. An 1856 agreement with Britain abolished monopolies
on farm produce except "tobacco, and other herbs used for smoking in
pipes", and a French observer identified a kif monopoly by 1860, noting
that it sat uneasily with Islamic prohibitions on intoxicants. By 1876 tobacco and kif were a primary
source of government income: in 1884 they supplied 37 percent of the revenues
of one northern administrative region. Morocco and Tunisia were the only
countries to monopolise cannabis, though several southern European states
used the same device for tobacco. Beyond the revenue, the monopolies were
meant to shape colonial agriculture and limit consumption.[@1]

## Farming for political support

In the 1870s Hassan I used his authority to permit cannabis farming in the
Rif, the home of the Heddawa brotherhood, building political support there.
He believed the monopoly helped limit consumption and valued its enormous
income for the treasury, but he had qualms about the state selling an
intoxicant.[@1]

## A ban that did not last

The 1888 ban broke the monopoly model without ending cultivation in the Rif.
Hassan's son restored the kif and tobacco monopolies in 1896, and European
diplomats later agreed to keep them as French influence grew. Accounts
conventionally dated around 1890 attribute Rif cultivation privileges to
Hassan I, though later scholarship finds no decree behind them. In 1910 the
tobacco monopoly passed to
a state-owned farm, with kif added to its crops in 1914; the protectorate
banned hashish imports in 1922 while leaving kif aside. Duvall dates the full
ban on kif to 1954; the timeline continues through the
[independence ban of 1956](/history/morocco-kif-ban-1956/), which extended
prohibition to both former zones while growing carried on around Ketama. The
[Morocco profile](/country/ma/) follows the later medical and recreational
history.[@1]
