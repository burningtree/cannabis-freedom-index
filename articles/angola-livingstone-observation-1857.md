David Livingstone found Angola full of cannabis and full of contradiction. Travelling in
the 1850s, the explorer noted that under Portuguese rule the use of the plant by a slave
was considered a crime — the Portuguese, he wrote in 1858, held such a belief in its
deleterious effects that a slave's use counted as an offence.[@1,2]

The prohibition was porous to the point of theatre. Another explorer wrote that although
the Portuguese forbade slaves to use it, diamba was sold openly at the market in Luanda
and grown round village huts almost everywhere.[@1] The plant was already woven into
daily life: the peoples of the interior used it widely, and the water-pipe made from a
calabash — earliest documented in Angola in 1832 — carried the custom across the
region.[@1]

Livingstone's note fixes the pattern that still defines Angolan cannabis: formally
forbidden, practically everywhere. A 2009 UN drugs report called cannabis the most common
illicit drug in the country, grown in almost every province — the open market of Luanda,
moved to the shadows but never closed.[@2]
