Public Law 728 was approved on 18 July 1956. Its purpose, in its own words, was "a more effective control of narcotic drugs and marihuana". It did not write a new drug code. It amended the tax code and the Narcotic Drugs Import and Export Act, the two federal instruments then in use, and raised the penalties attached to both.

## Marihuana as a tax offence

Federal law still reached cannabis through the transfer tax of 1937. Section 101 of the Act made it unlawful to acquire marihuana without having paid that tax, or to transport or conceal it. The proof rule was blunt: possession, together with a failure to produce the official order form on demand, "shall be presumptive evidence of guilt".

## The penalty ladder

Section 103 rewrote section 7237 of the Internal Revenue Code. Every term has a mandatory minimum, and each tier allows a fine of up to $20,000 on top.

| Offence | First | Second | Third or later |
|---|---|---|---|
| Offences with no specific penalty, including unlawful acquisition | 2 to 10 years | 5 to 20 years | 10 to 40 years |
| Sale or transfer without the written order form | 5 to 20 years | 10 to 40 years | 10 to 40 years |
| Sale or gift by an adult to a person under 18 | 10 to 40 years | | |
| Smuggling marihuana (section 106) | 5 to 20 years | 10 to 40 years | |

## No way out of the sentence

Subsection (d) is headed "No suspension of sentence; no probation". For any sale offence, and for a second or later offence in the general tier, the sentence could not be suspended, probation could not be granted and the federal parole statute did not apply. A judge who thought the minimum too harsh for the person in front of him had no lawful alternative.

The Act went furthest for heroin. An adult who sold or gave heroin to someone under eighteen faced life or at least ten years, "except that the offender shall suffer death if the jury in its discretion shall so direct".

## Why the index moved

The Boggs Act of 1951 had already introduced mandatory minimums, and the 1956 Act raised them and closed off probation and parole. The index lowers enforcement from 1 to 0 and the total from 9 to 7, the lowest point in the United States series. The other scores were already at the bottom for an illegal drug. The series turns upward in 1970, when the Controlled Substances Act replaced these provisions.
