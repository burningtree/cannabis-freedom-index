On 20 January 1923 the US Army banned marijuana possession around the Panama Canal: Circular No. 5 from the Panama Canal Department headquarters, the first curb on record.[@1] Soldiers had been smoking a "weed" with strange effects since about 1916, and by 1922 the habit was prevalent enough to show up in delinquency cases.[@1]

## A governor's committee investigated for eight months

In March 1925 the Department Commander asked the Canal Zone Governor for a conference of legal, medical and police officers on marijuana traffic.[@1] On 1 April the Governor appointed a committee: the Chief Health Officer, the District Attorney, the civil-affairs and police chiefs, the Judge Advocate, the laboratory chief, the superintendent of the Corozal Hospital for the Insane, and a Navy medical adviser.[@1]

From April to December 1925 the committee held hearings, visited Fort Davis and Corozal, watched soldiers smoke, and had four physicians and two policemen smoke in front of them.[@1] It reviewed courts-martial records and found marijuana behind only a tiny fraction of violent or insubordinate cases.[@1] Its conclusion: there was no evidence the local plant was habit-forming like alcohol, opium or cocaine, or that it did users appreciable harm.[@1] It recommended the Canal Zone authorities take no steps against sale or use and ask for no special legislation.[@1]

## The ban came off, then a sailor went to jail

The 1923 circular was rescinded on 29 January 1926.[@1] Ten months later a Panamanian judge sentenced an American seaman to a year in prison for possessing and smoking cannabis-indica cigarettes in the Zone.[@2] That case produced an official experiment on seventeen subjects, which found only minor effects.[@2]

In December 1928 Panama repealed its own law forbidding marijuana possession and use.[@1] A further year-long Army study reported in June 1929 that use was not widespread and discipline barely affected, seeing no reason to revive the penalties.[@1] In January 1930 commanders were told possession or use was not per se a military offence.[@1]

## The argument restarted within a year

By December 1930 the Department Commander had reversed course: smoking was declared efficiency-impairing and forbidden, with trial for every offence.[@1] In May 1931 he asked the Governor to reinvestigate, aiming at regulations against cultivation, possession and sale on the Zone — an admission that growing was still not covered.[@1] A new committee hospitalised thirty-four soldier smokers at Gorgas Hospital for observation, estimating one in five men at Fort Clayton used the drug.[@1]

The plant already had local names: 1935 court records call it canyac, identified by witnesses as cannabis indica or sativa.[@3] A century after Circular No. 5, Panama became the first Central American country to legalise medical use, with the [2021 medical law](/history/panama-medical-2021/).[@2]
