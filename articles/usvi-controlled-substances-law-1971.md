The US Virgin Islands brought a replacement controlled-drugs law into force on 23 March 1971. Cannabis went into Schedule I, but the law treated it as non-narcotic when determining penalties. That distinction would matter in later prosecutions, alongside a separate route allowing eligible first offenders to avoid a possession conviction.[@1,3]

## A new system kept cannabis controlled

The law repealed the earlier drug chapter and introduced schedules identifying controlled substances. The territory’s Supreme Court later explained that cannabis had remained on the original Schedule I list, finding no evidence before it that the list had been changed or republished.[@1]

The legislature’s stated purpose was to create a comprehensive drug-control law and strengthen enforcement. Its findings recognised legitimate medical uses of many controlled substances while describing illegal importation, manufacture, distribution and possession as harmful to public health and welfare.[@2]

## First offenders could leave without a conviction

The new law included a special procedure for eligible first-time possession defendants. With their consent, a judge could defer proceedings and impose up to a year of probation without entering a judgment of guilt. Successful completion required discharge and dismissal; a violation could lead to conviction and sentencing.[@3]

That protection became central to [Lee J. Rohn’s later appeal](/history/usvi-first-offender-driving-reversal-2012/). A trial judge had tried to combine probation without conviction with a two-year driving ban. In 2012, the Supreme Court held that the licence penalty did not apply to people receiving this first-offender treatment.[@3]

## Different offences followed different rules

The legislation also punished attempts and conspiracies. In [Bigby’s cannabis case](/history/usvi-drug-conspiracy-overt-act-2000/), an appeal court ruled that prosecutors had to charge an agreement and an act taken to carry it out. It overturned his conviction and ordered a new trial because the charge alleged only the agreement.[@4]

A separate [1970 prison law](/history/usvi-prison-cannabis-penalty-1970/) used its own definition of narcotic drugs, expressly including cannabis. The Supreme Court later held that the 1971 replacement had left this prison offence intact.[@1]

## Later cases showed the stakes

In 2011, the Superior Court described ordinary cannabis possession as carrying up to a year in prison and a $5,000 fine, while possession with intent to distribute could bring five years and $15,000. First-offender probation sat alongside those criminal penalties.[@2]

These cases fill part of the [territory’s history](/country/vi/#history) between its earlier cannabis-tax regime and the later reforms.
