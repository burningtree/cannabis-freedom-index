For most of a century, kif in [Morocco](/country/ma/) was not a contraband drug but a state product.
Chopped cannabis mixed with a little tobacco and smoked in a small pipe, the sebsi, it was grown under
licence and sold in tobacco shops. Independence in 1956 brought that to an end, at least on paper.

## A monopoly with qualms

The geographer Chris Duvall traces a monopoly on kif back to at least 1860, and by 1884 the tobacco and
kif monopolies brought in 37 per cent of the revenue of one northern region. Sultan Hassan I let the
Rif grow cannabis to win support there, but in [1888](/history/morocco-hassan-kif-ban-1888/), after
consulting a cleric, he banned both tobacco and kif and had stocks burned in public. His son
[restored the monopolies](/history/morocco-monopolies-restored-1896/) in 1896.

After the Algeciras Conference of 1906 the trade passed to the Régie marocaine des kifs et tabacs, a
Tangier company largely in French hands. Farmers needed its permission to grow and had to deliver their
harvest to its factories. When the country was split into French and Spanish protectorates in 1912, the
growing area of the Rif fell to Spain.

## Policing a legal drug

Even while kif was legal, the monopoly fought smugglers. In
[1949](/history/morocco-kif-seizures-1949/) agents in the French zone seized more than 7.6 tonnes of
kif sold outside it, mostly near the Spanish border. A UN study of 1951 found that many townspeople,
artisans and small shopkeepers among them, smoked a few pipes in the evening over coffee or tea. After
France's ban, seizures reached 30.7 tonnes in 1955, and even medical use was prohibited.

## Independence

When Morocco became independent in 1956 and joined the existing drug conventions, the prohibition was
extended to the former Spanish zone. The ban on growing helped provoke an uprising in the Rif. After
putting it down, King Mohammed V condoned cultivation in the five historical villages around Ketama, the
area Hassan I had licensed. The Transnational Institute estimates that close to a million Moroccans
smoked kif at the time, about 8 per cent of the population.

Morocco's [1974 law](/history/morocco-narcotics-law-1974/) punished use as well as trade, but the
fields stayed. From the late 1970s the Rif turned from kif to hashish, and in the 1980s growing expanded
fast to meet demand from Europe. In [2021](/history/morocco-law-13-21-2021/) the country legalised
growing cannabis for medical and industrial use.
