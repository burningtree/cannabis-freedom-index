Liberia's new drug law took effect on 21 October 2014, making personal possession of Schedule I drugs a felony while allowing courts to send people charged with use into treatment. It replaced criminal rules in the public-health law and separated use, possession, cultivation and trafficking into distinct offences.

## Drug officers had struggled to turn cases into convictions

A [regional assessment published in 2011](/history/liberia-drug-enforcement-assessment-2011/) described an agency without a forensic laboratory or officers at entry and exit ports. It reported that most investigated possession and trafficking cases ended with on-the-spot fines, and only one drug conviction had been recorded in 2010.

The assessors called for a stronger anti-trafficking law. The existing [public-health rules](/history/liberia-public-health-drug-controls-1976/) made unauthorised possession a second-degree misdemeanor and growing a first-degree felony.

## Personal possession became a felony

The new law distinguished possession intended for trafficking from possession otherwise. For Schedule I drugs, those were first- and second-degree felonies respectively.

That raised personal possession from a misdemeanor, ordinarily carrying up to thirty days, to a felony ordinarily carrying up to five years under the Penal Law. Cultivation moved in the other direction for people growing for themselves: the earlier first-degree felony became a second-degree felony when the purpose was outside trafficking.

Passing drugs to someone else could still bring trafficking charges. The definition included giving, providing and transferring them, so a payment was unnecessary.

## Courts could offer treatment for use

Use had its own offence. First-time Schedule I use was a first-degree misdemeanor, ordinarily carrying up to one year. A second apprehension raised it to a third-degree felony, with an ordinary maximum of three years.

The court could order treatment, education, aftercare or rehabilitation as an alternative to conviction or punishment. That discretion appeared in a separate paragraph applying to the use offence generally.

The law also provided juvenile procedures and referral to social welfare for children under fourteen. Aggravating circumstances, including violence, organised crime or exploiting minors, could support much longer sentences.

## The next reform tightened custody and bail

The [2023 replacement](/history/liberia-non-bailable-2023/) kept the distinction between use and possession but specified four months to one year for first-time Schedule I use. It raised repeat use to a second-degree felony and made first-degree trafficking offences non-bailable.

For [people using cannabis in Liberia](/country/lr/), the charge selected therefore mattered throughout these reforms: use, holding a personal supply and giving cannabis away each carried different legal consequences.
