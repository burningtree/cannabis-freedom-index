In 1970, East German authorities recorded 328 drug seizures weighing 142 kilograms, of which 141 kilograms was hashish. Historian Ned Richardson-Little uses these records to explain how a state with very limited domestic cannabis availability became involved in an international trade serving customers elsewhere.

Most of the intercepted traffic was heading towards West Berlin. The figures are border-enforcement records, not evidence that East Germans consumed the quantities seized.

## A city surrounded by another state

West Berlin's position created an unusual geography. Suppliers seeking its customers could cross East German territory even when they had little reason to sell within the GDR itself.

Richardson-Little argues that East Germany's currency and restrictions on international movement limited its attraction as a domestic market. Those conditions did not remove demand in the Western city surrounded by its territory.

## More than one kind of traveller

The chapter distinguishes people carrying small quantities for themselves or friends, travellers hoping to profit from purchases abroad, and organised trafficking groups. Their activities should not be collapsed into a single picture of either tourism or organised crime.

One documented case involved two West Germans stopped at Hirschberg in 1970 with hashish bought in Kandahar. Larger networks also used Schönefeld airport, whose international flights connected East Berlin with producing regions. These examples describe routes reconstructed from particular records, not the nationality or motives of every passenger.

## Rising interceptions

The chapter records one person caught bringing hashish across the border in 1968 and eight cases in 1969. The larger 1970 total preceded a reported peak of 211 kilograms of hashish seized in 1971.

Transit arrangements in 1971 and 1972 expanded road access between West Germany and West Berlin. Because they came after the initial increase, they cannot by themselves explain the appearance of the 1970 traffic.

Richardson-Little also compares the East German peak with much larger seizures in West Germany. Neither country's detected quantities independently establish the full volume moving through its territory.

## From a foreign problem to legislation

Official propaganda had presented cannabis as a symptom of capitalist decay. Interceptions made it an operational concern for East German authorities even while domestic use remained limited.

The [1973 Narcotic Drugs Act](/history/germany-gdr-narcotic-drugs-act-1973/) followed within that setting. The 1970 entry records a shift in the state's encounter with the trade, without assigning a national legal score change to the seizure statistics.
