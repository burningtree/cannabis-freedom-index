Tanganyika consolidated its drug law in 1935. Ordinance No. 28 of that year, dated 12 July, was "an ordinance to consolidate the law regulating the importation, exportation, production, manufacture, sale and use of opium and of certain other dangerous drugs" — published as Chapter 95 of the 1947 revised laws and filed with the United Nations in 1952.[@1]

## Indian hemp, defined twice over

"Indian hemp" means the dried flowering or fruiting tops of the pistillate plant known as cannabis sativa, from which the resin has not been extracted, by whatever name such tops are called.[@1] The definition sits beside the standard convention machinery — raw, prepared and medicinal opium, coca, ecgonine, morphine, diamorphine and the Hague and Geneva conventions named in full.[@1]

## A hemp Part of its own

Cannabis gets Part IV to itself. Section 7 bars importing or exporting any resin from cannabis sativa, and section 8 makes a criminal of anyone who possesses, produces, sells or otherwise deals in that resin or its preparations, who cultivates the plant, or who holds, sells or deals in any part of it outside medicinal preparations — transit through the territory excepted.[@1] Cocaine, morphine and the rest wait for Part V; hemp, like raw opium and coca leaf in their own parts, is handled separately and strictly.[@1]

## Ten years in the High Court

The penalty clause bites: up to twenty thousand shillings or ten years with or without hard labour in the High Court, up to five thousand shillings or twelve months in a subordinate court of the first class, with forfeiture of everything involved and court-ordered destruction in every case.[@1] The [country page](/country/tz/) follows the law from this consolidation to the present Act.
