Australia restricted cannabis imports in November 1926 to licensed doctors
and drug businesses serving medicine and research. Each shipment required its
own customs approval. Exporters faced controls too, as the
government brought cannabis into its existing restrictions on dangerous drugs.

## Cannabis joined an older system of drug controls

The federal government already restricted imports of opium, morphine, cocaine
and heroin. The new rules replaced an earlier proclamation and added cannabis
flowers, extracts and resin to the controlled trade.

This followed the [1925 Geneva Convention](/history/geneva-opium-convention-1925/),
which brought cannabis into international drug controls. A later Australian
working party on medical cannabis traced the federal decision to that wider
treaty movement, followed by state laws prohibiting unauthorised use.

Governor-General Lord Stonehaven issued the proclamations with the advice of
the Federal Executive Council. Trade and Customs Minister H. E. Pratten signed
them for the government.

## Importers had to account for every shipment

Customs could license qualified medical practitioners, pharmaceutical chemists
and people lawfully operating wholesale or manufacturing drug businesses.
Licences lasted a year and could be renewed, but a separate authorisation was
needed for every importation.

The allowed purpose was explicit: “for medical or scientific purposes only”.
Applicants had to give security that imported supplies would go to those uses
and keep books showing how they disposed of them and to whom. Customs officers
could demand the books and the remaining stock for inspection.

Importers also had to promise to make reasonable inquiries about the purpose
and destination of goods they later sold. Approval therefore carried continuing
responsibility for where the imported cannabis went.

## Flowers, medicines and hashish came under the rules

The proclamation defined Indian hemp as the dried flowering or fruiting tops
of the female cannabis plant with the resin still present. It separately
listed cannabis extract and tincture, a liquid preparation, alongside resin
and products based on it, including hashish.

The accompanying export rules required the minister's written consent.
Exporters also needed a certificate from the destination country's authorities
confirming that the quantity ordered served legitimate business and medical
or scientific purposes.

## State restrictions developed alongside federal controls

Australians had already encountered local controls on cannabis sales:
[Western Australia listed it as a poison in 1899](/history/australia-western-australia-cannabis-poison-schedule-1899/),
bringing covered transactions under pharmacy rules.

After the federal import restrictions,
[state prohibitions spread](/history/australia-state-bans-1928/).
[Australia's cannabis laws](/country/au/) developed through these overlapping
decisions: customs officials controlled overseas supplies, while state
governments set further limits on cannabis within their borders.
