For nine years the law of Nigeria prescribed death for growing cannabis. In 1975 the
military government that had written the penalty took it out.

## The law it amended

The [Indian Hemp Decree of 1966](/history/nigeria-indian-hemp-decree-1966/) had been
issued months after the first coup, as part of a campaign against indiscipline. It set
death for cultivation, twenty-one years for importation and heavy penalties for
possession and use.

## Why it changed

The decree had been overtaken by events. The civil war of 1967 to 1970 drew attention
elsewhere and spread cannabis use among soldiers, the group the law's authors had been
most anxious about. In the years that followed, growing and smoking became more common
for economic and social reasons, and public attitudes shifted with them.

In 1975 the decree was amended and its penalties reduced, in the words of one
historian, "to bring government policy more in line with the realities of cannabis use
and cultivation and popular attitudes toward it". Capital punishment for cultivation
was dropped and the prison terms came down.

## What did not change

The approach. The 1966 decree, the colonial Dangerous Drugs Ordinance and the
military's idea of drug control as social discipline remained the basis of Nigerian
policy. A decree in 1984 raised the penalties again.

The pattern has repeated since. In 2024 the Senate
[voted for the death penalty](/history/nigeria-senate-death-penalty-vote-2024/) for drug
trafficking, and in 2025 the president
[declined to assent](/history/nigeria-ndlea-bill-assent-declined-2025/).

## In the index

The index lists the 1975 amendment for context. Removing a death penalty for growers
matters a great deal to growers, but possession and use stayed crimes with prison, so
[Nigeria's](/country/ng/) scores for ordinary users did not move.
