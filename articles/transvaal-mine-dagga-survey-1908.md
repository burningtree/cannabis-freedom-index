A Transvaal inquiry in 1908 found little support among responding mine managers and officials for banning dagga, the local name for cannabis.[@1] Many described moderate use and worried that restrictions would make it harder to recruit African men to the gold mines.[@1]

## Officials asked whether smoking harmed the workforce

The Government Native Labour Bureau questioned mine inspectors and pass officers about how much cannabis was used, by whom and with what effects.[@1] The survey came as colonial officials were considering whether to follow restrictions imposed elsewhere.[@1]

The neighbouring [Orange River Colony](/history/orange-river-colony-dagga-ordinance-1903/) had prohibited supplying and receiving cannabis.[@1] In the Transvaal, however, decisions about drugs were closely tied to labour fitness and control in the mining economy.[@1]

The questions reflected that concern: officials wanted to know whether dagga made workers incapable of doing their jobs and whether prohibition would help.[@1]

## Recruitment mattered as much as intoxication

Responses varied in their estimates of how widely cannabis was used, but generally described it as moderate and unproblematic.[@1] Several respondents saw no need for prohibitory legislation.[@1]

Managers warned that adding restrictions to workers' lives could discourage men from travelling to the Rand.[@1] Some also described cannabis as helping sociability or vitality within the compounds.[@1]

Waetjen interprets this tolerance within a system concerned with keeping workers available and manageable.[@1] It grew from the interests of mine administration, alongside the practices and preferences of the workers themselves.[@1]

## Workers' health appeared in the replies

Some officials reported that miners smoked cannabis to bring on coughing, believing this helped clear inhaled dust from their lungs.[@1] Those accounts reveal contemporary ideas about relief from a dangerous working environment.[@1]

Other replies proposed restrictions on young smokers, and one linked poor work performance to combining cannabis with liquor.[@1] The survey therefore contained qualifications and disagreements within its overall resistance to prohibition.[@1]

## The findings did not settle the wider debate

No action was advised on the basis of the 1908 research.[@1] A [second inquiry in 1911](/history/transvaal-second-dagga-survey-1911/) extended to town magistrates and again found that most responding officials did not favour a total prohibition.[@1]

National controls nevertheless followed in [1922](/history/south-africa-national-ban-1922/).[@1] In [South Africa](/country/za/), the mine surveys preserved evidence of a substantial administrative constituency that had seen a ban as unnecessary or harmful to its own labour interests.[@1]
