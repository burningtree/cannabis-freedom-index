In 1908 the government of the Transvaal asked the managers of its gold mines whether
dagga should be banned. Their answer was that it did no harm, and that a ban would
cost them their workforce.

## Why the question was asked

The gold mines of the Witwatersrand depended on African migrant labour, and the
authorities watched closely anything that might affect how fit and how controllable
that labour was. Liquor prohibitions had been in place since 1896. A neighbouring
colony had [banned the supply of dagga](/history/orange-river-colony-dagga-ordinance-1903/)
in 1903, and the Transvaal's native affairs commissioner wanted the same, saying it
made men "intoxicated, useless, and sometimes uncontrollably violent".

## The survey

The Government Native Labour Bureau put a set of questions to mine inspectors and pass
officers. How much dagga was smoked, and by whom? What "evil effects" had they seen?
Would prohibition be beneficial?

## The answers

Respondents generally agreed that use was moderate and caused no trouble.

- One manager described "slight" usage with little negative effect and saw no benefit
  in prohibition.
- One said dagga hurt work only when combined with liquor.
- One thought a man who smoked was "equally as good" a worker as one who did not.
- Several recommended only that small boys be kept from it.

Some went further and credited it with keeping workers lively and sociable.

The decisive argument was recruitment. Putting workers "under innumerable restrictions
and depriving them of anything they may have been accustomed to", one report said,
"would deter natives from coming to the Rand".

No action was advised.

## The second round

In [1911](/history/transvaal-second-dagga-survey-1911/) the survey was repeated and
extended to town magistrates, while a Sunday newspaper ran a piece headed "Drugged and
Dazed", warning that dagga smokers endangered white women. Again no respondent shared
the alarm. Miners, it emerged, were using cannabis as medicine for lung disease.

## Why it did not matter

The evidence of the people who employed the largest number of dagga smokers in the
country was on file. The [national ban](/history/south-africa-national-ban-1922/) of
1922 was passed anyway, driven by doctors and farmers in the Cape and by a press
campaign linking dagga to crime.
