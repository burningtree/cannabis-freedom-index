Penang and Malacca were covered by the Straits Settlements’ excise legislation, whose 1870 ordinance regulated bhang through a licensed monopoly. The statutory text distinguishes authorized commerce from prohibited supplies, a distinction lost when the arrangement is described simply as cannabis prohibition.

## The territory and the source

This event concerns the settlements within the colony. It does not establish the legal position in every Malay state, and it should not be read as a nationwide Malaysian enactment.

The source consulted is John Augustus Harwood’s 1886 official compilation, which prints Ordinance No. IV of 1870 with the date 15 July. It incorporates amendments made after 1870. The date attached to this entry is the ordinance date; a separate commencement notice was not retrieved.

## A concession to prepare and sell bhang

The preamble included bhang among the articles from which excise revenue would be collected. Sections 1–2 provided for exclusive licensed rights to prepare and sell toddy and bhang in each settlement, assigning the concession the title Toddy Farmer.

The word farmer describes the revenue arrangement. The law’s wording does not establish cannabis cultivation by concession holders. Its definition of bhang covered intoxicating preparations from Indian hemp and hemp used for smoking, chewing or eating.

## Routing trade through the concession

Section 47 limited preparation and manufacture to the farmer and controlled imports and exports. An importer supplying the concession holder could bring bhang into the settlement, but had to take it directly to the farmer’s principal shop. The Governor could fix the purchase price, and refusal to buy required the importer to export the goods.

Section 48 targeted unauthorized dealings and knowing possession outside the authorized supply system. Proof of the qualifying purchase rested on the person charged, and the text provided for penalties and forfeiture.

## Shops, fees and inspection

The retail provisions show that the framework allowed licensed sales. Under section 53, a licensing officer could authorize farm shops for a year or a shorter period, with official control of their number and location.

The following sections required displayed rules in English, Malay, Chinese and Tamil, an annual twelve-dollar licence fee and additional signage charges. They also authorized inspection of shops, books and stocks.

These are substantial controls on commerce, but they do not support a claim that every cannabis transaction or possession was forbidden. Nor does the statute measure actual availability.
