Kenya's colonial drug law made growing cannabis, possessing the plant outside its medicinal preparations, and dealing in it criminal offences.[@1] It began operating on 1 February 1933.[@2] Depending on the court, an offence could bring up to one year or ten years in prison, a fine, or both.[@1]

## Cannabis prosecutions already preceded the ordinance

The new law replaced the earlier Abuse of Opiates Prevention Ordinance and its amendments.[@1] That earlier system had already produced cannabis prosecutions: Samson Ndanyi's archival study describes two Nakuru defendants jailed in 1930 for bhang and smoking pipes, with different terms and a fine in one case.[@3]

The 1932 ordinance received the Governor's assent in December, but its opening clause left commencement to a later proclamation.[@1] The government text subsequently communicated to the United Nations records February 1933 as the start.[@2]

## The plant and its medicines followed different rules

The cannabis provisions prohibited cultivation and made possessing or dealing in the plant an offence, while excluding medicinal preparations from those plant clauses.[@1] Resin production, dealing and possession were separately covered.[@1]

Extracts and tinctures of Indian hemp appeared in another part of the ordinance, alongside other controlled medicines.[@1] That part required licences for import and export and authorised rules governing manufacture, sale, distribution and possession.[@1] It also provided for rules on medical prescriptions and the dispensing of prescribed drugs.[@1]

Qualified poison retailers could be authorised to prepare and dispense these medicines in the ordinary course of their business, subject to the statutory rules and possible withdrawal of their authority.[@1] The law therefore combined criminal restrictions on cannabis with controls over its medicinal preparations.[@1]

## The court determined the available maximum

A conviction in a first- or second-class subordinate court could bring up to one year in prison, a fine up to £250, or both.[@1] In the colonial Supreme Court, the maximum rose to ten years or £1,000, or both.[@1]

The law also required forfeiture of the articles involved in the offence, which the court could order destroyed or otherwise disposed of.[@1] A narrower protection applied to inadvertent bookkeeping or prescription breaches unconnected with another offence: those cases retained a fine alternative.[@1]

## A new law replaced the colonial statute in 1994

The later [1994 narcotics Act](/history/kenya-narcotics-act-1994/) expressly repealed the Dangerous Drugs Act.[@4] It introduced separate penalties for cannabis possession, use, trafficking and growing, becoming the basis for subsequent amendments in [Kenya](/country/ke/).[@4]
