Argentina's Law 11,331 received legislative sanction on 16 July 1926 and was
published in the official gazette on 13 August. It added a possession offence
to the earlier drug legislation, extending punishment beyond the acts of supply
already regulated.

The change is an important part of the country's drug-law history. Its place in
cannabis history requires a further distinction: the amendment referred back to
the drugs covered by the earlier law, rather than naming cannabis itself.

## A clause about possession and justification

The official legislative register reproduces the added clause. It concerned
people not authorised to sell the covered drugs who held them and could not
justify a legitimate reason for possession or custody.

The qualification about legitimate possession belongs in the account of the
rule. So does its link to the earlier legislation: the amendment created a new
basis for punishment without supplying a fresh list of named substances.

The verified July date is legislative sanction. Publication in August is a
separate recorded step, and neither should silently be substituted for the other.

## From supply controls to consumers

R. Alejandro Corda's historical study describes the
[1924 law](/history/argentina-first-narcotics-law-1924/) as a Penal Code reform
addressing narcotics and alkaloids, including unauthorised supply and problems
with prescription-based distribution.

His account links the 1926 amendment to dissatisfaction with the ability to act
against people holding drugs outside the authorised trade. The resulting
possession offence did not distinguish traffickers from consumers. That helps
explain why the amendment matters even though its wording was not a dedicated
cannabis provision.

## Cannabis coverage was disputed

Corda notes that some interpretations did not regard cannabis as covered by
the early criminal-law terminology. He also identifies earlier administrative
measures that named Indian hemp, showing why administrative regulation and the
scope of a criminal offence cannot simply be treated as interchangeable.

The historical question is therefore more precise than whether Argentina had
any drug law in 1926. It concerns which substances a particular offence reached
and what contemporary authorities understood its terms to mean.

## Later laws made cannabis explicit

The [1968 narcotics framework](/history/argentina-narcotics-regulatory-law-1968/)
and [1977 cultivation law](/history/argentina-cannabis-cultivation-law-1977/)
provide separate milestones. The latter expressly named Cannabis sativa and
prohibited specified cultivation and dealings involving cannabis products.

For [Argentina's timeline](/country/ar/), retaining those distinctions avoids
turning a general possession amendment into an unsupported claim that every
form of cannabis activity first became unlawful on a single day in 1926.
