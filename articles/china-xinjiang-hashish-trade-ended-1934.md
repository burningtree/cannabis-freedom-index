Until 1934 hashish was a legal export of China. It left Xinjiang by the tens of
thousands of kilograms a year, bound for British India, and paid a tariff on the way — part of a legal, high-value drug trade through Ladakh that the British carefully regulated and taxed.[@1,2]

## The trade

Xinjiang, in China's far west, was a major producer of hashish in the nineteenth
century, with Yarkand as its centre. The market was India, where charas, as the resin
was called, was one of the three cannabis drugs the British taxed.

The trade was lawful at both ends. It crossed the border openly and was subject to a
tariff.

## The end

In 1934 the Chinese authorities cut off the legal trade. The source read for this
article does not say who took the decision or why. Smuggling continued for some years
afterwards.

The timing fits what was happening elsewhere. Cannabis had come under international
control at [Geneva in 1925](/history/geneva-opium-convention-1925/), with a ban on
exporting resin to countries that prohibited it, and in India the provinces were
tightening their rules on charas.

## Cannabis in China since

China made possessing or using marijuana illegal in 1985, when it joined the UN
convention on psychotropic substances. A user faces detention of 10 to 15 days and a
fine of up to 2,000 yuan under the public security law.

Hemp is another matter. Growing cannabis for industrial purposes has never been
prohibited in China, and cannabis seed has been listed continuously in the Chinese
pharmacopoeia.
