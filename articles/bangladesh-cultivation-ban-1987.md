In 1987 the [Bangladesh](/country/bd/) government banned the cultivation of cannabis.[@1,2] Two years later it [banned the sale](/history/bangladesh-ban-1989/) of the crop, and from January 1990 the [Narcotics Control Act](/history/bangladesh-narcotics-control-act-1990/) made the trade illegal outright.[@3] Together they closed a licensed industry that had supplied [ganja](/culture/language/ganja/) from Naogaon for more than a century.[@1,2]

## A taxed crop

Under British rule ganja was taxed, not prohibited.[@2] From 1854 the colonial government confined commercial growing to a tract around Naogaon known as the Ganja Mahal; from 1876 it controlled the licensing of growers and sellers.[@2] In 1917 the growers formed the [Naogaon Ganja Cultivators' Co-operative Society](/history/bangladesh-ganja-society-1917/), which came to represent about 7,000 farmers working some 10,000 hectares.[@1] From 1918 to 1947 its harvest averaged about 55,000 maunds a year, and the society's funds paid for schools, dispensaries, mosques, a temple and a bridge.[@1]

The trade survived partition and independence.[@1,2] After 1971, Bangladesh signed an international anti-narcotics agreement in 1974; the government attributed the 1987 ban to the commitment it made.[@1,2] Licensed cultivation ceased that year.[@2]

## The order

The 1987 decision ended cultivation; the 1989 decision ended sales.[@1,3] The 1990 Narcotics Control Act then repealed the colonial excise and dangerous-drugs laws and prohibited the cultivation, production, sale, possession and use of narcotics other than alcohol.[@3] For Naogaon the effect was immediate: about 50,000 people from 7,000 families lost their work, and many turned to rice, mustard and vegetables.[@1] The government sealed the cooperative's warehouses, which still held more than 226 maunds of ganja.[@1]

## What was lost

The ban ended more than a crop.[@2] Rahman and his co-authors describe a three-circle rotation in which fields grew ganja only every third year, and a "ganja doctor" who removed male plants before they flowered.[@2] Ganja was far more profitable than rice, beans, rapeseed or wheat: 15 rupees a maund against 0.66 to 2 rupees for the other crops.[@2] Production had already fallen from 880 tonnes in 1858 to 232 tonnes in 1897, 170 tonnes in 1947 and 21 tonnes in 1951; the 1987 order ended what remained.[@2] The knowledge of the rotation and processing is being lost with the last generation of farmers who worked under the society.[@2]

## A ban completed

Cannabis did not disappear: the Brac University study records that demand and cultivation continued after the ban.[@3] The penalties also grew.[@4,5] The [2018 Narcotics Control Act](/history/bangladesh-narcotics-control-act-2018/) sets six months to five years for up to 5 kg of herbal cannabis and one to five years for up to 50 plants.[@4] In July 2026 a [further amendment](/history/bangladesh-digital-drug-offence-2026/) created an offence of buying, selling or communicating about drugs online that can be punished with any term of imprisonment or death.[@5]

The four sealed warehouses at the narcotics department's Naogaon office still hold more than 226 maunds, the last stock of the licensed trade.[@1]
