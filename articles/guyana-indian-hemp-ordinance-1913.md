In 1913 British Guiana stopped treating imported cannabis as ordinary cargo. Under the Indian Hemp Ordinance every consignment had to sit in a colonial bonded warehouse and leave only with a trader who had paid the British a licence fee — taxation and control on the Bengal model, not prohibition.[@1] Trinidad copied the system wholesale in its Ganja Ordinance of 1915.[@1]

## A licence system, not a ban

The colony had regulated the trade for decades: an [1861 ordinance](/history/guyana-licensing-1861/) had already licensed the sale of ganja and bhang, introduced before cannabis counted as a significant local problem.[@2] The 1913 law updated the machinery rather than reversing it. Its template came from India, where the Indian Hemp Drugs Commission had recommended Bengal's bonded-warehouse administration — and across the empire the British were, as the history puts it, equally concerned to have "a slice of the profits of the drug trade" by taxing it heavily and channelling its supply and distribution.[@1]

The colony had form for early action: the 1861 licensing ordinance is read as anticipatory control, imposed before cannabis counted as a significant local problem.[@2]

## Aimed at Indian habits

Like its Caribbean counterparts, the law was instituted to control the habits of Indians: some 8,900 indentured migrants had arrived in British Guiana between 1838 and 1918, with a further 144,000 in Trinidad, brought in to replace formerly enslaved Africans for the sugar planters, and they had carried the ganja complex of techniques, vocabulary and justifications with them.[@2] The warehouse walls were aimed at their supply lines, while the licence fees turned the trade into revenue.[@1,2]

## The model spreads

Trinidad's 1915 copy made the warehouse-and-licence the West Indian standard, explicitly following Guiana's initiative in updating ganja-trade controls.[@1] The colony kept the machinery but folded it into the modern framework: a Dangerous Drugs Ordinance, No. 10 of 1937, followed, and a 1948 order brought the new synthetics — amidone, desomorphine, metopon, pethidine — plus any non-external preparation made from Indian-hemp extract or tincture under its Part IV.[@3] The licensing lineage runs through [Guyana](/country/gy/) law to the present, where [possession of small amounts no longer means jail](/history/guyana-no-jail-2022/).
