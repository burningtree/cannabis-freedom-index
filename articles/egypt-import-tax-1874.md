Six years after banning cannabis, Egypt put a tax on importing it. The two rules stood
side by side, and that says most of what needs saying about the first Egyptian
prohibition.

## The contradiction

In [1868](/history/egypt-first-ban-1868/) the growing, use and import of cannabis had
all been forbidden. In 1874 a tax was imposed on cannabis imports, although possessing
the drug remained illegal.[@1]

A government that taxes a thing expects it to arrive. The tax was an admission that the
ban had not stopped the trade, and a decision to take a share of it.

## The same logic, ten years on

The pattern repeated. When cultivation became a criminal offence in
[1884](/history/egypt-cultivation-ban-1884/), customs officers were allowed to sell the
hashish they seized abroad, instead of destroying it, to pay informers and the officers
who had made the seizures. Enforcement was financed by the drug it was meant to
eliminate.

## The alternative that was not taken

In [1892](/history/egypt-caillard-regulation-proposal-1892/) the British head of
Egyptian customs reported that prohibition had produced trafficking and that hashish
was too widely used to stamp out. He proposed the system Britain ran in India: register
it, license it, tax it openly. A law was drafted and never put into effect.

Bengal’s Board [recommended taxation in 1798](/history/india-bhang-tax-1798/)
rather than total prohibition. The commission’s later account reproduces that advice;
it does not establish when a particular tax began.[@2] Egypt combined prohibition
with an import tax, and later asked the rest of the world to adopt its prohibition
policy at [Geneva in 1925](/history/geneva-opium-convention-1925/).
