Palau enacted a wide-ranging anti-drug law in May 2026. RPPL 12-5 expands testing,
links a positive test to possession and brings drug impairment into the driving
law, while also directing money to rehabilitation.

## From bill to law

The legislature passed the measure in April. President Surangel Whipps Jr. signed
it on 7 May 2026, after Congress returned a version he described as much stricter
than the original proposal.

The reporting covers illegal drugs generally and focuses heavily on
methamphetamine. It names marijuana as one of the substances covered by the
driving rule, but does not identify a new marijuana-specific quantity or penalty.

## Testing becomes central

Designated employees must undergo drug testing at least twice a year. The groups
listed include police, customs and immigration personnel, health workers, and
operators of buses, boats and heavy equipment. Applicants must pass a test before
being hired.

A positive test can lead to immediate dismissal and referral to prosecutors,
subject to limited exceptions such as a valid prescription. Foreign workers and
their family members also face medical screening, including a drug test, soon
after arrival. A result showing illegal drug use can lead immigration officials to
review whether they may remain in Palau.

The law treats illegal drugs found in a person's blood or urine as proof of
possession of a small amount. A June report on a separate traffic-stop prosecution
described this as one of RPPL 12-5's strengthened enforcement rules.

## Driving and possession

Driving under the influence was expanded beyond alcohol. A driver under the
influence of marijuana, methamphetamine, cocaine or an illegal opioid can now face
a felony possession charge. Drivers are treated as having consented to drug-related
sobriety tests as well as alcohol testing.

The measure also increases penalties for serious drug crimes. The sources read do

## Enforcement and treatment

RPPL 12-5 formalises drug-enforcement and rehabilitation funds. Seized money and
property are to be divided equally between enforcement and rehabilitation, while
part of collected fines also supports treatment.
