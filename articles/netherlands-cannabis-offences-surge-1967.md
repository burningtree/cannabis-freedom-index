In 1967, cannabis accounted for 76 per cent of recorded offences against the Dutch Opium Act. By 1972, its share had reached 86 per cent. Historian Stephen Snelders uses these figures to trace a rapid change in the Netherlands, from a market authorities considered small to one they struggled to contain.

The figures describe recorded offences. They are evidence about the interaction between cannabis activity and policing, rather than a count of everyone who used the drug.

## A changing field of enforcement

National drug-offence figures had been rising since 1964. Most of the subsequent growth concerned cannabis. In the same period, Amsterdam attracted visitors interested in its counterculture and access to drugs, while domestic demand also expanded.

Snelders places this development before the later toleration associated with [coffeeshops](/culture/traditions/the-coffeeshop/). The retail institution emerged within a history of demand and illegal supply, rather than starting that history.

## Seizures and the unseen market

The chapter reports 274 kilograms seized in 1970 and almost 6,000 kilograms in 1973. Those are quantities intercepted, not totals imported or consumed.

Estimating the unseen market required another step. Dutch law enforcement commonly assumed it caught one-tenth of smuggled goods. Snelders traces this assumption to earlier border policing and calls the proportion arbitrary. Multiplying seizures by ten could produce a model, but could not independently verify the size of the trade.

The chapter's calculations also depend on assumed numbers of regular users and quantities consumed. These are useful questions for historical reconstruction, provided the resulting estimates remain distinct from observations.

## The festival estimate

Undercover Rotterdam detectives estimated that 70 to 80 per cent of more than 60,000 visitors to the Kralingen pop festival in June 1970 smoked cannabis. Snelders uses the account to illustrate its place in youth culture.

That was a police assessment of a particular crowd, not a representative survey of the Netherlands. The chapter also records widely varying estimates of regular and occasional use, with marked differences between national and urban populations.

## What the figures explain

Together, the records show increasing visibility, enforcement and supply during a period of cultural change. They do not reduce that change to one reliable national prevalence figure.
