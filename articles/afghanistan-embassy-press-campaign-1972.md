On February 12, 1972, the US embassy in Kabul reported using foreign newspaper coverage to encourage Afghan action against narcotics. The confidential telegram, now published in the State Department's historical collection, records an effort to turn concern about Afghanistan's reputation into pressure for domestic enforcement.

The document concerns narcotics generally, including hashish. It is evidence about diplomatic persuasion, rather than a new Afghan law.

## A market attracting attention

Historian James Bradford describes how Western tourism helped connect Afghan hashish producers with new customers. Around [Chicken Street](/culture/traditions/chicken-street/), hotels and sellers served an overland community whose interest in the product was becoming internationally familiar.

Some visitors carried purchases home. Better-financed traffickers subsequently sought much larger supplies, drawing American narcotics agents' attention to connections between Afghanistan and Western markets.

That expansion occurred alongside an older regional trade and established Afghan consumption. Foreign travellers changed the market; they did not introduce cannabis to the country.

## Circulating an alarming picture

The embassy said US and British articles were copied to officials and newspaper editors. It also reported embassy cooperation with one journalist and distribution through a US information bulletin. The telegram treated reputational anxiety as a means of encouraging action.

These are the embassy's descriptions of its own campaign and its effectiveness. They do not independently verify sensational newspaper portrayals of tourists or establish how every recipient responded.

## Competing views of the trade

Bradford's chapter supplies a wider setting for that pressure. Tourists could romanticise Afghanistan as a haven for intoxication, while Afghan critics objected to foreign entitlement. Producers and traders, meanwhile, saw opportunities for income.

The chapter reproduces Afghan cartoons satirising visitors and describes the tension between economic benefits and pressure for stricter drug control. Neither enthusiastic travel narratives nor alarmist foreign reports capture the whole setting.

## Publicity and enforcement

The [1972 enforcement drive](/history/afghanistan-smuggling-crackdown-1972/) and the [1973 cultivation ban](/history/afghanistan-ban-1973/) followed within this changing political environment. The February telegram helps document one mechanism of pressure, without proving that publicity alone caused either policy.

The event records an acknowledged information campaign. It carries no legal score change, and its date identifies the embassy's report rather than the first day articles began circulating.
