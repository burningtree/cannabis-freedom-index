The Straits Settlements’ Excise Ordinance of 1870 placed bhang inside a licensed monopoly system. Its provisions restricted who could prepare, import and sell the drug, while recognizing authorized shops and supplies. That is a more precise description than calling the measure a blanket cannabis ban.

## Reading the statutory compilation

The ordinance is dated 15 July 1870 in John Augustus Harwood’s official 1886 compilation. That edition incorporates later amendments, which its notes identify. The original 1870 session-law printing and a separate commencement notice were not inspected for this article.

The account below therefore describes the ordinance as reproduced in 1886. It does not assume that every provision or definition in the consolidated text was unchanged from the first enactment.

## Exclusive rights and the revenue farmer

Section 1 provided for exclusive rights to prepare and sell toddy and bhang in each settlement. The Governor could license the holders of those rights through public or private sale. Section 2 called the relevant concession holder the Toddy Farmer.

Here, farmer refers to the holder of a revenue concession. It should not be read as proof that the person cultivated cannabis. Section 95 defined bhang as an intoxicating drug obtained from Indian hemp, or hemp itself when used for smoking, chewing or eating.

## Imports and possession outside the system

Section 47 restricted preparation and manufacture to the settlement’s Toddy Farmer. It also restricted imports and exports, while allowing imports intended for sale to that concession holder. Imported supplies had to go to the farmer’s principal shop, at a price that the Governor could fix. If the farmer refused to buy them, the importer had to export them.

Section 48 penalized unauthorized dealings and knowing possession of supplies not purchased from the authorized farmer or shopkeeper. It placed the burden of proving the relevant purchase on the accused. The provisions carried penalties up to one hundred dollars and forfeiture.

## A licensed retail route

Section 53 expressly provided for licensed farm shops selling bhang and other excisable articles. Officials controlled their number and location. Sections 54–56 addressed displayed rules, licence fees and inspection; the annual licence fee was twelve dollars, with an additional charge for identifying signage or marks.

These provisions establish a restrictive permitted market. They do not establish unrestricted access, and the legal text alone cannot show how widely licences were issued or how enforcement worked. The same colonial law also covered [Penang and Malacca](/history/malaysia-straits-ban-1870/). Existing historical scores have been preserved pending a separate reconstruction of their legal basis.
