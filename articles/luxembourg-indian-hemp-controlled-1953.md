Luxembourg put cannabis under a licence regime in late 1953. A grand-ducal order of 25 September 1953, executing the 1922 law on toxic medicines and published in the Mémorial on 3 December, put every substance the World Health Organization recognised as addiction-producing — and every preparation of those substances — under prior authorisation from the health minister for import and export.[@1]

## Nobody holds without a licence

The order's second article is the operative one: apart from open pharmacists, authorised doctors and vets, licensed medicine depots and emergency stocks, nobody could hold, supply or acquire the listed substances and preparations, for payment or free, without the minister's prior authorisation.[@1] Selling or offering them for sale, and manufacturing them, likewise needed ministerial permission; every authorisation was revocable and named the premises where the operations took place.[@1] Holders kept locked strongrooms, day-by-day registers balanced monthly, and poison-skull labels; prescriptions were capped at seven days' supply and could not be renewed.[@1]

## Item 25: Indian hemp

The list itself came a few weeks later. Citing the expert work of the WHO, the health minister's order of 19 October 1953 named the addiction-producing substances under the September order — and item 25 reads "Indian hemp, Indian hemp resin, and Indian hemp extract and tincture".[@2] Only preparations of extract or tincture meant for external use escaped the regime.[@2] From publication, then, holding, buying, selling, giving away, making or moving cannabis in Luxembourg required the minister's authorisation, and only pharmacists could dispense it, on prescription.[@1,2]

## Penalties and lineage

Breaches carried the penalties of the 1922 law as later increased, alongside criminal-code and disciplinary liability.[@1] The 1953 order repealed the 1922, 1923 and 1952 execution orders it replaced, and its preamble shows the post-war lineage: it was made under the 1922 medicines law as amended, in view of the 1949 law approving the 1946 New York protocol that had folded the drug conventions into the United Nations system.[@1] The [country page](/country/lu/) follows the regime from licensing to the later reforms.
