Croatia was subject to a federal ban on growing Indian hemp under Yugoslavia’s 1950 narcotics law. Specified scientific institutions could grow it on their own experimental fields, subject to conditions and quantities fixed by the federal public-health authority. Ordinary growers had no equivalent exception.[@1]

## Drugs became part of the state economic plan

The law put drug cultivation, manufacture, preparation and trade under state control. Indian hemp appeared among controlled raw drugs, with extracts, tinctures and resins listed separately as preparations. Manufacturing and preparing narcotic drugs was limited to medical and scientific purposes through appointed public undertakings.[@1]

Retail supply went through pharmacies, dispensaries and specified veterinary outlets. Authorised businesses had to keep special registers of drugs received and supplied, while imports required authorisation for each consignment.[@1]

## Unlawful trade carried prison terms

Unlawful production for gain and unlawful selling, purchasing or transferring drugs could bring correctional labour or up to two years in prison. Import and export offences had a separate maximum of three years, with higher terms for specified aggravated cases. Drugs involved had to be confiscated.[@1]

The decree was dated 23 January 1950; the act specified that it would enter into force on publication in the federal official gazette.[@1]

## Croatia later separated users from suppliers

The [2013 reform](/history/croatia-misdemeanour-2013/) removed personal drug possession from the criminal code and left it punishable as a misdemeanour. The Justice Ministry described the change as a way to reduce criminal proceedings and the stigma of a criminal record, while keeping criminal penalties for production and supply.[@2]

It also explained why there was no fixed personal-use gram threshold: the purpose of possession had to be assessed from the circumstances, including where a small quantity was intended for resale.[@2]

## Hemp and medical crops gained their own routes

The [2019 amendment](/history/croatia-medical-production-2019/) defined industrial hemp as EU-listed varieties with no more than 0.2% total THC and required growers to register before sowing. It also created a route for legal entities holding the appropriate medicines or active-substance manufacturing licences to grow cannabis for medical purposes, subject to approval.[@3]

These later permissions distinguish industrial crops and authorised medical production from recreational home growing. The [Croatia profile](/country/hr/) explains those routes alongside the penalties users and growers face today.[@2,3]
