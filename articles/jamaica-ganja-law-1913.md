Jamaica's first law against cannabis came in through a side door. In 1913 the colony's Legislative Council
was asked to ratify the Hague Opium Convention, a treaty about opium. Its members used the occasion to ban
growing and importing ganja.

## A worry about "coolies"

Ganja had come to Jamaica with indentured labourers from India. For decades officials barely noticed it.
The lunatic asylum first blamed an admission on "gonja" smoking in its report for 1883–84, and the
Protector of Immigrants mentioned in 1892–93 that Indian labourers were believed to use it. Only a handful
more asylum cases followed.

Concern grew from 1911–12, when the asylum report backed the Council of Evangelical Churches' view that
ganja smoking was spreading among "the natives of the island". A church petition to the Legislative
Council failed. Then, on 10 June 1913, the Daily Gleaner ran an editorial headed "Ganja Smoking as a
Danger to the Natives of this Colony". It claimed that a large share of the murders committed by Indian
labourers came from ganja, and warned that people of African descent might suffer even worse. It asked
why the government did not ban the plant and destroy every patch.

## The debate

When the opium bill came before the council, members turned to ganja.

| Speaker | Position |
|---|---|
| Mr Stedman | Asylum and police reports linked ganja to crime; employers should destroy plants on their estates |
| Mr Evans | He had Indian labourers under him and often had to threaten them over the habit |
| The Colonial Secretary | An Indian inquiry had found ganja one of the least dangerous drugs |
| Mr Corinaldi | Indian labourers were entitled to their own form of smoking, as members were to their cigars |

The council agreed to add ganja. The [Indian Hemp Drugs Commission](/history/india-hemp-drugs-commission-1894/)'s
mild findings and the doubts of British officials made no difference. Rubin and Comitas argue that the
law belonged with other measures of the time against unlawful drilling, obeah and sedition, driven by
planters' fear of the black working population.

## What it did, and what came after

The amended law banned only cultivation and importation, with a fine of up to £100 or up to twelve months
in prison. Possessing and smoking ganja were not yet offences. The penalties rose in steps:

- [1924](/history/jamaica-dangerous-drugs-law-1924/): possession, sale and smoking become crimes;
- [1941](/history/jamaica-mandatory-prison-1941/): prison becomes compulsory;
- [1964](/history/jamaica-mandatory-minimum-1964/): an 18-month minimum for possession.

By the time the authors wrote, sixty years of control had failed to reduce growing or smoking in
[Jamaica](/country/jm/). In 1972 parliament [abolished the mandatory
sentences](/history/jamaica-mandatory-prison-ended-1972/).
