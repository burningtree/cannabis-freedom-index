Growers and people holding cannabis faced criminal offences under Sierra Leone's
colonial drug law, while medicinal preparations followed a separate regulated
route. Adopted in 1926 to implement the Geneva Opium Convention, the law covered
both the Colony and the Protectorate. Its later revised edition shows the system
that growers, traders and chemists faced.

## Diamba was already part of local life

Cannabis had been [described in Sierra Leone in 1851](/history/sierra-leone-cannabis-long-in-use-1851/),
when colonial surgeon R. O. Clarke wrote about diamba cultivation and smoking.
The local plant had a history well before international controls arrived.

Yet knowledge of it was uneven. Historian Chris Duvall recounts how the president
of Sierra Leone's Chamber of Commerce told an inquiry in 1899 that there was
nothing resembling bhang or Indian hemp in the colony. The international names
could obscure a plant already known to local growers and smokers.

## The law reached plants as well as resin

The [1925 Geneva convention](/history/geneva-opium-convention-1925/) provided the
stated reason for the ordinance. Its definition of Indian hemp described dried
flowering or fruiting tops of female cannabis plants whose resin had not been
removed, whatever commercial name they carried.

The revised law's offences reached further. Growing the plant, possessing it or
any part of it, and selling or otherwise dealing in it were offences, with an
exception for medical preparations. Separate rules prohibited importing or
exporting cannabis resin and made producing, holding or dealing in resin offences.
The controls therefore reached farmers, holders and traders along the supply chain.

## Medicines followed pharmacy rules

Medicinal extracts and tinctures came under another part of the law. Their
imports and exports required licences. The governor could regulate manufacture,
possession and distribution, including prescriptions and the records kept by
people making or supplying the drugs.

Qualified sellers of poisons had a route to prepare and dispense these medicines
within their pharmacy businesses. That professional channel sat alongside the
offences covering the plant and its resin.

## Officials eventually named the local drug

In [1933](/history/sierra-leone-diamba-official-report-1933/), an official drug
report identified diamba as Cannabis sativa and described it as the colony's
only known illegally used dangerous drug. The report connected local use with
the vocabulary of international drug control.

The colonial law remained important long afterwards. Sierra Leone's
[2008 drug law](/history/sierra-leone-drugs-control-act-2008/) repealed it,
created a national enforcement agency and gave officers specific powers to
destroy cannabis crops. Control in [Sierra Leone](/country/sl/) had developed
from a colonial treaty law into a national enforcement system.
