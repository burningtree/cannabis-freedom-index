Iran made cannabis illegal in May 1959. The plant had been part of religious and social
life there for centuries, and by one scholar's account the ban has never been enforced
with much conviction.

## Before the ban

Cannabis had its own institutions in Iran. The Safavid-era bang-khaneh, or hashish
house, served it to be inhaled, eaten or drunk. In the early 1900s coffeehouses in
working-class districts would cook, on request, a stew made with cannabis resin, known
as "the stew of unity". Sufis and dervishes used hashish in devotion, and still gather
to do so on one night of Ramadan.

Islamic jurists were ambiguous about it and rarely called it forbidden.

## The modern state

Prohibition came from secular state-building. Reza Shah, who took power in 1925,
regarded intoxicants as backward, but his concern was opium, which he monopolised for
revenue. Under his son, Mohammad Reza, policy hardened. In 1955 the shah approved the
Opium Ban Law, the first systematic prohibition of the poppy.

Four years later, in May 1959, the government added cannabis and its derivatives to the
list of illegal drugs, with the same penalties as for opium. Through the 1960s and
1970s the anti-narcotics effort covered both.

## After the revolution

The Islamic Republic kept the ban and its rhetoric. In practice, the anthropologist
Maziyar Ghiabi found, toleration of cannabis became the norm. Police concentrated on
heroin, methamphetamine and alcohol, and there were few interventions over cannabis.
He describes this as deliberate policy, not neglect.

A survey of senior clerics found most of them willing to accept medical cannabis if
science showed it did good. The law itself punishes growing only when it is done to
produce narcotics.

## Today

Since about 2000 the market has been dominated by gol, "flower", a strong herbal
cannabis grown inside Iran, alongside hashish imported from Afghanistan and Pakistan.
It is sold through Instagram and delivered to the door.

In the index the 1959 decree took [Iran](/country/ir/) from an unregulated state to
prohibition. The score reflects the law, which still provides for flogging, and not
the tolerance described above, which rests on a single study.
