Gambia raised the minimum fine for the general drug-trafficking offence from one million to ten million dalasi in April 2011. That fine accompanied a prison term of at least ten years. The change reached the cannabis quantity rules, including possession of more than ten grams of resin or two kilograms of cannabis unless the accused proved a different purpose.

## A heavy penalty became more expensive

The [2003 drug-control law](/history/gambia-drug-control-act-2003/), as subsequently amended, distinguished ordinary possession from possession for trafficking. Quantity could determine which route a prosecution took.

Above the cannabis thresholds, the law put the burden on the accused to disprove trafficking. The general trafficking clause required both imprisonment and a fine, unlike possession provisions that allowed a fine, prison, or the two together.

The 2011 amendment changed the minimum fine in that trafficking clause to ten million dalasi. It left the existing minimum ten-year prison term in place.

## A separate capital offence concerned cocaine and heroin

The amendment also changed an aggravated trafficking offence introduced the previous year. That separate offence concerned possession of more than 250 grams of cocaine or heroin.

The 2010 text required a death sentence for that offence. In 2011, parliament replaced it with life imprisonment. For cocaine or heroin holdings between 100 and 250 grams, the same amendment raised a separate fine to fifty million dalasi.

Cannabis remained within the general trafficking and possession rules. Its relevant change in this amendment was the increased fine attached to the general trafficking clause.

## The constitution prompted the reversal of capital punishment

Justice Minister Edward Gomez argued in parliament for a law that would deter traffickers. The changes also addressed the constitution’s limits on capital punishment, which required an offence involving violence or the administration of toxic substances resulting in another person’s death.

Parliament passed the changes on 4 April. President Yahya Jammeh assented on 29 April, the date stated in the enacted text; the gazette published it the following month.

For cannabis defendants, the resulting legislation preserved the quantity-based distinction and made the financial consequences of a trafficking conviction substantially heavier.

## First-time possession later gained a separate scale

The government returned to cannabis penalties in [2014](/history/gambia-first-offenders-2014/). Interior Minister Ousman Sonko argued that heavy fines pushed young people into prison when they could not pay.

The later amendment introduced separate bands for first-time cannabis possession, beginning at 0.1–150 grams, with a fine, a prison term, or both available. It added a different sentencing route for those offenders within [Gambia’s drug-control system](/country/gm/).
