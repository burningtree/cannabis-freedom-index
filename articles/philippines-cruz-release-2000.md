The Philippine Supreme Court ordered David Cruz released in January 2000 after he had spent six years and three months in prison for selling 2.70 grams of marijuana.[@1] His original life sentence had outlasted a change in the law: the applicable reduced penalty had a six-year ceiling.[@1]

## A life sentence survived a failed appeal

Cruz had been convicted in 1993 for a sale alleged to have taken place in April 1992.[@1] He appealed, but his lawyer repeatedly failed to file the required brief.[@1] The Court of Appeals ultimately dismissed the appeal as abandoned.[@1]

By then, the [1993 drug-law amendment](/history/philippines-death-penalty-1993/) and the Supreme Court's [Simon ruling](/history/philippines-simon-quantity-ruling-1994/) had introduced a route to a much shorter sentence for his quantity of marijuana.[@1,2] A missed filing had left the original life sentence in place while he remained in the national penitentiary.[@1]

## The lower penalty applied to old offences

The Simon decision held that the favourable parts of the amendment applied retrospectively.[@2] It divided marijuana below the 750-gram capital threshold into three lower bands.[@2] Amounts below 250 grams belonged to prision correccional, whose statutory range was six months and one day to six years.[@1,2]

Cruz's 2.70 grams fell within that lowest band.[@1] By the time the Supreme Court decided his case, he had already served longer than its maximum.[@1] The Solicitor General acknowledged that his continued detention had become unlawful.[@1]

## His mother sought an order for release

Cruz's mother, Maria, sought help from the University of the Philippines College of Law's legal-aid office in 1998.[@1] She petitioned for habeas corpus, asking the courts to examine the legal basis for keeping her son imprisoned.[@1]

The Supreme Court upheld the dismissal of his abandoned appeal and refused a new trial, but granted the separate habeas corpus petition.[@1] It directed the prison authorities to release him immediately unless another lawful ground required his detention.[@1] It also ordered his former lawyer to explain the failure to file the appeal brief.[@1]

## The case showed what retrospective relief could mean

Simon had identified habeas corpus as a way for people already serving final sentences to obtain the benefit of the reduced penalties.[@2] Cruz's release gave that rule a concrete result: the older life sentence no longer justified keeping him in custody.[@1]

The [2002 drug law](/history/philippines-drugs-act-2002/) subsequently replaced the earlier statute and made unauthorised marijuana sales punishable by life imprisonment to death regardless of quantity.[@3] Cruz's case belongs to the intervening period when the amount involved could substantially reduce a seller's sentence in [the Philippines](/country/ph/).[@1,3]
