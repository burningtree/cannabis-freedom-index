In 1871–1872 the Government of India collected provincial opinions on hemp drugs. The
formal policy response reproduced by the later Hemp Drugs Commission is dated
17 December 1873; the inquiry period and the resolution should not be conflated.

## The question

In 1871 the government of India, responding to a spreading belief that hemp drugs drove
people mad, sent a questionnaire to every provincial government. What were the medical
and moral effects of cannabis? Should cultivation be restricted further, or the drugs
prohibited outright?

## The verdict

The [resolution of 17 December 1873](/history/india-hemp-restriction-resolution-1873/)
asserted a link between cannabis and insanity. That was an official historical claim. The
governor-general called on the provinces to discourage the use of ganja and bhang by
restricting their cultivation, preparation and sale, and by taxing them as heavily as
could be done without driving the trade underground.

Some of the material was of this kind: officials in Hyderabad reported that when a man
ran amok or a widow burned herself, cannabis had been taken beforehand.

## The answers

The provinces were unpersuaded about the remedy.

- **Punjab** warned that people denied hemp and opium would probably turn to alcohol,
  which caused more violent crime than all India's stimulants together.
- **The North-Western Provinces** said hemp grew freely along the foot of the Himalayas
  and that preventing its production would be almost impossible.
- **An officer in Berar** who thought intemperance disastrous still held that reform
  must begin at home: close one source and people devise another, usually worse.
- **A collector in Madras** wrote that government should not check the culture of a
  useful plant because one of its properties was sometimes abused.

Only the Central Provinces and Burma favoured a system like
[Bengal's](/history/india-bengal-ganja-mahal-1854/). The governor-general conceded that
elsewhere it would probably be impracticable.

## The second look

The belief did not go away, and twenty years later a question in the House of Commons
forced a proper inquiry. The
[Hemp Drugs Commission](/history/india-hemp-drugs-commission-1894/) examined how asylum
statistics were compiled and found the effect of hemp on insanity "greatly
exaggerated".

The belief also travelled beyond India. Colonial doctors in
[Trinidad](/history/trinidad-cultivation-licence-1885/) and British Guiana cited Indian
opinion when they blamed ganja for filling their asylums.
