Tanganyika banned cannabis in three instalments: a customs notice in 1924, a cultivation ban in 1926, and a consolidated Dangerous Drugs Ordinance in 1935.[@1] All three named the plant in Swahili as well as English — bhang or bangi — a sign of who the laws were aimed at.[@1]

## A customs notice in 1924

The first step stopped the plant at the border. A 1924 order under the customs law prohibited importing "Cannabis sativa (known as Hemp, or True Hemp, and in Swahili as Bhang or Bangi)".[@1] Cannabis was one item in a longer prohibited list that also covered prepared opium and poppies.[@1] The notice said nothing about growing or possessing cannabis inside the territory; that came two years later.[@1]

## Growing, holding and using banned in 1926

The [Cultivation of Noxious Plants (Prohibition) Ordinance](/history/tanganyika-noxious-plants-ban-1926/), dated 18 June 1926, made it an offence to cultivate any scheduled plant, or to consume, use or possess it or any substance obtained from it.[@1] Its schedule named only one plant: "Cannabis Sativa (known as True Hemp and in Swahili as Bhang or Bhangi)".[@1] Offenders faced a fine of up to 2,000 shillings or twelve months in prison, and courts could order the plants destroyed.[@1] Magistrates and administrative officers could issue day-or-night search warrants, police could arrest without a warrant anyone caught offending, and the Governor could vary the schedule by gazette notice.[@1]

## Consolidation in 1935

The [Dangerous Drugs Ordinance of 1935](/history/tanganyika-dangerous-drugs-ordinance-1935/) folded cannabis into the territory's treaty-driven drug law, naming the Hague Convention of 1912 and both Geneva conventions alongside it.[@1] "Indian hemp" meant the dried flowering or fruiting tops of the pistillate Cannabis sativa plant, whatever they were called, and the external-trade rules covered raw opium, coca leaves, Indian hemp and hemp-resin preparations, with diversion certificates for transit cargo.[@1] Penalties rose sharply: up to 20,000 shillings or ten years with or without hard labour in the High Court, or up to 5,000 shillings or twelve months in a subordinate court.[@1]

The three texts show a colony learning prohibition by stages — border first, fields second, the full treaty apparatus third — and the story continues through the linked history entries and on [Tanzania's page](/country/tz/).[@1]
