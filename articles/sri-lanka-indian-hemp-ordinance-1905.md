Ceylon's colonial government extended its cannabis prohibition to growing plants
in 1905. The new law made growing, keeping or supplying any part of the plant
punishable by up to six months in prison, a 100-rupee fine, or both.

## A grower had won his appeal

The [existing drug law](/history/sri-lanka-opium-consolidation-1899/) prohibited
possessing and selling bhang and ganja. Whether those names also covered living
plants became decisive when an official found a man weeding leased land planted
with cannabis, plantains, coconuts and other trees.

A magistrate fined him 30 rupees for possessing ganja. He appealed, and in
[June 1904](/history/sri-lanka-growing-plants-ruling-1904/) Acting Chief Justice
Moncreiff set the conviction aside.

Moncreiff examined the earlier laws and the meanings of bhang and ganja. Those
terms referred to cannabis products, including dried leaves and flowering tops.
He found no wording that extended the possession offence to the growing plant.
For the man on the leased land, that distinction overturned the conviction.

## The new ban named the plant itself

The Indian Hemp Ordinance received assent on 21 September 1905. It defined
Indian hemp to include every part of both Cannabis indica and Cannabis sativa,
then expressly prohibited planting, growing and cultivation. Importing, selling,
distributing and knowingly possessing it were also offences.

Responsibility reached people in charge of land. When cannabis was found growing
on occupied land, the immediate occupier or person in charge was presumed to
have planted it unless they satisfied the court otherwise.

## Police could search by day or night

A magistrate could issue a search warrant on sworn information giving reasonable
grounds for suspicion. Searches could take place at any hour, with forced entry
if specifically authorised. Officers could also arrest without a warrant someone
they found committing an offence.

Courts could award an informer up to half the fine actually recovered. The law
also required cannabis involved in a charge to be forfeited and destroyed.

## Chemists needed an exception

The ban soon prompted a [proposal to allow medicinal sales](/history/sri-lanka-medicinal-hemp-proposal-1906/).
Acting Attorney-General J. H. Templer explained that the 1905 law had left
chemists and licensed poison dealers without permission to possess or sell
cannabis, despite its acknowledged medicinal usefulness.

Published in August 1906, the draft would let those dealers import, possess and
sell cannabis under poison regulations while retaining the cultivation ban.
Access to cannabis medicine had become another issue for the colonial
government in what is now [Sri Lanka](/country/lk/).
