In 1968 Argentina did something no military government would be expected to do: it stopped
punishing drug possession for personal use. Law 17,567 folded the word *estupefacientes* into
the law, listed the trafficking offences with one to six years in prison — and made unlawful
possession a crime only when it exceeded what personal use required.[@1]

## Six years of breathing room

The exemption sat on top of forty years of blanket prohibition. Argentina's first narcotics law
dated from 1924, and since 1926 even simple possession without a justification had been
punishable.[@1] The 1968 reform kept the trafficker's penalty but drew, for the first time, a
line between the dealer and the user.
[Argentina's first narcotics law passed in 1924.](/history/argentina-first-narcotics-law-1924/)
[Possession became a crime in 1926.](/history/argentina-possession-crime-1926/)

## Repealed, then reversed

The breathing room lasted six years. In 1973 Law 20,509 repealed the 1968 code reform and the
1926 rules returned; a year later Law 20,771 — framed as national security — set one to six
years for possession even for personal use.[@1]
[The exemption was repealed in 1973.](/history/argentina-1968-law-repealed-1973/)
[Personal possession became a crime again in 1974.](/history/argentina-recriminalisation-1974/)

## The long echo

It took the courts 35 years to get back to 1968. In 1986 the Supreme Court spared the rock
guitarist Bazterrica and Capalbo, holding the 1974 law unconstitutional for private
possession — then Menem's 1989 law re-criminalised it and the court upheld that in
1990.[@2] Only in 2009, in Arriola, did the court unanimously return to the principle the
dictatorship's reformers had written in 1968: private personal use is none of the criminal
law's business.[@2]
[The court first protected personal use in 1986.](/history/argentina-bazterrica-1986/)
[The court protected private use in 2009.](/history/argentina-arriola-2009/)
[Argentina](/country/ar/) still waits for Congress to write that principle into statute.
