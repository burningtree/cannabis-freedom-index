On 1 January 1930 the kingdom's first law sanctioning drug abuse took effect across Yugoslavia, threatening up to six months in prison for "serving" narcotic drugs.[@1] It landed in a country that grew hemp on a world scale and smoked hashish in Belgrade salons — where, as one trial would show, simply enjoying the drug was not even punishable.[@2,1]

## A hemp kingdom

Hemp had been an important Serbian crop since Italian farmers and hempseed strains arrived in the 1700s.[@1] In interwar Yugoslavia it occupied about one-third of the land under industrial plants, making the kingdom the world's third-largest grower after the Soviet Union and Italy.[@2] The crop served the growers' own needs — fibre and woody mass above all — and domestic hemp earned a poor reputation for its low THC beside Indian hemp, until post-war production almost ceased entirely.[@2]

## What the code did

The Criminal Code of the Kingdom of Serbs, Croats and Slovenes passed on 27 January 1929 and entered into force on the first day of 1930.[@1] Its section on crimes against public health punished serving narcotics with up to six months' imprisonment.[@1] The timing was international: Yugoslavia had ratified the Geneva opium convention the previous September, binding itself to the world's drug-control regime.[@1] The code applied kingdom-wide, from Slovenia to Macedonia, and its 1930 commencement is the recorded first ban for all seven successor entries.[@1]

## What the code met

Belgrade's salons gathered "men and women with strange passions" on Kralja Aleksandra Boulevard, Knez Mihailova and around Đeram market, where cocaine was used and hashish smoked.[@2] The clientele ran to failed actors, journalists, society ladies and even ministers, the staff were almost all Russians, and police tolerated the circles through the 1920s — raiding them more often only later, when they moved to the outskirts and smaller towns.[@2]

Skopje had its own scene: Indian hemp grew in the walled gardens of once-rich aghas and beys, Thessaloniki supplied hashish, and the prized local derivative was asrar, charas resin smoked in pipes passed hand to hand or in hookahs, often over strong black coffee in the midday bazaar break.[@2] When police raided the dealer Ja'far Ismailović's flat in 1934 and found motionless young men smoking kief, the court gave the veteran seller a suspended sentence plus costs — enjoyment itself was not punishable, and having served the time in custody he walked out free.[@2] Treated addiction barely registered: Kovin hospital admitted one drug-addiction case against 1,800 patients between 1924 and 1934.[@2]

The code thus criminalised supply into a culture it could hardly see: a hemp-growing peasantry that did not abuse its crop, salon circles the police had long left alone, and courts that sent users home.[@2,1] Its six-month ceiling stayed the law until later Yugoslav drug laws replaced it, and the 1930 commencement remains the base layer under [Serbia's](/country/rs/) scores.[@1]
