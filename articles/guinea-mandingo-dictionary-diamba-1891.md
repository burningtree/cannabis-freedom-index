In 1891 a French-Mandingo dictionary made a distinction no modern drug law bothers
with: it translated *diamba* specifically as tobacco for smoking, while *sira* meant
tobacco in general.[@1]

The entry is one of many European records of the cannabis word as tobacco across
Manding languages — proof that the plant arrived with a name already in use, filed by
outsiders under the nearest familiar leaf.[@1] Duvall's history keeps it as evidence
of how deep and how named the trade already was: not a new intoxicant but an old one,
mislabelled.[@1]

Guinea's profile still carries the word: *diamba*, smoking tobacco, distinguished
from tobacco in general — the 1891 dictionary's distinction surviving two prohibition
regimes later.
