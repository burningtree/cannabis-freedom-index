Nigeria's first Dangerous Drugs Ordinance restricted cannabis in 1927, while the
colonial administration maintained that the territory had little drug use to
control. Historian Gernot Klantschnig explains the apparent contradiction through
the law's international origins: officials were implementing a treaty framework,
rather than answering a domestic cannabis campaign.

## A law administered through medicine

The ordinance covered cannabis, opium and coca products. It restricted their use
and trade to medical and scientific purposes, under the supervision of the
colony's chief medical officer. Other unlicensed use and trade became criminal
offences.

This arrangement placed cannabis within a system of authorised exceptions. Its
legal availability depended on official permission and purpose; ordinary use
outside that framework was criminalised. The chief medical officer's role also
shows that the machinery of control initially extended beyond the police.

## A colonial template

Klantschnig connects the ordinance directly to the
[1925 Geneva Opium Convention](/history/geneva-opium-convention-1925/).
Colonial governments circulated legislative models, including Hong Kong's drug
ordinance, that could be reproduced elsewhere in the empire.

Nigeria's experience differed from the earlier regulation of alcohol. Alcohol
had generated substantial arguments about commerce, taxation and African welfare.
The historian finds no comparable local campaign behind the 1927 drug law.
International obligations supplied an administrative reason to legislate even
without a publicly recognised Nigerian cannabis problem.

## Official absence and incomplete evidence

Colonial reports presented dangerous-drug use and illicit traffic as rare or
absent. Those statements describe what the administration reported, rather than
establishing that cannabis was unknown or unused.

Klantschnig stresses gaps in the evidence for West African use and trade,
particularly between the world wars. The familiar account linking cannabis's
spread to returning Second World War soldiers cannot explain a statute passed
in 1927. Neither the statute nor the government's assurances supplies a reliable
count of contemporary users or growers.

## Prohibition before a policing campaign

A [replacement ordinance in 1935](/history/nigeria-dangerous-drugs-1935/) continued
the legal framework. The later
[police narcotics squad](/history/nigeria-police-narcotics-squad-1953/)
belongs to a separate stage in the development of enforcement.

The distinction matters for [Nigeria's history](/country/ng/): a criminal rule
could exist well before cannabis became a prominent public or political issue.
The 1927 entry records that legal restriction. It does not establish that police
immediately enforced it everywhere, or that the administration possessed a clear
picture of the practices it had prohibited.
