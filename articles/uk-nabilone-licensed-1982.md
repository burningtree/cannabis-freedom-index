In 1982 Britain licensed a cannabis-based medicine while keeping the plant itself fully
banned. The synthetic cannabinoid nabilone became available for hospital patients whose
chemotherapy nausea resisted every other drug — the first lawful medical use of a
cannabinoid since herbal cannabis medicines were [withdrawn in
1973](/history/uk-cannabis-medicines-withdrawn-1973/).

## A synthetic analogue, hospital-only

Nabilone is a synthetic analogue of THC, made by Eli Lilly.[@1] The licence covered a
single indication: nausea and vomiting caused by cytotoxic chemotherapy and unresponsive
to conventional antiemetics.[@1] It was prescription-only and hospital-only, sold in
1 mg capsules, and — unusually — it was never made a controlled drug.[@1]

That legal detail mattered. Herbal cannabis sat in Schedule 1, with no recognised
medical use, while its synthetic cousin could be prescribed on an ordinary hospital
prescription.

## Effective, then eclipsed

The licence rested on 1970s trials in which nabilone matched prochlorperazine and the
other antiemetics of the day against chemotherapy nausea.[@1] But the drug was little
used even for its licensed purpose.[@1] In the 1980s the serotonin antagonists —
ondansetron, granisetron and tropisetron — arrived, worked in up to 90 per cent of
patients, and could be given intravenously as well as by mouth.[@1] No trial ever
pitted cannabinoids against them.[@1]

Sales tell the story: 5,400 packs of twenty capsules in 1997–98, at £102 a pack.[@1]
One Nottingham specialist told the Lords inquiry that nabilone was not widely used for
nausea; an MS Society witness said it was used "very infrequently" for multiple
sclerosis — probably less than cannabis itself.[@1]

## Off-label persistence

A few doctors kept faith. At James Paget Hospital in Great Yarmouth, Dr William
Notcutt treated 60 chronic-pain patients with nabilone off-label; about half got some
relief, though side effects — unpleasant psychoactive effects and drowsiness — kept the
overall success rate near 30 per cent.[@1] The BMA urged wider licensed indications,
for wasting in cancer and AIDS and for MS-related spasticity, but the manufacturer
never applied and the regulator would not accept anecdote in place of trials.[@1]

## A footnote that outlived the ban

Nabilone's narrow corridor stayed open for 36 years while herbal cannabis remained
Schedule 1. When [specialist doctors could finally prescribe cannabis itself in
2018](/history/uk-medical-cannabis-2018/), nabilone was already there — the exception
that had proved, since 1982, that a cannabinoid could be a lawful medicine in Britain.

## What moved in the index

The [United Kingdom's](/country/gb/) cultivation score stands at 1 rather than 3 from
this point, and enforcement at 3 rather than 4, while access stayed at 0: a
hospital-only synthetic for chemotherapy nausea did not count as general access.
