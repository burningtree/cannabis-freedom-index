On 3 December 1975 South Korean prosecutors arrested three folk singers for smoking marijuana.[@1] Within a month more than eighty entertainers had been taken in.[@1] The country's strict cannabis law, passed the following year, grew out of that winter, and the winter itself grew out of a five-year campaign against the way young people looked and sounded.[@1]

## A campaign against youth

Under President Park Chung-hee the state had been policing youth culture since 1970, when a month-long drive went after long hair, short skirts and experimental art.[@1]

| Year | Measure |
|---|---|
| 1970 | First yearly crackdown on long hair and miniskirts |
| October 1971 | Almost 50,000 young men given forced haircuts in a month |
| 1973 | Long hair on men made an offence |
| 1975 | A "healthy song" required on every record; over 100 songs banned |
| Summer 1975 | The authorities film an anti-marijuana picture |

By 1974 Korean folk, rock and pop had topped the charts for the first time. Park warned that young people smoking marijuana would "bring ruin to our country".[@1]

## A law nobody knew about

Korean hemp had always been legal: the 1957 Narcotics Act banned only imported Indian marijuana.[@2,4] Washington pressed Seoul to close the loophole around the American bases, and in July 1970 parliament passed the Habit-Forming Drug Control bill, through the [marijuana criminalisation of July 1970](/history/south-korea-habit-forming-drugs-law-1970/).[@2] For four years arrests stayed inside the camp towns, so home smokers never realised the law had changed.[@2]

## The arrests

The crackdown on smokers began late in November 1975; prosecutors had planned to arrest several dozen.[@1] On 3 December they took in three folk singers, among them Lee Jang-hee. The rock musician Shin Joong-hyun and Kim Jung-ho followed, then members of a band and a film director.[@1] Shin drew the harshest treatment: a psychiatric hospital, prison time with torture, and a blacklist from concerts and radio that lasted until Park's assassination in 1979.[@3]

Most of those arrested were not sent to prison. The Entertainers' Association banned them from recording and performing, some for life, and magazines reported that some were confined in mental institutions as addicts.[@1] Newspapers printed cartoons teaching readers the signs of a user, and bans on more than 200 Western pop songs followed.[@1]

## The law that came after

Hemp had been an ordinary crop, grown in every province into the 1930s and on 9,000 hectares in the late 1950s.[@4] A law of 1957 had forbidden only imported "Indian marijuana".[@4] The [Cannabis Control Act of 1976](/history/south-korea-cannabis-control-act-1976/) banned the plant as a drug in every form, and in 2000 it was folded into a single narcotics law, with the ban unchanged.[@4]

## Why the index moves before the ban

The December arrests changed no statute, but mass arrests, prison cells and lifetime performance bans are why the
[South Korea](/country/kr/) enforcement score falls in 1975, before the 1976 Act completes the ban.[@1]
