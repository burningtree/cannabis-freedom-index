The [Indian Hemp Drugs Commission](/history/india-hemp-drugs-commission-1894/) of 1894 told the British
government not to ban cannabis but to control and tax it. Ten years later Bengal, the province held up as
the model, had only just built the warehouses the commission asked for.

## Control, not prohibition

The commission rejected prohibition, quoting John Stuart Mill on the harm of restraining what people chose
for themselves. It recommended four things: adequate taxation, cultivation only under licence, fewer shops,
and a limit on how much anyone could hold. It wanted the same system across British India, on the Bengal
model, with government warehouses for the crop and one limit on personal possession everywhere.

Two Indian members dissented. Lala Nihal Chand wanted ganja and charas eventually prohibited. The
Governor-General answered that there were many matters in which adults must be allowed to judge for
themselves.

## Ten slow years

The provinces were in no hurry. Berar took three years to pass its
[1897 law](/history/india-berar-hemp-drugs-law-1897/), and Bombay did not limit personal possession until
[1913](/history/india-bombay-possession-limit-1913/). Even in Bengal progress was slow.

| Year | Step in the Ganja Mahal |
|---|---|
| 1894 | A tenth of the fields measured with ropes, unchecked |
| 1895 | Government resolves to build public warehouses at Naogaon |
| 1896 | The crop weighed for the first time before storage |
| 1902–03 | Fields measured with Gunter's chain, a fifth of the work checked |
| 1904 | The whole crop stored in public warehouses for the first time |

A magistrate on special duty, G. Rainy, reported in 1904 that smuggling continued. He proposed bringing in
the railway police, putting every village watchman on alert at harvest time, and having watchmen visit the
houses of convicted smugglers twice a night through February and March. In the 1870s about a dozen excise
officers had supervised the trade; now Rainy wanted the whole police force of eastern Bengal involved.

## The money kept coming

Behind the controls lay revenue. Taxes on hemp drugs raised about 5.6 million rupees across British India
in 1900, 7.8 million in 1905 and 18.3 million [in 1921](/history/india-hemp-drugs-revenue-1921/). In Bengal
they made up about a fifth of all excise income, in Delhi a third.

Use did not fall everywhere. In Assam consumption rose by 88 per cent between 1901 and 1911, and a
[committee](/history/india-assam-ganja-report-1918/) found ganja smoked by cultivators, boatmen and higher
castes alike. [India](/country/in/)'s cannabis policy stayed one of licensing and tax, and national
prohibition came only with the [NDPS Act of 1985](/history/india-ndps-act-1985/).
