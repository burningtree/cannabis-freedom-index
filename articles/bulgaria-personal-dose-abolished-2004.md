In 2004 Bulgaria repealed the single-dose exemption: paragraph 3 of Article 354a had spared a dependent person holding a single dose from punishment, and the amendment known as the single-dose law removed it.[@2] From then on, possessing any drug in any quantity was a crime, whether or not the person was dependent; wholesalers, small dealers and users were treated alike.[@2]

## What the repeal took away

The old paragraph had drawn a line between dependence and dealing: a dependent user with a single dose stayed outside punishment.[@2] Its removal erased the only quantity-based shelter in the law. A study a year later judged the ban and its effects part of a wider failure of law and institutions.[@2]

The repeal made Bulgarian law among the harshest in Europe — and it stuck. Ordinary possession still carries one to six years. Minor cases can receive a fine, while qualifying first offenders can be released from criminal responsibility with an administrative penalty under Article 78a.[@2]

## The march that followed

Fourteen years later, protesters still marched against the 2004 logic. The 2018 "Freedom for Weed" procession demanded decriminalisation of small-quantity possession and cultivation for personal needs.[@1] The Liberation Society argued the criminalising law did not work: in fifteen years, the number of Bulgarians who had tried marijuana had grown five times.[@1]

The march did not restore the dose. But it named the repeal's legacy precisely — a law that treats every user like a dealer, and a user count that kept climbing regardless.[@1] The repeal is traced in [Bulgaria's](/country/bg/) history as the moment the personal dose disappeared.
