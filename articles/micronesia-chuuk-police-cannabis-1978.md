Marijuana came to Chuuk the way most foreign things come to small islands: carried in by a stranger, planted, and talked about.[@1] A foreigner living on Nama toward the end of the 1960s sowed seeds he had brought with him, and they grew into healthy plants.[@1] Peace Corps volunteers were planting the same introduction across Micronesia in that decade.[@2]

## Students, sailors and seeds

By the early 1970s a small amount of marijuana was filtering into Chuuk, brought by students returning from Palau, Yap and Saipan.[@1] In 1973 a sailor off a cargo ship carried seeds in from Saipan and planted them on a lagoon island, where they grew extremely well.[@1] It was not until the late 1970s that the plant invaded the islands in quantity: better boats and radios, and more Chuukese leaving for school, gave it new roads in.[@1]

## The first uprooting

Chuuk's police records show the first reported marijuana case on Moen late in 1978, when officers uprooted a single plant.[@1] In the years that followed the raids mounted: from 1979 to 1984 confiscations averaged $21,010 worth of marijuana a year.[@1] Yet for all that buying and selling, the courts saw almost nothing — a single recorded case in Chuuk's judiciary, against a trade that stayed a family venture, growing and selling to meet daily needs, with little shipped beyond the lagoon.[@1] More than half the regular users first smoked out of curiosity about something they had only heard of, while others held back fearing the craziness that smokers warned of.[@1]

## A borrowed code

Chuuk never wrote its own drug law.[@1] The state simply adopted the FSM Criminal Code of 1980, itself the old Trust Territory Code, which fined possession of an ounce or less up to $50, punished an ounce to a kilo with up to three months or $500, over a kilo with up to a year or $1,000, and trafficking with up to five years or $5,000.[@1] The code met a plant that had been Chuukese for barely a decade: children of five knew its smell and its slang, and called it the different cigarette.[@1] Today it is the second most used drug in the country after alcohol — an import, first a curiosity, then a crop, then a crime.[@2,1]
