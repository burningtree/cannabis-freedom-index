In 1896 Hassan I's son and successor restored the kif and tobacco monopolies
his father had abolished eight years earlier. Where the [1888
ban](/history/morocco-hassan-kif-ban-1888/) had lasted less than a decade,
the restored monopolies endured for half a century, carried through the
French protectorate era into the 1950s.[@1]

## A revenue machine restored

The monopolies were the makhzen's fiscal backbone. Tobacco had been
monopolised since the 1844 war with France; an 1856 treaty with Britain kept
tobacco and smoking herbs outside its abolition of farm monopolies; a kif
monopoly was identified by 1860; and by 1876 tobacco and kif were a primary
source of government income, worth 37 percent of revenues in one northern
region in 1884. Hassan I had valued the income and believed the monopoly
limited consumption, but his qualms about selling an intoxicant led him to
consult a cleric and ban both trades in 1888. His son felt no such qualms:
the monopolies came back in 1896.[@1]

## Guaranteed by the Europeans

As French influence over Morocco grew, European diplomats agreed to keep the
monopolies in place — a foreign guarantee for a Moroccan fiscal device.
Between the ban and the restoration falls a [disputed
episode](/history/morocco-hassan-regulations-1890/): accounts conventionally
dated around 1890 attribute cannabis cultivation privileges in five Rif
villages to Hassan I, but later scholarship finds no decree behind them,
and the database keeps 1890 only as a conventional reference year. Whatever happened in 1890, the 1896 restoration is the documented
return of the monopoly system. In 1910 the tobacco monopoly passed to a
state-owned farm, with kif added to its crops in 1914, folding cannabis
revenue directly into the colonial-era state.[@1]

## A long goodbye

The restored monopoly outlived the sultanate itself. The protectorate banned
hashish imports in 1922 while leaving kif aside, and kif was not fully
banned until 1954. After independence the [1956 ban](/history/morocco-kif-ban-1956/)
extended prohibition to both former zones, though growing carried on around
Ketama. The Rif farming that Hassan I had permitted in the 1870s to build
political support survived every version of the regime — monopoly, ban and
restoration alike. The [Morocco profile](/country/ma/) follows the later
medical and recreational history.[@1]
