Adults in the Northern Mariana Islands gained legal cannabis possession, home cultivation and gifts when Governor Ralph Torres signed the legalisation law on 21 September 2018. The law also authorised medical use and a licensed commercial market, with shops and lounges to be regulated by a new cannabis commission.

## Torres backed regulation but challenged parts of the bill

An [earlier federal assessment](/history/cnmi-eradication-imports-assessment-2003/) had linked crop destruction to reduced local cultivation and a greater reliance on imported cannabis.

Representative Joseph P. Deleon Guerrero introduced the bill that reached the governor. Its findings argued that a regulated market could provide treatment options, reduce access by young people and generate money for public services.

Torres supported those aims and the prospect of jobs and economic diversification. His signing statement said the law drew on Oregon's approach, while raising concerns about home cultivation, medical exceptions, business eligibility and the resources needed for enforcement.

He approved the law with several item vetoes, removing particular fee and tax terms while allowing the wider legalisation to proceed.

## Households could grow and keep a personal supply

People aged twenty-one and over could keep up to eight ounces of usable cannabis at a household or cultivation site. The household growing allowance covered six mature plants and twelve immature plants, with requirements to keep them out of public view and secure against access by young people.

A patient or caregiver could grow up to twelve mature and twenty-four immature plants when a physician considered the larger allowance necessary and practical for treatment. That exception required a physician statement and a homegrown-registry card.

The law kept public possession to one ounce of usable cannabis, alongside separate limits for other products.

## Giving cannabis away became lawful within caps

Adults could give another adult up to one ounce of usable cannabis for noncommercial purposes. Separate limits covered solid and liquid products, extracts and immature plants.

The recipient had to accept the gift. Home growers could share within those conditions, but selling their cannabis or plants for payment remained prohibited. Commercial sales required a business licence.

## The commercial market took longer to open

Torres vetoed the proposed five-dollar homegrown registration fee and several flat business fees, arguing that the amounts should better reflect regulatory costs. He also rejected the proposed cannabis excise-tax terms.

Lawmakers returned to those issues in the [2019 amendment](/history/cnmi-cannabis-registry-tax-amendment-2019/). Rules, applications and inspections followed before the [first shop opened in 2021](/history/cnmi-first-dispensary-2021/).

The reform therefore gave adults a lawful home-growing and sharing route while the [Northern Mariana Islands' retail system](/country/mp/) was still being built.
