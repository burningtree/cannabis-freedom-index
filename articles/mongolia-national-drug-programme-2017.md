On 7 March 2017, Mongolia’s cabinet approved a national programme against the illicit circulation of narcotic drugs and psychotropic substances. The government announced it as a coordinated response combining enforcement, prevention and treatment.

The programme covered drugs generally. It belongs in cannabis history as part of the national control system, but its statistics cannot be presented as a count of cannabis cases.

## The case for coordination

The [cabinet announcement](https://www.mongolia.gov.mn/news/view/16381) reported 388 registered cases and 1,112 people investigated over the preceding ten years. These concerned prohibited preparation, acquisition, storage, transport, dispatch, sale or cross-border movement of controlled substances.

An investigation is not the same as a conviction. The announcement separately reported that courts had decided 131 criminal cases and found 189 people guilty during 2014–2015 and the first ten months of 2016. Neither series identifies how many defendants were cannabis users, how much they possessed, or what proportion received each sentence.

## Prevention alongside enforcement

The cabinet proposed closer control of illicit circulation, identifying the causes and conditions of offences, training personnel and protecting the population from drug-related harm. The announcement also described changing methods of concealment and delivery as part of the rationale for a coordinated response.

Implementation was to be financed through annual state budgets, assistance from donor countries and international organisations, and private investment. These were funding directions, rather than evidence that every proposed activity had already been delivered.

The programme did not itself establish a new cannabis-possession offence or a new personal cultivation allowance. Its significance was the organisation of state action around the existing prohibition framework.

## Treatment within the programme

Voluntary and compulsory treatment were both named among the programme’s objectives. That combination had a legal background: Article 12 of the [2002 control law](/history/mongolia-narcotics-law-2002/) already addressed voluntary treatment and compulsory treatment ordered by a court.

The cabinet’s language should not be interpreted as proof that treatment replaced criminal punishment, or that cannabis consumption alone triggered compulsory treatment. Those conclusions would require the applicable procedures and evidence of implementation.

## What the announcement establishes

The programme was approved before the replacement [Criminal Code](/history/mongolia-criminal-code-2015/) entered into force in July 2017. Programme adoption and commencement of criminal legislation are distinct events, even when both shape the same enforcement system.

For the [Mongolia timeline](/country/mn/), this announcement documents a policy priority and the government’s stated reasoning. It supplies no cannabis-specific outcome evaluation and no demonstrated change in ordinary users’ legal freedoms. Assessing its effects would require later evidence about prosecutions, treatment provision and the experience of people using cannabis.
