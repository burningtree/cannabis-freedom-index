In the early 1930s Lebanon was shipping fifty to sixty tons of Egypt-bound hashish a year, and the man who finally forced Paris to act was an Egyptian: Russell Pasha, chief of Cairo's drug police, who used the League of Nations to shame the French into tightening narcotics control in Lebanon and Syria.[@1]

## How the Beqaa became Egypt's supplier

Lebanese growing began as an experiment around 1907; as Greece bowed to pressure and curtailed its own cannabis, cultivation in Lebanon "increased enormously".[@1] Peasants grew on orders from the big notables who owned the land — ministers, deputies, powerful republicans — and the French, frank about peasant livelihoods, let it be.[@1] When destruction was ordered, landlords balked: in the Beqaa's Baalbek district proprietors refused to destroy their crops and barred peasants from entering land to cut the hemp.[@1]

## Shaming Paris

Russell personalised the fight, complaining to the League about Mandatory Lebanon's failure and urging the French delegate to criminalise even possession of a substance whose growing, making and moving were already forbidden.[@1] Embarrassed, the French answered with stricter prohibitive regulations: uniform narcotics legislation conforming more closely to the 1925 Opium Convention, a Sûreté Générale antidrug squad with a regional mandate, and harder field destruction.[@1] Cairo noticed — the Bureau sent its "keenest satisfaction" at a temporary "diminution in Syrian arrivals".[@1]

## A Pyrrhic victory

The shortage filled instantly with Turkish hashish, and clientelism did the rest: stricter measures could not hold the line long.[@1] By 1939 Russell was back before the Opium Advisory Committee, complaining that France had broken its assurance that all cultivated areas would be torn up and stocks destroyed — "many of the governing classes of the Lebanon" were planting vast areas of hemp themselves.[@1] The [country page](/country/lb/) follows the law from the Mandate to the modern republic.
