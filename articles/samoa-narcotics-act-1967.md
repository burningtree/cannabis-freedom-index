Samoa's drug law dates from 1967 and is still in force. The Law Reform Commission, which reviewed it fifty years later, describes it as "largely based on the old New Zealand's Misuse of Drugs Act 1965". The Ministry of Health administers it.

## What the Act prohibits

| Section | Conduct |
|---|---|
| 6 | Cultivating a prohibited plant, or possessing any of its seed |
| 7 | Knowingly possessing an illegal drug, or attempting to |
| 7 | Using it: the Act names procuring, consuming and smoking |
| 7 | Supplying or administering it to another person |

Part 4 sets out exemptions. A person who holds a licence to deal in a narcotic is not liable, and the chief executive of the Ministry of Health may grant such a licence with the Minister's approval. The only regulations ever made under the Act concern licences to import, export and deal, and carriage by ship and aircraft.

## Two amendments in fifty years

The Commission counts only two changes to the Act. In 2006 parliament added offences for manufacturing a narcotic and selling a controlled precursor. In 2009 it raised the penalties: possession of the seed of a prohibited plant, for example, went from a maximum of seven years to fourteen. The same amendment introduced the three classes of drugs, A, B and C. Cannabis is in Class B, described as a high risk of harm.

## How it is used

Police figures given to the Commission show that the most common drug offence in Samoa is possession of marijuana, and that most of those cases involve small quantities. Possession of seeds comes second and cultivation third.

The Commission called the penalties "highly disproportionate": up to fourteen years for possessing cannabis, against three months or a $500 fine in New Zealand. It still recommended keeping them. Its reasoning was that the court "in practice does not strictly apply them" and has discretion to choose a suitable sentence case by case.

## Why the index moved

The index treats 1967 as the start of prohibition in independent Samoa, so all six scores fall here from their unrestricted starting values. Possession, growing, supply and use each became an offence under a single Act, with no amount set aside for personal use and no lawful source outside a ministry licence.
