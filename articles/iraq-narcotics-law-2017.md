Iraq's 2017 drug law set one to three years in prison and a fine for possessing, producing or growing drugs for personal use, replacing an older law whose personal-use maximum had reached fifteen years.[@1,2] It also protected people who voluntarily sought specialist hospital treatment from prosecution under specified conditions.[@1]

## Personal use and trafficking had different penalties

The [1979 amendment](/history/iraq-drug-treatment-hemp-reform-1979/) had allowed three to fifteen years for personal-use possession or growing.[@2] The new law's one-to-three-year offence covered personal-use conduct including importing, manufacturing, buying and cultivating, with a fine of 5–10 million dinars.[@1]

Growing for trade and specified trafficking offences still carried death or life imprisonment.[@1] The new treatment options therefore sat within a law that continued to punish supply severely.[@1]

The statute repealed the 1965 law while keeping earlier rules temporarily in force where they did not conflict with the replacement.[@1]

## Seeking treatment could prevent prosecution

People who presented themselves voluntarily to a specialist hospital for drug treatment were protected from a criminal case.[@1] The law provided observation, further treatment and medical decisions about the person's needs.[@1]

That protection came with obligations: if a patient failed to follow the treatment programme, the institution had to notify the competent court so legal action could be considered.[@1]

For someone seeking help, the hospital route could therefore change the response from prosecution to a medically supervised programme.[@1]

## A correction linked court treatment to personal use

The original court-treatment article referred to a separate offence involving premises where drugs were used.[@1] A [January 2018 correction](/history/iraq-treatment-reference-correction-2018/) changed that reference to the personal-use offence.[@3]

The corrected rule allowed hospital treatment for proven dependence or outpatient visits for people shown to use drugs, with further court decisions based on medical reports.[@1,3] Those routes gave courts alternatives when dealing with eligible personal-use offenders.[@1,3]

## Judges' sentencing choices remained contested

A later judicial-supervision review described [three Badra judgments in 2019](https://tracker.cannabisfreedom.fyi/report/iraq-badra-personal-use-fines-2019/) imposing fines alone under general mitigation rules.[@4] Inspectors challenged the one-million-dinar amounts, saying a fine chosen under those rules still had to respect the drug law's five-million minimum.[@4]

The debate in [Iraq](/country/iq/) continued over how criminal penalties, sentencing discretion and treatment should operate together.[@1,4]
