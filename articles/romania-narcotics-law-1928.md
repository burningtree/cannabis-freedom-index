In 1928 Romania established narcotics laws covering hashish and its
preparations.[@2] The law came two years after the country prohibited growing
cannabis for resin, and it belonged to a chain of Balkan bans that chased
hashish farming from country to country toward the Egyptian market.[@1]

## Chasing the crop around the Balkans

Greece banned cultivation in 1920, then had to pass a more drastic 1932
law providing for the destruction of stocks.[@1] In between, traffickers
tried to plant cannabis for resin in one Eastern European country after
another: Bulgaria, where growing was
introduced in 1919-1920 and prohibited in 1925; Romania, where prohibition
came in 1926; Yugoslavia, tried around 1927 and banned in 1932; and
Albania.[@1] Turkey, always a large producer, grew more as Greek output dried
up until its own 1933 law banned hashish cultivation, possession, use and
trade.[@1] Seizures in Egypt and North Africa, the traffickers' best
customers, traced the whole route.[@1]

## What Romania's law did

The 1928 law put hashish and its preparations under narcotics
control.[@2] Growing for resin was already outlawed: the United Nations'
1951 cannabis survey dates Romania's cultivation prohibition to
1926.[@1] The pair of measures — the 1926 cultivation ban and the 1928
narcotics law — closed the fields the traffickers had opened after Greece's
1920 ban.[@1,2] The 1928 law thus did for hashish preparations what the 1926
ban had done for the crop, covering the processed drug as well as the
plants.[@1,2] The 1928 law came eight years after Greece's first ban and
three years after Bulgaria's, following the crop around the Balkans with
the lawbook.[@1,2]

## Bans that held, more or less

The Balkan governments passed prohibition measures and Bouquet credited
their efforts to trace and destroy secret plantations.[@1] Farmers kept
growing until the late-1920s bans shut the region's fields.[@1] Consumption
did not vanish: Bouquet noted clandestine use persisting in Turkey, Bulgaria
and Greece.[@1] Writing in 1951, he judged cannabis no longer of outstanding
importance for Europe — the bans, on his account, had worked.[@1] The sequence
runs through the [Greek cultivation ban of 1920](/history/greece-cultivation-ban-1920/),
the [Bulgarian resin-cultivation ban of 1925](/history/bulgaria-resin-cultivation-ban-1925/)
and the [Romanian resin-cultivation ban of 1926](/history/romania-resin-cultivation-ban-1926/);
the [Romania profile](/country/ro/) follows the later history.[@1]
