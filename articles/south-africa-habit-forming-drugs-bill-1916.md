South Africa's government drafted a national drug bill in 1916 that would have prohibited cannabis use, possession, trafficking and unlicensed cultivation.[@1] It proposed fines of up to one hundred pounds and prison terms of up to three months, but was never presented to parliament.[@1]

## Provincial policies had left a divided landscape

Before Union in 1910, different colonies had pursued different cannabis rules.[@1] The [Orange River Colony](/history/orange-river-colony-dagga-ordinance-1903/) prohibited supplying and receiving dagga, while the [Cape Colony](/history/cape-colony-dagga-poison-listing-1905/) reserved cannabis sales to chemists through a poison listing.[@1]

In the Transvaal, [mine surveys](/history/transvaal-mine-dagga-survey-1908/) recorded substantial opposition to a total ban.[@1] Officials concerned with African customs and labour recruitment did not always share the medical campaigners' demand for suppression.[@1]

The national draft would have brought a broader range of conduct under one drug-control scheme.[@1]

## International drug policy provided a reason to act

The bill was prepared in response to the drug-control arrangements arising from the 1912 Hague convention.[@1] It grouped cannabis with substances such as opium, cocaine and morphine within a national prohibition scheme.[@1]

The proposed law also targeted pipes and other material used for smoking the listed drugs.[@1] For users, the controls would therefore have reached smoking equipment as well as the plant and transactions involving it.[@1]

## The draft contained two different kinds of dagga

Its terminology reflected confusion over the name dagga.[@1] It listed cannabis as Indian hemp while separately including plants it identified as wild or red dagga.[@1]

Both groups were to be controlled, with plant parts ranging from roots and stems to leaves and seeds covered by the descriptions.[@1] The shared everyday name had therefore entered the draft even though the plants were classified separately.[@1]

Waetjen places this within a longer history of officials and medical authorities disagreeing over the plant's identity, uses and effects.[@1]

## A national ban eventually arrived through another law

The draft's intended start date was January 1917, but it did not reach parliament.[@1] Nationwide prohibition came instead through the [1922 customs and excise law](/history/south-africa-national-ban-1922/).[@1]

Officials continued to contest enforcement after that ban, producing a [December compromise for remote African areas](/history/south-africa-enforcement-compromise-1922/).[@1] In [South Africa](/country/za/), the unused bill marked an earlier attempt to unify cannabis control before the government found another legislative route.[@1]
