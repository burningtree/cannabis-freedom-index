For ten years Trinidad ran a licensed cannabis trade modelled on British India. Then it
banned the plant.

## Who used it

Ganja came to Trinidad with indentured labourers from India, more than 140,000 of whom
arrived between 1838 and 1918 to work the sugar estates. It was widely grown on the
island, particularly around Cedros.

## The first attempt

Officials had tried to limit it before. An
[ordinance of 1885](/history/trinidad-cultivation-licence-1885/) imposed licence duties
on growing cannabis so heavy that they were meant to work as a prohibition. The
superintendent of the asylum, who had campaigned for it, credited the ordinance with
halving the share of Indian patients. It was
[repealed in 1899](/history/trinidad-repeal-1899/).

## The 1915 system

The Ganja Ordinance of 1915 took a different approach. Cannabis sold on the island was
collected into bonded warehouses and released only to sellers who had paid a licence
fee. The model was Bengal, where the government had run a supervised, taxed trade since
[1854](/history/india-bengal-ganja-mahal-1854/).

It was a way of making ganja visible to the state and expensive to the user, without
making either a criminal.

## Why it ended

The licensed trade lasted a decade. In
[1925](/history/trinidad-ganja-ordinance-1925/), the year cannabis entered
international control at Geneva, Trinidad prohibited it.

The ban held for 94 years. [Trinidad and Tobago](/country/tt/) decriminalised
possession and home growing in [2019](/history/trinidad-decriminalisation-2019/), and
in [2026](/history/trinidad-age-limit-bill-2026/) its Senate voted to raise the age
for cannabis to 25.

In the index the 1915 ordinance is the first step down from an unregulated state:
access, possession and growing all became subject to a licence.
