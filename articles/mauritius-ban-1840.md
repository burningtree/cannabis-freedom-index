British Mauritius prohibited cannabis under Ordinance No. 2 of 1840. Known locally as gandia, the plant became an object of colonial policing decades before international treaties placed cannabis under common controls. The history also contains a later repeal, so 1840 should not be treated as the start of an unbroken prohibition lasting to the present.

## The concern behind the ordinance

Historian Jamie Banks connects the measure with official concern about cannabis being used in robberies. His account distinguishes this early rationale from the arguments about cannabis and insanity that became prominent in later colonial debates.

That distinction matters. A nineteenth-century prohibition cannot be explained simply by borrowing the medical claims made by officials many years afterwards. The sources identify particular concerns, institutions and moments; they do not establish that every campaign against gandia had the same cause.

The legal compilation containing the ordinance has been identified, but its original pages were not independently inspected for this article. The ordinance number and historical explanation here rest on Banks’s scholarly account. No exact enactment day or detailed penalty schedule is claimed.

## Migration and colonial government

The wider setting was the movement of Indian labourers through the British Empire. Banks discusses Mauritius alongside other colonies where Indian migration made cannabis consumption a subject of official attention.

A parliamentary debate on 14 July 1840 shows how contested that migration was. Members discussed whether Britain should permit and supervise labour migration from India to Mauritius and other possessions. One proposed a select committee; others disputed the timing and the government’s ability to oversee the colonial authorities.

The parliamentary record supplies contemporary evidence of those labour-policy disputes. It does not itself discuss or enact the cannabis ordinance, and it cannot establish that the two measures were parts of a single coordinated plan.

## A law with a later history

Further restrictions followed in [1848](/history/mauritius-possession-tightened-1848/). Eventually, the effectiveness of prohibition itself became a matter of official dispute. The later [1883 repeal](/history/mauritius-legalisation-1883/) has its own entry and article.

Those changes are important when interpreting the first ban. Enactment tells us what authorities attempted to prohibit. It does not tell us how consistently the law was enforced, how easily consumers obtained cannabis, or whether the same regime survived unchanged.

## An early colonial prohibition

Mauritius belongs in the early history of statutory cannabis control alongside the [Rio municipal ordinance of 1830](/history/brazil-rio-pango-ban-1830/). Their jurisdictions and social settings differed. Neither should be presented as proof of a worldwide prohibition already in place during the early nineteenth century.
