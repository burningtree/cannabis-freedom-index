Mexico's Supreme Court invalidated the absolute statutory ban on adult
recreational cannabis on 28 June 2021. The decision made a personal-use permit
available without requiring each applicant to win an individual constitutional
case, but it stopped well short of establishing legal retail sales.

## From repeated rulings to a general declaration

Between 2015 and 2019, the court's First Chamber ruled five times that the blanket
prohibition disproportionately restricted the right to free development of the
personality. The court contrasted that serious interference with the prohibition's
limited protection of health and public order.

The full court then issued General Declaration of Unconstitutionality 1/2018. It
removed the parts of the General Health Law that allowed cannabis and THC
activities to be authorised only for medical or scientific purposes. Unlike the
earlier individual judgments, the declaration had general effect.

The court described the decision as protecting personal autonomy throughout the
country: adults could choose and carry out their own life plans without
unjustified interference by the state or third parties.

## A permit, not an open market

Under the decision, adults could ask the health authority, through COFEPRIS, for
authorisation covering cultivation, harvesting, preparation, possession and
transport for recreational personal use. The route therefore protected a complete
personal-use chain rather than consumption alone.

The limits were equally important. An authorisation could not allow importing,
commercial trading, supplying another person, or any other transfer or
distribution. The right could not be exercised in front of minors, in a public
place before people who had not consented, or while doing something dangerous to
others, such as driving.

The ruling thus removed the absolute administrative prohibition while leaving
Mexico without ordinary licensed shops. Adults had to use the permit process, and
the judgment did not turn cannabis into a generally saleable product.
