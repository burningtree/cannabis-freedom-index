Britain built its modern drug laws between 1916 and 1923 and left cannabis out of all
of them. Nobody decided to; nobody thought of it.

## The army goes first

On [11 May 1916](/history/uk-army-council-cannabis-order-1916/) the Army Council
forbade the sale of a list of drugs to servicemen without a prescription. The targets
were opium and cocaine, but Indian hemp was on the list. The records do not say why;
the historian James Mills guesses at Indian troops convalescing in Brighton.

## The civilians forget

Eleven weeks later, on [28 July](/history/uk-defence-regulation-40b-1916/), Regulation
40B extended controls to everyone. It aimed at two things: drugs reaching soldiers
through civilians, and opium being smuggled out of Liverpool to China when it was
wanted for the wounded. Opium and cocaine were restricted. Hemp was not mentioned,
then or at the conference that drafted the rule.

The Dangerous Drugs Act of 1920 made the wartime rules permanent for morphine,
cocaine, ecgonine, heroin and medicinal opium. The committee that wrote the
regulations worried about forged prescriptions and about opiates for farm animals.
Cannabis was not discussed.

## Seen only abroad

Cannabis reached Whitehall as a foreign matter. In 1914 a British subject, Alex Tanti,
was tried by the consular court in Cairo for letting his café be used for smoking
hashish, after twice defying the Egyptian courts on the strength of his passport. And
from 1922 officials [tracked ten tons](/history/de-monfreid-ten-tons-1922/) of Indian
cannabis shipped by a French adventurer, until it turned into fertiliser.

## The police ask

From 1921 the Metropolitan Police pressed the Home Office to add hashish to the Act,
saying it had "practically the same effect as cocaine and morphine". An official
noted that "no sort of evidence is adduced that there is any real prevalence of the
hashish habit". The 1923 revision left it out again.

## Then, quickly

That August two café workers were
[prosecuted in London](/history/uk-london-hashish-acquittal-1923/) over what police
took for opium. It was hashish, and no law covered it. Within two years cannabis was
on the [poisons schedule](/history/uk-cannabis-poison-schedule-1925/), and in
[1928](/history/uk-ban-1928/) possession without a prescription became an offence.

The 1920 Act moves no score for the [United Kingdom](/country/gb/): cannabis stayed
as freely sold as before.
