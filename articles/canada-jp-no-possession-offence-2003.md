On 12 April 2002 a young person in Ontario, identified only as J.P., was charged
with possessing cannabis. His lawyer did not argue that he had a medical need.
He argued that on that day there was no offence of possessing cannabis in
Ontario at all, and three courts in a row agreed.

## How the ban stopped working

The argument went back to [R. v. Parker](/history/canada-parker-2000/), decided on
31 July 2000. The Court of Appeal for Ontario held that the ban on possessing
cannabis in section 4 of the Controlled Drugs and Substances Act was
unconstitutional unless it came with an acceptable medical exemption. It
declared the prohibition invalid and suspended that declaration for twelve
months, so the ban stayed in force until 31 July 2001.

On 30 July 2001, one day before the deadline, the federal government brought in
the [Marihuana Medical Access Regulations](/history/canada-medical-access-2001/).
They let authorised patients possess and grow cannabis. They did not touch the
section of the Act the court had struck down.

## Three courts, one answer

| Court | Date | Result |
|---|---|---|
| Ontario Court of Justice | 2 January 2003 | Charges dismissed |
| Superior Court of Justice | 16 May 2003 | Crown's appeal dismissed |
| Court of Appeal for Ontario | 7 October 2003 | Crown's appeal dismissed |

The first two courts held that regulations could not undo a declaration of
invalidity. The ban was dead from 31 July 2001 until Parliament re-enacted it,
and Parliament had not done so.

The Court of Appeal reached the same result by another route. Parliament did
not need to re-enact anything: the ban was valid whenever an adequate medical
exemption existed. In Hitzig, released the same morning, the court held that the
2001 regulations did not provide one. So on the day J.P. was charged, the
prohibition was "of no force or effect". The court then struck out the faulty
parts of the regulations in Hitzig, and that revived the ban from that day on.

## What it meant

For a little over two years, from 31 July 2001 to 7 October 2003, possessing
cannabis was not a valid crime in Ontario, Canada's most populous province. The
gap closed on the same day the Court of Appeal confirmed it.

It is a reminder that the story of [Canada's](/country/ca/) cannabis laws was
written largely by its courts. Patients won medical access in Parker and a
government supply through Hitzig, which also explains
[why the federal government sold cannabis grown in a mine](/history/canada-mine-cannabis-sales-2003/).
J.P.'s case showed what happened in between, when a government answered a court
with regulations that did not meet its test.
