On 29 October 2016 the Commonwealth's licensing provisions for medical cannabis
commenced. The Narcotic Drugs Act 1967 now allowed the federal government to
license the cultivation and manufacture of cannabis for medical and scientific
use, creating Australia's first lawful domestic supply.[@1]

## A supply-side reform

Parliament passed the Narcotic Drugs Amendment Act in February 2016, but the
licensing provisions did not commence until 29 October, after no proclamation was
made. The Act did not legalise possession for patients or create a pharmacy
product. It set up a licensing system for growers and manufacturers, leaving the
patient route to the therapeutic goods system.[@1]

## The patient route

Patients still needed approval through the Therapeutic Goods Administration's
Special Access Scheme or an authorised prescriber. Most products were unapproved
therapeutic goods: they had not been assessed by the TGA for safety or efficacy,
and patients generally paid privately.[@2] The Act did not create a patient
register, a national product list or a subsidised supply channel.

That gap explains the shape of the market that followed. Telehealth clinics and
private prescribers expanded faster than the licensed domestic industry, and
regulators later began reviewing closed-loop models where the prescriber,
referral and pharmacy were part of one business.[@2]

## A narrow legalisation

The 2016 Act is best understood as a supply-side legalisation. It made licensed
production possible and gave doctors a lawful route to prescribe, but it did not
make cannabis a normal medicine or a retail product. The access score moved
because a patient route existed; the possession and supply offences stayed.[@1,2]

The licensing date is often confused with the February assent. The Act was passed
in February, but the provisions that mattered for cultivation and manufacture
commenced on 29 October 2016.[@1]
