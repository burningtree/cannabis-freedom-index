Cambodia's parliament approved a ban on growing cannabis in December 1996,
with limited permission for researchers. People growing or buying drugs for
themselves faced prison or a fine, while prosecutors and judges could show
leniency toward very small quantities consumed in customary village practice.

The law drew several lines at once: between research and ordinary cultivation,
between personal consumption and supply, and between punishable use and the
traditional practices singled out for possible leniency.

## Growers and sellers came under broad controls

The law organised narcotic plants, drugs and manufacturing ingredients into
four lists. Cannabis and cannabis resin appeared in the most restricted list,
alongside substances drawn from international drug conventions.

Growing cannabis was expressly prohibited. Controls over the most restricted
list also reached production, transport, storage, purchase, use and supply.
Giving a drug away was covered as well as selling it, so supplying another
person without payment still fell within the prohibition.

These rules connected the field, the seller and the consumer. Restrictions
applied both to cultivation and to the transactions through which harvested
cannabis could reach other people.

## Researchers needed permission and records

The health minister could authorise activities for medical, scientific,
educational and scientific police research. The authorised quantities had to
stay within what the work required.

Researchers also had to record purchases, production, use and destruction,
retain their logbooks for at least ten years and report annually on quantities
used, destroyed or still held. Permission brought continuing responsibilities
for accounting for the material.

## Personal use could bring a short prison sentence

People who unlawfully stored, bought or grew drugs for their own consumption
faced six days to one month in prison, a fine of 100,000 to one million riels,
or both. Unlawful consumption carried the same range of penalties. Health
professionals faced doubled punishment terms.

The separate treatment of personal use mattered: the law addressed a person's
own consumption directly, alongside its wider controls on production and
distribution.

## Customary village use could receive leniency

Where an offence involved a very small quantity consumed as a customary habit,
the prosecutor could forgo charges. Once a case reached court, a judge could
waive punishment or issue a warning. The law described the relevant custom as
non-addictive consumption by villagers following longstanding ancestral practice.

That discretion made traditional use a consideration for the authorities
handling a case. The statutory text left the decision with prosecutors and
judges.

The [king's promulgation followed in January 1997](/history/cambodia-drug-law-promulgation-1997/).
Cambodia later adopted a [new drug law in 2012](/history/cambodia-drug-control-law-2012/),
continuing the [country's history](/country/kh/) of cannabis prohibition alongside
rules governing punishment and treatment of people who use drugs.
