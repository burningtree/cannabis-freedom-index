Cambodia's parliament approved a ban on growing cannabis in December 1996, with limited permission for researchers.[@1] People growing or buying drugs for themselves faced prison or a fine, while prosecutors and judges could show leniency toward very small quantities consumed in customary village practice.[@1]

The law drew several lines at once: between research and ordinary cultivation, between personal consumption and supply, and between punishable use and the traditional practices singled out for possible leniency.[@1]

## Growers and sellers came under broad controls

The law organised narcotic plants, drugs and manufacturing ingredients into four lists.[@2] Cannabis and cannabis resin appeared in the most restricted list, alongside substances drawn from international drug conventions.[@2]

Growing cannabis was expressly prohibited.[@1] Controls over the most restricted list also reached production, transport, storage, purchase, use and supply.[@1] Giving a drug away was covered as well as selling it, so supplying another person without payment still fell within the prohibition.[@1]

These rules connected the field, the seller and the consumer.[@1] Restrictions applied both to cultivation and to the transactions through which harvested cannabis could reach other people.[@1]

## Researchers needed permission and records

The health minister could authorise activities for medical, scientific, educational and scientific police research.[@1] The authorised quantities had to stay within what the work required.[@1]

Researchers also had to record purchases, production, use and destruction, retain their logbooks for at least ten years and report annually on quantities used, destroyed or still held.[@1] Permission brought continuing responsibilities for accounting for the material.[@1]

## Personal use could bring a short prison sentence

People who unlawfully stored, bought or grew drugs for their own consumption faced six days to one month in prison, a fine of 100,000 to one million riels, or both.[@1] Unlawful consumption carried the same range of penalties.[@1] Health professionals faced doubled punishment terms.[@1]

The separate treatment of personal use mattered: the law addressed a person's own consumption directly, alongside its wider controls on production and distribution.[@1]

## Customary village use could receive leniency

Where an offence involved a very small quantity consumed as a customary habit, the prosecutor could forgo charges.[@1] Once a case reached court, a judge could waive punishment or issue a warning.[@1] The law described the relevant custom as non-addictive consumption by villagers following longstanding ancestral practice.[@1]

That discretion made traditional use a consideration for the authorities handling a case.[@1] The statutory text left the decision with prosecutors and judges.[@1]

The [king's promulgation followed in January 1997](/history/cambodia-drug-law-promulgation-1997/).[@2] The [2005 amendments](/history/cambodia-personal-use-penalties-2005/) changed the personal-use penalty, before a [new law in 2012](/history/cambodia-drug-control-law-2012/) replaced the earlier legislation.[@3,4] In [Cambodia](/country/kh/), criminal controls continued alongside rules governing punishment and treatment.[@3]
