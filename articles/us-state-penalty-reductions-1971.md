In 1960 arrests and seizures for marijuana in the United States were at their lowest ever. Ten years later,
in 1970, an estimated 200,000 people were arrested for possession.
Within four years almost every state had cut the penalties it had raised in the 1950s.

## Too harsh to enforce

By 1967 marijuana had become part of campus life, and the felony laws that sent users to prison began to
look out of proportion. Police and prosecutors quietly diverted or dropped perhaps half of the cases
against young users. By 1971 only 13 per cent of judges in one survey said they would jail an adult for
possession, and 4 per cent a minor.

Virginia shows how change came. On 31 July 1969 Frank LaVarre, a twenty-year-old former student, was
[sentenced to twenty-five years](/history/us-lavarre-sentence-1969/), five suspended, for possessing
marijuana. After Life magazine used his case to illustrate the nation's drug laws, the governor pardoned
him on 2 January 1970. In April Virginia
[made a first possession offence a misdemeanour](/history/us-virginia-penalty-reduction-1970/), with up to
a year in jail.

## State by state

| Period | States reducing possession penalties |
|---|---|
| By the start of 1970 | 21 |
| During 1970 | 11 more |
| During 1971 | 15 more, and 14 cut again |

Bonnie and Whitebread call much of this tokenism: many states simply gave back to judges the discretion
over sentencing they had taken away in the 1950s. By the end of 1971 only Texas, Pennsylvania and Rhode
Island still had mandatory felony penalties for possession. Bills to cut penalties failed in New York and
California, and repeat offences stayed felonies in many states.

Congress moved too. The [Controlled Substances Act](/history/us-controlled-substances-act-1970/) of
October 1970 dropped the federal mandatory minimums of the [Boggs](/history/us-boggs-act-1951/) and
[Narcotic Control](/history/us-narcotic-control-act-1956/) acts.

## From smaller penalties to none

In March 1972 the national marijuana commission
[recommended decriminalising](/history/us-shafer-commission-1972/) possession for personal use. In April
the American Medical Association's committees
[agreed](/history/us-ama-marihuana-statement-1972/) that users should not be punished. In May the city
council of Ann Arbor, Michigan, [set a $5 fine](/history/us-ann-arbor-five-dollar-fine-1972/) for use,
possession or sale.

The next year went further. In May 1973 Texas [replaced](/history/us-texas-penalty-reform-1973/) what the
authors call the country's most severe marijuana law. In July [Oregon](/history/us-oregon-decriminalisation-1973/)
became the first state to treat possession of up to an ounce as a violation with a civil fine. In August
the uniform-law commissioners and the American Bar Association
[voted for decriminalisation](/history/us-lawyers-back-decriminalisation-1973/).
