Charles Baudelaire published *Les Paradis artificiels, opium et haschisch* in Paris in 1860. The Bibliothèque nationale de France records the edition issued by Poulet-Malassis et de Broise. The book placed hashish and opium inside an argument about imagination, desire and the will.

Its title can sound like a celebration of intoxication. Its judgement is much more troubled: the attraction of an apparently enlarged inner world becomes a reason to ask what is lost by obtaining it chemically.

## The wish for something beyond ordinary life

The opening of *Le Poème du haschisch* considers the desire to escape ordinary conditions and reach an intensified state. Baudelaire treats that desire as a serious human impulse. His account gives it literary and spiritual weight rather than presenting drug use only as a curiosity.

That framing helps explain the book's place in cannabis culture. Hashish becomes a subject through which a writer can discuss the limits of experience and creativity. It is not merely an exotic object in the background of a Paris gathering.

## Imagination and the will

The chapter *Morale* turns strongly against the promised shortcut. Baudelaire argues that hashish can enlarge the appearance of thought while undermining the will needed to put thought to use. The apparent gift of imagination is therefore paired with a loss of agency.

These are the author's judgements, expressed in the moral and religious language of his time. They should not be reported as modern clinical findings. The chapter even records Balzac examining a hashish preparation and declining it, an anecdote about resistance to surrendering control rather than a catalogue of famous consumers.

## An English opium narrative

The second major part, *Un mangeur d'opium*, works with Thomas De Quincey's writing. Baudelaire explicitly explains his intention to abridge and present that material. It should not be read as a straightforward account of his own opium consumption.

Hashish and opium occupy the same book but remain different substances and different literary sources. Collapsing them into one undifferentiated drug experience obscures how the argument is constructed.

## Literature, rather than a statute

The book belongs beside the [Club des Hashischins](/culture/traditions/club-des-hashischins/) in the history of French cannabis culture. Its publication changed neither possession rules nor medical access. It supplied a durable literary argument about intoxication and creative freedom.

Reading it through its own text preserves its ambivalence. Baudelaire understood the appeal of the artificial paradise while questioning whether the person who entered it retained the power to act outside it.
