Liberia's 1976 public-health law punished unauthorised cannabis possession as a misdemeanor, a criminal offence carrying a short prison term. Growing cannabis, allowing it on one's property or possessing it for sale brought the more serious classification of a first-degree felony.

## Possession and growing had different penalties

The law's definition covered the cannabis plant, its seeds, resin and preparations. Mature stalks, fibre, seed oil and cake, and sterilised seeds incapable of germination were excluded.

For someone holding cannabis without authorisation, the second-degree misdemeanor carried up to thirty days under the Penal Law's general sentencing rules. Growing cannabis was a first-degree felony, ordinarily carrying up to ten years. The cultivation rule also covered people who permitted growing or harvesting on property they owned, used or controlled.

Sale and possession with intent to sell were first-degree felonies too. For those offences, the law expressly ruled out probation and suspended sentences.

## Landholders had to remove wild plants

The duties extended to cannabis growing wild. A person who knew it was on their property had to destroy it or notify a police officer or local health officer.

If officials discovered unreported plants, the health minister could give the landholder ten days to remove them. After that, enforcement officers could enter and destroy them. The law therefore enlisted property holders in crop destruction alongside its criminal penalties.

Seized cannabis also faced destruction once it was no longer needed as evidence.

## Treatment could mean years under supervision

A separate procedure allowed anyone, including the person concerned, to ask a court to commit someone believed to be dependent on narcotics for treatment. Medical examination and a hearing formed part of that process, with rights to counsel and to question witnesses.

The treatment rules combined withdrawal care, psychiatric supervision, education and vocational training. A first move to outpatient care came after an initial year and within three years. Inpatient treatment and aftercare under one order could together last seven years.

## The later law raised the possession grade

The [2014 drug law](/history/liberia-controlled-drugs-act-2014/) expressly repealed these public-health criminal sections. It made personal possession of Schedule I drugs a second-degree felony while distinguishing cultivation for trafficking from cultivation otherwise.

It also created a separate drug-use offence with an option for treatment instead of conviction or punishment. [Liberia's later 2023 reform](/history/liberia-non-bailable-2023/) added explicit prison ranges and stricter bail rules, producing the rules described in the [current country profile](/country/lr/).
