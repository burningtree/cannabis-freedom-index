In 1941 Jamaica made prison compulsory for ganja. An amendment to the Dangerous Drugs Act that year imposed a mandatory one-year incarceration for cultivating, possessing or using the plant — no fine, no discretion — and the Rastafarian community, the island's main cultivators and users, became its principal targets.[@1,2]

## From £100 to mandatory time

The 1941 law capped a thirty-year escalation. In 1913 the Legislative Council, pressed by evangelical churches alarmed at East Indian labourers' ganja smoking, had banned cultivation, possession and use — a ratification of the previous year's Hague Opium Convention — at a £100 fine or twelve months with or without hard labour.[@1] The Depression years then hardened attitudes worldwide, and Jamaica's approach, blind to the customs of communities like the Rastafarians and Maroons who rejected European values and embraced ganja, turned openly hostile to them.[@1]

## Aimed at the lower strata

The campaign fell on the lower social strata: Rastafarians from those ranks grew and used ganja most, and the 1941 amendment was built for them.[@1] Independence changed nothing at first — the new political class inherited the colonial legislation and its anxieties, both major parties agreed still tougher laws were needed, and 1964 amendments gave police wider powers to raid cultivation premises and seize transport vehicles.[@1]

## 1972: discretion returns

The class edge of the law eventually told against it, and a 1972 amendment removed mandatory sentencing for cultivation, trading and possession, restoring judicial discretion and freeing people convicted under the harsher regime.[@1,2] But prosecution ground on: the prison population nearly doubled between 1972 and 2014, 15,000 people were arrested for possession in 2014 alone, and cannabis cases cost an estimated $64 million a year.[@2] The [country page](/country/jm/) follows the law from mandatory time to decriminalisation.
