Suriname’s replacement Drugs Act took effect on 20 January 1999, after being signed the previous February.[@1] It set a thirteen-year ceiling for deliberately possessing or using ordinary cannabis and life or up to twenty years for deliberately cultivating or importing it.[@2] The new penalties arrived through the replacement of a law that had governed drug offences since the 1950s.[@1]

## Why the government wanted a new law

The government argued that drug trafficking threatened the country through organised crime and corruption. Its explanatory memorandum linked the rewrite to Suriname’s recent treaty commitments and regional cooperation, while saying the changes were also needed for domestic reasons.[@3]

It considered simply amending the Opium Act, then chose a complete replacement. Ministers said a continuous new text would let the National Assembly examine the system more effectively and replace older colonial terminology.[@3] The National Anti-Drugs Council later described the old law as inadequate for the scale of production and international drug movements.[@4]

## Different conduct brought different ceilings

The Act prohibited growing Cannabis sativa. Cannabis plant material and customary solid mixtures of resin and plant parts went into List II, while other controlled substances appeared in List I.[@2]

For ordinary cannabis, deliberate possession, use and domestic supply fell under the thirteen-year tier. Deliberate cultivation and importing, exporting or transiting it fell under the life-or-twenty-year tier. The law also specified penalties for offences committed without deliberate intent.[@2]

This widened the distance from the original [1955 Act](/history/suriname-opium-order-1955/), whose deliberate-offence maximum had been four years.[@5] The comparison is between the original texts: the earlier law had also been amended before its repeal.[@1]

## Preparation could itself be punished

The replacement law reached conduct before a completed offence. It punished specified efforts to organise, assist or equip offences in the highest penalty tier, and conspiracy followed by action preparing the offence.[@2] The memorandum identified these additions as part of meeting the anti-trafficking convention’s requirements.[@3]

Medical and scientific permissions remained, including specified professional activities and possession for a legitimate medical need where the drugs had been acquired lawfully.[@2]

## Passage and commencement were separate

President Jules Wijdenbosch signed the Act on 12 February 1998, but its final article left the starting date to a later decision.[@2] The National Assembly’s annotated text identifies 20 January 1999 as commencement. Existing permissions under the old Act temporarily survived that replacement.[@1] The [Suriname timeline](/country/sr/#history) now records both stages, keeping the signing date distinct from the day the new rules began.
