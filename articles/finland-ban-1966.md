Finland first made drug use punishable in 1966, and the 1972 Narcotics Act
carried full drug control into the criminal law.[@1,2] The new rules made
Finnish drug policy one of the strictest in Europe, and parliament decided
the question by drawing lots.[@2,3]

## A strict start

Drugs reached young Finns in the mid-1960s, and the police, with almost no
experience of drug crime, had to learn investigation from scratch.[@3] The
1966 legislation outlawed the use of illicit drugs.[@1] A narcotics squad
was formed at the National Bureau of Investigation in 1968 and another at
the Helsinki Police Department the year after, the latter with the
country's first narcotics dog.[@3] Alcohol moved the other way: the sale of
medium-strength beer in grocery stores was legalised in 1969, while the
drug squads spread across the country.[@3]

## Decided by drawing lots

The 1972 bill split parliament over whether using drugs should be a crime
at all.[@2] The government had proposed leaving use unpunished, and its Commerce
Committee backed that lenient line, but the Legal Affairs Committee wanted
criminalisation, and the Grand Committee backed it — after drawing
lots.[@2] In the decisive vote the government's bill fell by 92 to 80, and
using drugs became a narcotics offence, something few countries had
done.[@2]

## Prevention as the point

Supporters framed criminalisation as prevention: parliament should show
young people that society did not accept drug use, and the threat of
punishment would deter experimenting.[@2] The ban was conceived as a
principled stand meant to foster anti-drug attitudes.[@1] The left objected
that users were sick people needing help, not criminals, seeing the drug
problem as a consequence of other social ills.[@2] Once the lines were drawn, open
criticism of criminalisation vanished, even on the left, replaced by
reluctance to question the control policy.[@2] Prosecutors later gained room
to drop cases: the 1993 Penal Code chapter let charges or punishment be
waived where the act was not liable to weaken respect for the law.[@1]

## The criminal line held

The criminal line held for decades, with a waiver power available to
prosecutors while the offence itself stayed on the books.[@1] The timeline runs
through the [1972 criminalisation of use](/history/finland-use-criminalised-1972/);
the [Finland profile](/country/fi/) follows the later medical and reform
debates.[@1]
