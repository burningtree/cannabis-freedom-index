Hong Kong's lawmakers voted on 5 April 1928 to prohibit possession of the
cannabis plant or any part of it. Medicinal tincture made from cannabis would
come under dangerous-drug controls. The colonial government was responding to
an international drug treaty, while choosing a broader possession ban at home.

## A treaty brought cannabis into the debate

Hong Kong already had a dangerous-drug law dating from 1923. The government
wanted to amend it to meet the
[1925 Geneva Convention](/history/geneva-opium-convention-1925/), which had
added Indian hemp to international drug controls.

Attorney General Sir Joseph Horsford Kemp introduced the changes. He told
legislators that some existing rules already met the treaty's requirements,
while others needed tightening. The government also planned to bring its drug
regulations into line with those used in England.

Kemp presented the bill as part of a wider effort to close gaps in control
over dangerous drugs. Cannabis was the important addition he singled out
when explaining why the law needed to change.

## The government wanted the plant prohibited

Kemp said Hong Kong would go “even further than the Convention” by prohibiting
possession of the plant and all its parts. For someone holding cannabis, that
meant the proposed restriction reached the material itself, beyond controls
on particular forms of drug commerce.

He drew a separate line for medicinal tincture. Because that preparation was
used as medicine, it would enter the dangerous-drug list, while the plant
would be prohibited. The government's plan therefore distinguished a cannabis
medicine from possession of the plant from which it came.

## Drug traders faced tighter border rules too

The bill also widened the meaning of importing and exporting drugs. The
government wanted the rules to cover air transport and drugs carried through
the post in transit.

Its explanation even considered a packet thrown across the land frontier.
Words dealing only with carriage might leave room for an argument that such
an act escaped the law, so the government proposed broader wording. These
examples show the practical border problems officials wanted the drug law
to reach, alongside the new cannabis controls.

## The vote carried the government's plan through

The Legislative Council passed the bill without changing it. Kemp moved its
passage and the Colonial Secretary supported it, carrying the government's
proposed cannabis distinction through the legislature.

Four decades later, Hong Kong adopted a
[replacement drug law](/history/hong-kong-dangerous-drugs-ordinance-1968/).
That government argued for harsher treatment of traffickers and greater
leniency toward people possessing drugs for personal consumption. Cannabis
remained controlled as [Hong Kong's drug policy](/country/hk/) developed around
the different treatment of suppliers and users.
