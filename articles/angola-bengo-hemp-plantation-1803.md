In 1803, near the Bengo river in what is now Angola, there stood a hemp plantation promising a good harvest — proof, to the Portuguese administration, that the land suited the crop.[@2] The same plant was already in African hands: Black cultivators grew it, smoked it dry, and intoxicated themselves, in the administrator's words, "in a terrible and disastrous way".[@2]

## Fibre hopes, smoking reality

The plantation embodied a colonial hope: that Angola could grow European-style fibre hemp.[@1] The Africans' use embodied something else entirely — a smoking culture the Portuguese could only describe through the vocabulary of damage.[@1] Satisfaction at the promising harvest and horror at African smoking sit in the same paragraph: one plant, two economies.[@1][@2] Duvall renders their phrase as a "terrible and disastrous" use of *canhâmo*, the Portuguese word for European hemp, now stretched to cover a drug plant.[@1]

## A crop among crops

Saldanha's page sets the plantation in a working foodscape: millet ground into fuba, sugarcane, sweet cassava as the people's staple, yams and beans around the town — and the hemp plot among them.[@2] Cannabis travelled inside ordinary farming.[@1] By 1832 a French traveller found it common around Bailundo, an inland slave-market town, grown as part of cropping systems farmers kept adapting to drought, warfare and population shifts — planted as widely as manioc, maize, calabash, melons, tomatoes and tobacco, though rarely in abundance.[@1]

## The earliest Atlantic record

Duvall treats the 1803 notice as the start of the plant's documented history in western Central Africa.[@1] It is also the earliest documented presence of cannabis in the tropical Atlantic world, centred on the Luanda hinterland where Portuguese administrators first confronted large-scale African cultivation for smoking rather than fibre.[@1]

The record then goes quiet for half a century until [Livingstone's 1857 observations](/history/angola-livingstone-observation-1857/) pick up the story further inland.[@1] The full arc continues on [Angola's page](/country/ao/).[@1]
