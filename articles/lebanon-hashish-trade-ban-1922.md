In January 1922 the French mandate authorities prohibited the trade and
possession of hashish in Lebanon, months after the same ban in
Palestine.[@1] Growing was not covered; the production ban followed in
1926.[@1,2] Lebanon's first cannabis rules came not from the drug
convention itself, but from the mandate system wrapped around it.[@1]

## Prohibition through the mandate

The 1925 Geneva convention only invited states to control cannabis trade,
not to prohibit it outright.[@1] In Syria and Lebanon the convention
combined with mandate obligations to produce outright prohibition: the
mandate charters pressed for drug-treaty ratification, and France ratified
the 1925 convention in both territories' name.[@1] Enforcement then fell
under two Genevan bodies at once, the Opium Advisory Committee and the
Permanent Mandates Commission.[@1]

## A reluctant enforcer

The High Commission, the French institution ruling the mandate, did not
rush. France had never ratified the 1912 convention for the mandates, its administration escaped Mandates
Commission review until 1924, and the hashish trade's patrons were often
the colonial administration's own upper-class clients, while the crop
assured peasant livelihoods.[@1] An October 1925 order finally banned
cultivation in the Bekaa from January 1926, in French: any hashish
sowing there was forbidden.[@1,3]

## Nominal from the start

The bans were nominal. By 1928 the Bekaa was producing some 60 tons, 90
percent of it bound for Egypt, and the 1926 production ban went unenforced
for lack of territorial presence.[@4,2] From January
1930 Egypt's Russell Pasha singled out Syrian and Lebanese production as
priority targets at the Opium Advisory Committee, and Egyptian pressure
kept the French administration at least appearing to enforce.[@1]
Landowning politicians with cannabis farms made real enforcement
impossible.[@1] A 1939 French police list of hashish producers named
members of parliament, a finance minister and local notables.[@4]

## The pattern set

The mandate years fixed Lebanon's posture for decades: prohibition on
paper, cultivation continuing in the Bekaa. Lebanon's open rejection of the
League's agenda before the war led the independent state to turn a blind
eye to the plantations.[@1] The timeline runs through the
[1926 production ban](/history/lebanon-hashish-ban-1926/) and the
[1946 cultivation law](/history/lebanon-cultivation-law-1946/); the
[Lebanon profile](/country/lb/) follows the later history.[@2]
