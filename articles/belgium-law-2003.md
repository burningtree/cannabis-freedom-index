On 2 June 2003 Belgium told its police to stand down — a little. The Law of 3 May 2003, in force on publication, said an adult's personal-use quantity of cannabis, held without public nuisance or problematic use, required only police registration: no report to the prosecutor.[@1] Possession stayed an offence, but the paper trail stopped at the station.[@1]

## Three grams and one plant

The accompanying prosecution guideline of 16 May gave the rule numbers: up to 3 grams, or one cultivated plant, counted as personal use absent indications of sale or trafficking.[@2] Below those lines the police wrote a simplified report and walked away; above them, or with nuisance or aggravating circumstances, came full prosecution.[@3]

## The court that unpicked it

The compromise did not survive the Court of Arbitration. In judgment 158/2004 the court annulled the reporting exception, finding its criteria — nuisance, problematic use — too vague to delimit police discretion.[@2] Rather than criminalising more, the government answered with a new ministerial directive in February 2005 that treated up to 3 grams and one plant as the lowest prosecution priority nationwide.[@3]

## What the procedure looks like

The 2005 directive standardised the station-house routine: a simplified report, a fine in the low hundreds of euros for a first case, and no prosecutor unless the holder exceeded the user amount, caused a nuisance, or triggered an aggravating circumstance — repeated possession within a year, use around minors, or dealing.[@3] The full offence still carries three months to a year and fines up to 800,000 euros for those cases.[@3]

The 2003 lines still define [Belgium's](/country/be/) cannabis law: a fine of about 120 to 200 euros for a first low-priority case, rising to 208 to 400 for a second, and eight days to a month inside for a repeat — while prosecutors may still bring charges, with reasons, even below the lines.[@3]
