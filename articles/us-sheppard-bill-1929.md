On 30 October 1929 Senator Sheppard of Texas introduced a bill, S. 2075, to add cannabis to the federal
Narcotic Drugs Import and Export Act. It never reached a vote. The officials who killed it were not
defenders of marijuana. They feared that a federal marijuana law would endanger the whole of federal drug
control.

## Pressure from Congress

By the late 1920s local police and newspapers in Texas, Colorado and Louisiana were drawing attention to
marijuana. In December 1928 a New Orleans congressman asked the Bureau of Prohibition to draft a law after a
constituent wrote that marijuana was being sold to schoolchildren. Colorado's Senator Phipps wanted it put
in the Harrison Narcotic Act. In January 1929 Congress passed the
[narcotic farms act](/history/us-narcotic-farms-act-1929/), whose definition of habit-forming drugs
included Indian hemp, the first time federal law classed cannabis with the narcotics. Senators began asking
why, if marijuana was a narcotic, it was missing from the main drug laws.

## Why Washington said no

The surgeon general's report of 1929, written at Phipps's request, called marijuana "definitely" a narcotic
and repeated claims about crime and madness without any new study. So the medical authorities had no
objection to a ban. The objections were legal.

| Proposal | The officials' objection |
|---|---|
| Add cannabis to the Harrison Act | That act was a tax law upheld by the Supreme Court only five to four in 1919. A tax on medical cannabis would raise almost no revenue and might be struck down, taking the rest of the act with it |
| Add cannabis to the Import and Export Act (S. 2075) | That act presumed that anyone holding a drug had imported it illegally, which was irrational for a plant growing all over the country |

The bureau's legal adviser, A. L. Tennyson, warned that a court defeat on cannabis could undo the same
presumption for opium and cocaine. Others in the bureau doubted the horror stories. The drug industry and
the American Medical Association objected too; the AMA's William Woodward answered a questionnaire from
Harry Anslinger with open sarcasm.

## The bill dies

On 28 June 1930 Anslinger, then secretary of the Federal Narcotics Control Board, sent the officials'
objection to the Senate Finance Committee, adding that a separate law banning imports and exports would be
acceptable. S. 2075 died in committee. Weeks later Anslinger became head of the new
[Federal Bureau of Narcotics](/history/us-federal-bureau-of-narcotics-1930/). He chose to pursue a ban
through the states instead, by way of the
[Uniform State Narcotic Drug Act](/history/us-uniform-state-narcotic-act-1932/), and to put the federal
case to Congress "at the appropriate time". That time came in 1937, when the Treasury
[introduced its marijuana tax bill](/history/us-tax-bill-introduced-1937/).
