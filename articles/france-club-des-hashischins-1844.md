The Club des Hashischins is conventionally dated to 1844, but its beginnings are less precise than the name suggests. Historian [Erwan Pointeau-Lagadec](https://isor.pantheonsorbonne.fr/publications/club-hachichins-mythe-realite) identifies documented cannabis experiments at Paris's Hôtel Pimodan between 1845 and 1849. The record supports gatherings among friends, physicians and artists; it does not automatically support the later image of a formal secret society.

The familiar founding date should therefore be read as a conventional marker for a circle, rather than a verified inauguration ceremony.

## An experiment and a dinner

Jacques-Joseph Moreau de Tours brought medical questions about altered perception into this artistic setting. His [1845 book on hashish and mental alienation](/history/france-moreau-du-hachisch-1845/) is a separate document of that inquiry. The social gatherings also supplied material for literature.

In [Gautier's account](https://fr.wikisource.org/wiki/Le_Club_des_hachichins), the narrator enters a richly decorated old mansion, receives green paste and joins a dinner. This ingested preparation matters: the scene is not an account of a room full of people smoking joints. Its changing perceptions unfold through taste, conversation, objects and the passage of time.

## A story makes the club

Gautier's text appeared in the *Revue des Deux Mondes* in February 1846. The fantastic transformations give the gathering a powerful literary identity. The surroundings become unstable and the experience passes between amusement and distress.

That narrative is evidence of how the experience was represented, not a clinical transcript. Its extravagance cannot establish what happened to every participant or what cannabis would do to every reader. Gautier's tale helped make a handful of experiments seem like a much larger institution.

## Names and legends

Pointeau-Lagadec's research distinguishes the documented meetings from the myths around them. The distinction also cautions against treating every later list of famous members as a reliable attendance register.

Gautier repeats a story about the Assassins and the Old Man of the Mountain. This belongs to the nineteenth-century literary framing of hashish through an imagined East; it is not independent evidence that cannabis caused medieval assassinations.

## The surviving record

The [tradition entry](/culture/traditions/club-des-hashischins/) follows the preparation, place and literary setting more fully. Baudelaire's later [Les Paradis artificiels](/history/france-paradis-artificiels-1860/) belongs to its cultural afterlife, rather than proving that the same club continued.

What survives is a combination of social practice and writing. Reading the two together preserves the genuine experiments while avoiding the mistake of turning a memorable title into an organisation more definite than the evidence allows.
