In 2018, Kibra MP [Ken Okoth](/culture/people/ken-okoth/) sought assistance preparing a Marijuana Control Bill for publication in Kenya's National Assembly. Reporting on his letter to Speaker Justin Muturi described an attempt to replace prohibition with a regulated cannabis sector.

The initiative was a proposal. It did not itself authorise cultivation, erase convictions or create a legal market.

## More than a medical exception

The reported plan included registration of participants in cultivation, production and manufacture, as well as users. It connected medical research with industrial, textile and other uses of cannabis and hemp.

Protection of children formed part of the argument for rules governing the sector. The idea was not simply that every existing restriction should disappear, but that a different system could distinguish permitted activities from prohibited ones.

## Convictions and economic opportunity

Okoth also proposed relief for people with criminal records for cannabis use and linked the industry with employment and taxation. These objectives addressed both the future market and the continuing effects of earlier prosecutions.

Anthropologist Neil Carrier places the initiative alongside Okoth's earlier economic argument about replacing struggling sugarcane production with cannabis. The proposed benefits remained political claims about a possible industry, rather than measured outcomes of an enacted Kenyan programme.

Carrier's wider discussion also raises a question about market access: a licensing system can benefit well-financed firms while leaving traditional small producers outside it. That possibility explains why the design of regulation matters, even when reformers agree that prohibition should change.

## A separate parliamentary route

Gwada Ogot had pursued a [petition to the Senate](/history/kenya-legalisation-petition-2015/), where debate brought medicinal and economic arguments into conflict with strong moral objections. Okoth's request concerned preparation of a bill in the National Assembly. The two initiatives should not be mistaken for different stages of one enacted measure.

## What followed

Carrier records that Okoth's effort ended with his death in 2019. The sources examined here do not establish a completed parliamentary reading or passage of the proposed text.

The event is therefore listed as debate, with no change to Kenya's score. The 2018 chronology follows Carrier's account; the migrated news page has a conflicting later display date, which is retained as a source issue rather than used to invent a new legislative event.
