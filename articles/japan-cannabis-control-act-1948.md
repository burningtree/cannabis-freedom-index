Japan banned a plant it had grown for thousands of years, and for 76 years the ban
had a hole in it: using cannabis was not an offence.

## An everyday crop

Hemp was cultivated in Japan from the Jōmon period. By the third century hemp clothing
was common, and in 645 it was among the goods taxed by the Yamato court. Shinto priests
used its fibre for ritual cleansing, as they still do. Cotton slowly replaced it as
the main fibre crop in the Meiji period, but in the early twentieth century
cannabis-based remedies for insomnia and muscle pain were still sold in drug stores,
and in the Second World War hemp was a strategic crop for rope and parachute cord.

## The law of July 1948

The Cannabis Control Law was passed in July 1948, during the American occupation. It
banned possession and made growing subject to a licence. Months earlier Emperor
Hirohito had assured farmers that growing hemp for industrial use could continue.
Historians have speculated that American petrochemical interests wanted hemp
restricted to open the market for nylon and polyester; that remains speculation.

## The missing offence

The law did not punish consumption. The explanation usually given is that this
shielded hemp farmers, who might breathe in their crop without meaning to.

Fewer than 100 licences have gone to Shinto shrines, which grow small amounts for
ritual use.

## Closing the gap

The Supreme Court [upheld the law](/history/japan-supreme-court-upholds-ban-1985/) in
1985. In 2021 a health ministry panel recommended making consumption a crime, and
parliament [passed the change](/history/japan-reform-passed-2023/) in December 2023.
Since [12 December 2024](/history/japan-use-criminalised-2024/) use itself is an
offence and the maximum for possession is seven years. The same reform allowed
cannabis-derived medicines.

In the index the 1948 law took [Japan](/country/jp/) from an open country to a closed
one in a single step. Today it scores 0 for possession, growing, sharing, use and
access.
