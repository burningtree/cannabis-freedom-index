In Bengal the British taxed cannabis for almost a century before they criminalised it. From 1793 the East India Company made retailers buy licences, in 1854 it taxed wholesalers instead, and in 1876 it licensed the growers themselves — building one of the most closely watched drug economies in the empire.[@1] The cultivators of the Ganja Mahal spent the whole period finding ways around it.[@1]

## A district organised around one crop

By the 1870s Bengal's ganja came from the Ganja Mahal, a tract straddling Bogra, Dinagepore and Rajshahye totalling about 60,000 acres.[@1] In 1876 the Board of Revenue sent deputy tax collector Hem Chunder Kerr to survey a trade it realised it barely understood, and his report remains the most detailed account of a cannabis-producing economy available to historians.[@1] Ganja fitted into a four-year rotation — sown once every four years, reaped in March, followed by jute, mustard, potato, turmeric, tobacco, rice and ginger — and demanded heavy labour: repeated ploughings, fresh earth from ditches, weeded borders as manure.[@1]

## Three generations of licences

Between 1793 and the 1850s the Company wanted steady income, not control: a retailer paid the local officer for a licence, then bought as much as he liked and sold it where he liked.[@1] By mid-century the money looked bigger at wholesale, so from 1854 the district collector assessed the wholesaler's warehouse stocks and taxed what retailers took from him.[@1] The 1876 licences closed the remaining gaps: peasants licensed to cultivate under the collector's eye, licences to store stated amounts, export permits fixing how much a wholesaler could carry home, and every authorised bale travelling with an excise stamp after weighing and searching.[@1] Nowgong staff toured the tract estimating acreage and watching crops mature.[@1]

## The cultivators fought back

Kerr found declared and measured acreage 998 bighas apart — a yearly revenue loss near Rs 479,000 — because too-few supervisors depended on the growers' own figures.[@1] Manufactured ganja was scattered across cowsheds, verandahs and open halls rather than gathered in one searchable store.[@1] What supervisors might find could vanish before their visit, or burn: sheds were cheap to replace, a couple of bales equalled over Rs 212 in duty, and fire covered a midnight sale.[@1] Smugglers worked the year-round navigable Jabuna until Kerr proposed a guard boat and a preventive station at Sahebgunge on the Atraie.[@1]

## Codified in 1878

The Bengal Excise Act of 1878 wrote the system into statute: no manufacture or cultivation without the Collector's licence, no sale without one, retail capped at a quarter-ser of ganja or bhang and five tolas of charas, growers selling only to authorised buyers.[@2] Cultivation licences were free, confined to about 64 square miles under Rajshahi's Collector, averaging 740 acres, with three days' notice before cutting.[@2] The licensed trade that began with a retailer's permit in 1793 ran on through the [1876 licences](/history/bangladesh-bengal-ganja-licences-1876/) and the [1878 Act](/history/bangladesh-bengal-excise-act-1878/).[@1,2] The later chapters play out on [Bangladesh's](/country/bd/) and [India's](/country/in/) pages.
