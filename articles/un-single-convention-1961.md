One treaty explains why cannabis law looks so alike from country to country. It was
negotiated in two months in New York in 1961, and it classed cannabis with heroin.

## How cannabis was judged

By the 1950s the United Nations had settled its view. The World Health Organization's
experts [declared](/history/who-no-medical-value-1954/) cannabis preparations
practically obsolete, and a [1955 report](/history/who-wolff-report-1955/) for the
WHO called the drug dangerous from every point of view. On that basis the Commission
on Narcotic Drugs concluded that cannabis had no medical value. The 1958 draft of the
new treaty carried a section headed "prohibition of cannabis".

## The conference

Representatives of 73 states met from 24 January to 25 March 1961. The outright ban
did not survive. India defended bhang, a drink made from the leaves, as "far less
harmful than alcohol". Pakistan and Burma also objected, and the delegates at one
point discussed the cannabis given to working elephants in the timber trade. Others
wanted to keep cannabis in medicines.

The compromise was in the definition. "Cannabis" in the treaty means the flowering
tops; leaves and seeds are left out, which let bhang continue.

## What it requires

- **Schedules:** cannabis and its resin were placed in Schedule I, with the most
  addictive drugs, and also in Schedule IV, the strictest list, reserved for
  substances with particularly dangerous properties.
- **Purpose:** production, trade and use must be limited to medical and scientific
  purposes.
- **Traditional use:** countries could keep it for a transitional period of at most
  25 years.
- **Hemp:** growing the plant only for fibre and seed is outside the treaty.
- **Penalties:** Article 36 requires serious offences to be punished. Whether simple
  possession for personal use must be a crime has been argued ever since.

The treaty was signed on 30 March 1961 and
[took effect](/history/un-single-convention-in-force-1964/) on 13 December 1964, after
forty ratifications. It has 186 parties.

## What it did

Through the 1960s and 1970s governments rewrote their drug laws to match, which is
why so many national prohibitions date from those years. The transitional period for
India, Nepal, Pakistan and Bangladesh
[ran out in 1989](/history/single-convention-phase-out-1989/).

The treaty has proved almost impossible to change. The Dutch government said in
[1974](/history/netherlands-memorandum-1974/) that it would legalise cannabis if the
convention allowed it, and settled for not prosecuting. Later reforms took the same
shape: decriminalisation, tolerance, court rulings. Only in
[2020](/history/un-cannabis-rescheduled-2020/) did the UN remove cannabis from
Schedule IV, by 27 votes to 25, and it stays in Schedule I.
[Uruguay](/history/uruguay-legalisation-2013/) legalised in open breach of it, and
[Canada](/history/canada-cannabis-act-2018/) followed.

The index lists the convention for context. It binds governments, so no score moves
until a national law does.
