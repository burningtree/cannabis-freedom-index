In September 1933 Sweden put prison into its drug law. The royal kungörelse of 16 September 1933 on narcotic substances and preparations introduced imprisonment for aggravated circumstances across a range of drug offences — and penalised doctors, dentists and veterinarians who exceeded their authority to prescribe narcotics.[@1]

## The 1930 baseline

Cannabis had entered special regulation three years earlier. The kungörelse of 17 October 1930 on trade in opium and other narcotics brought coca leaves, cannabis and several opium derivatives under the regime: processed opium and cannabis resin (haschisch) could not be imported, exported, manufactured, possessed or offered for sale, and unlawful possession — holding narcotics without special right — drew fines.[@1]

## Why 1933, and why Geneva

Coca leaves and cannabis had sat outside the Hague Convention's controls; a new Geneva convention in 1925 added them, and the 1930 kungörelse carried that extension into Swedish law.[@2] The 1933 sharpening kept the structure and raised the stakes: prison where aggravating circumstances existed, prescriber liability, and power for the Medical Board to issue rules on dispensing prescriptions written where the goods were plainly not meant for medical use.[@1,2]

## Fines to prison in three years

The arc is short: fines for unauthorised possession in 1930, prison for aggravated cases in 1933, all inside royal ordinances rather than statute.[@1] The [country page](/country/se/) follows the controls from these kungörelser to the modern drug-punishment law.
