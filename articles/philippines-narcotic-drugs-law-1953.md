The Philippines' first national controls on marijuana came on 20 June 1953, when Republic Act 953, the Narcotic Drugs Law, was approved and took effect. It is a tax and registration statute, and it names "marihuana" beside opium, coca leaves and opium poppies.

## A system of stamps and forms

The Act did not simply ban the drugs it listed. It built a closed channel for them.

- **Registration.** Anyone importing, making, selling or dispensing a covered drug had to register each January with the Collector of Internal Revenue and pay a fixed yearly tax, from 72 pesos for importers and manufacturers down to 6 pesos for physicians and dentists.
- **Order forms.** A sale or gift needed a written order on an official form, kept by both sides for five years. Dispensing by a physician and filling a prescription were exempt.
- **Transfer tax.** A transfer of marihuana or opium poppies under an order form was taxed at two pesos for every 30 grams to a registered person, and at 200 pesos to an unregistered one.

## What it meant for an ordinary person

Section 10 made it unlawful for an unregistered person to possess or control a covered drug, and treated possession as presumptive evidence of a violation.

| Offence | Penalty under Section 11 |
|---|---|
| Any violation of the Act | a fine of up to 5,000 pesos, up to five years in prison, or both |
| Third or later offence of selling, importing or exporting | a fine of up to 10,000 pesos, up to twenty years, or both |

Section 15 went further for the plant itself. It declared as national policy that growing marihuana, which it also calls Indian hemp, and the opium poppy is prohibited.

The Act says nothing about addicts or treatment. It left the older provisions of the Revised Penal Code in place, and in [1958](/history/philippines-marijuana-possession-1958/) Congress wrote Indian hemp into those as well.

## Why the index moved

The [1953 law](/history/philippines-narcotic-drugs-law-1953/) is the first step in the [Philippines'](/country/ph/) score, from the unregulated state to 7. Possession, cultivation and sharing fall to 0, since each could now bring years in prison, and legal access disappears. Consumption is left at 3 and enforcement at 2, because the Act punished possession and trade but did not yet name use as an offence.
