In the mid-1800s people in Ottoman Tunisia smoked cannabis openly, but in
1875 its cultivation was banned.[@1] Growing returned under state control:
from 1884 farming was legal only with a license and only in certain parts of
the country, and the state controlled sales for decades, down to five-gram
boxes that remained standard into the 1950s.[@1]

## Smoked openly

Cannabis in the Maghreb went by the names kif and takruri, words that also
meant the herbal material prepared for smoking.[@1] People smoked the
locally grown flowering tops, often mixed with tobacco, in dry pipes,
especially the long-stemmed sebsi with its small red clay bowl.[@1] Use cut
across classes: precolonial leaders smoked while in power, as did their
soldiers and slaves.[@1] Takruri records a trans-Saharan journey: the word
means "from Takrur", a name for the Sudan region, and reached North Africa
from south of the Sahara rather than across the Mediterranean, first
appearing in print in Algeria in the late 1700s and surviving as a plant name
only in Algeria and Tunisia.[@1] Resin was unknown; the earliest trace of
cannabis resin in the region is an 1884 Tunisian ban on importing shira, a
Syrian word for the substance.[@1]

## A ban, then a monopoly

The 1875 ban ended open cultivation.[@1] Nine years later the tobacco
monopoly brought cannabis back as a licensed crop, farmed only where
permitted, with the state running sales to limit the farmland spent on the
crop and to stop prepared products being adulterated.[@1] Takruri joined the
monopoly in 1890, with prices set separately for flower heads and mixed
herbal material.[@1] From 1902 the monopoly sold a single product, trimmed
and chopped flower heads in five-gram boxes, the common preparation across
the Maghreb.[@1] In the 1910s some five thousand kilograms were sold each
year, and the monopoly lasted into the 1950s.[@1]

## Farming replaces imports

Tunisia first relied on imports from around Constantine in Algeria, which is
why early restrictions on home farming stayed loose.[@1] Over the following
decades the country became self-sufficient, growing its own supply under the
monopoly instead.[@1] The story runs through the
[first decree on Indian hemp and takrouri of 1884](/history/tunisia-hemp-decree-1884/),
which laid down the prohibitions and regulations the monopoly rested on; the
[Tunisia profile](/country/tn/) follows the later history.[@1]
