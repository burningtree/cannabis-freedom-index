Thirty-seven opposition deputies asked Chile's Constitutional Court to stop part
of a drug-law reform that Congress had already approved. Their filing, dated 13
April 2026, argued that the provision was too vague and could expose cannabis
users and medical growers to trafficking penalties.

## A new test for small amounts

The challenged provision came from merged bills 15.347-07 and 16.430-07. It would
allow trafficking penalties to apply even when a person held a small quantity if
the drug was capable of causing serious toxic effects or considerable harm to
health.

That would shift the legal test away from quantity alone. According to
*BioBioChile*, the deputies objected that the text did not clearly exclude acts
connected with personal or medical cannabis use. Cannabis remained classified as
a substance capable of producing serious harm under Decree 867, making the
wording especially important for people who use or grow it.

## The constitutional challenge

The deputies asked the court to review numeral 1 of the reform's sole article.
They said it could let conduct associated with consumption be treated as
trafficking and argued that its open-ended language failed to tell people clearly
which acts would be criminal.

Their filing invoked six constitutional protections. The objections described by
*BioBioChile* included proportionality, privacy in the home and the risk that the
rule would deter people from pursuing medical treatment involving cannabis. The
challenge was therefore about both the severity of the possible punishment and
the uncertainty over who could face it.

## What the court could do

The Constitutional Court first had to decide whether the filing was admissible.
If accepted, it would then consider the substance of the deputies' claims. A
successful challenge would prevent the disputed provision from becoming law in
that form.

At the time of the filing, the reform had passed Congress but had not yet been
promulgated. The challenge did not itself repeal an existing rule or create a new
right for cannabis users; it opened another stage in deciding whether the tougher
language could take effect.
