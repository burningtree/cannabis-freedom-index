Sultan al-Zahir Baybars prohibited hashish during his rule over Mamluk Egypt and Syria. The episode belongs to a wider campaign against activities condemned on religious and moral grounds. It is among the earliest government prohibitions described by historians, although the frequently repeated year 1265 is not secure.

## A ruler’s religious policy

Hatim Muhammad Mahamid’s study places the restrictions within Baybars’s efforts to act as an Islamic ruler. His authority involved cooperation with religious scholars as well as military and administrative power. Regulating conduct was one expression of that authority.

The study describes campaigns against wine, taverns and other condemned activities across Egypt and Syria. Hashish appears within this larger programme. It was not presented as a separate modern drug-control system with the definitions, schedules and licensing arrangements familiar from twentieth-century legislation.

## What was prohibited

Mahamid reports prohibitions of wine and hashish under the events of 664 AH/1266 CE. His account also records an order against the hashish trade and disciplinary measures against users and traders. The cited medieval histories describe consumption in Syria and other places, including coastal areas.

The distinction between the drugs matters when reading penalties. A threat of hanging quoted in the same discussion concerns wine. It cannot automatically be transferred to cannabis users. The evidence supports a prohibition and disciplinary enforcement; it does not establish a single verified cannabis sentencing tariff for every part of the sultanate.

## The problem with 1265

Sami Hamarneh’s earlier history explicitly places a Damascus hashish prohibition in 1265. However, the preceding passage equates a date in 664 AH with that CE year, creating a chronological problem. Mahamid’s later treatment places the broad prohibition in 1266.

The chronology here follows 1266. This is a correction of the recorded year, rather than evidence that a different new decree has been discovered. The precise relationship between the Damascus order and the wider campaigns still requires comparison of the cited Arabic chronicles.

## Prohibition without a continuous modern regime

Hamarneh also reports continued clandestine consumption. A ruler’s command and complete disappearance of a practice are different historical claims.

This event therefore records a Mamluk prohibition associated with the history of [Syria](/country/sy/). It does not imply that the law or its enforcement remained unchanged until the modern Syrian state. The earlier [Cafour garden destruction](/history/egypt-cafour-cannabis-destruction-1253/) supplies another kind of evidence: a campaign against a particular growing place, with its own limits of scope and dating.
