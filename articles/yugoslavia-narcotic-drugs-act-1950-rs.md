In January 1950 Yugoslavia put narcotic drugs under state control. The federal Act on
Narcotic Drugs, promulgated by the Presidium of the National Assembly, listed Indian
hemp among the controlled drugs and prohibited growing it except for science — the
first union-wide cannabis ban to apply on Serbian soil.[@1]

## Health of the nation, control of the state

Article 1 states the purpose plainly: to protect the health of the nation and prevent
unlawful use, the cultivation, manufacture, preparation and traffic of narcotic drugs
and their raw materials fall under state control, exercised as part of the state
economic plan.[@1] For the purposes of the Act, "narcotic drugs" begin with Group I:
raw opium, coca leaves and Indian hemp.[@1] Preparations of Indian hemp — extracts,
tinctures and resins — sit in Group II.[@1]

## Growing banned except for science

Article 4 draws the line for the plant: the cultivation of coca leaves and Indian hemp
is prohibited.[@1] The single exception is scientific: specified research institutions
may grow it in their own experimental fields, in quantities set by the chairman of the
federal public health committee.[@1] Poppy-growing peasants and co-operatives kept a
licensed role for opium: poppy could be sown only in areas fixed each year by the
agriculture minister, with the approval of the authorities.[@1] Hemp growers got no
equivalent role. Flexibility was built in at the top: the chairman of the federal
public health committee could designate further drugs and products known to cause
addiction.[@1]

## A federal ban for a federal state

The Act applied across the federation, including Serbia, and it reached Belgrade
through the standard channel: Yugoslavia communicated the text to the United Nations
under the 1931 manufacturing-limitation convention, and the Secretary-General
circulated it to member states in May 1950.[@1] It stayed the framework until the
republic-level drug laws of the later Yugoslav decades.
