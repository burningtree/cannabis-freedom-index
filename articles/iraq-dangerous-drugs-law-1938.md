Iraq's 1938 drug law restricted possession of cannabis and its resin to authorised professionals and people obtaining it through lawful prescription or pharmacy channels.[@1] Manufacturers and traders also faced licences, import and export permits, and records that health officials could inspect.[@1]

## Cannabis was named alongside other controlled drugs

The law expressly included Indian hemp, its resin and preparations made from them.[@1] It covered official pharmaceutical preparations as well as other forms, and referred to names used for resin in different markets.[@1]

Its definitions linked control to the Geneva Opium Convention and the later convention limiting drug manufacture and distribution.[@1] Further drugs brought within those conventions could also come under Iraqi control with government agreement.[@1]

That approach connected the local rules with a wider international system while spelling out that cannabis products were included.[@1]

## Possession depended on professional authority or a prescription

Doctors, dentists, veterinarians, licensed pharmacists and drug dealers, and other specially authorised people could hold controlled drugs within their lawful work.[@1] Other people needed to have obtained the substance from a licensed pharmacist on an authorised doctor's prescription, or through the named pharmacy laws.[@1]

For someone holding cannabis, lawful authority therefore depended on the professional or medical route through which it had been obtained.[@1] The controls reached possession as well as buying and selling.[@1]

## Paperwork followed the drug across borders

Manufacturing required a licence from the public-health authorities.[@1] Imports and exports needed permission, and an exporter had to provide authorisation from the importing country's government before an export permit could be granted.[@1]

The law also addressed consignments passing through Iraq.[@1] Authorised traders and pharmacists had to enter receipts and supplies in official registers within twenty-four hours and make annual returns.[@1]

Pharmacists had to retain prescriptions for controlled drugs for at least three years, allowing later inspection of how the medicines had been supplied.[@1]

## Penalties changed before a replacement law arrived

The [1955 amendment](/history/iraq-drug-penalties-1955/) set a general penalty of 100–1,000 dinars, six months to five years in prison, or both, for breaking the law.[@4] It also required confiscation of materials seized in the offender's actual use.[@4]

The [1965 narcotics law](/history/iraq-narcotics-law-1965/) replaced both this law and the earlier [cultivation ban](/history/iraq-hemp-cultivation-law-1933/).[@2] It kept unauthorised possession and cultivation restricted while permitting supervised crops grown exclusively for fibre and seeds.[@2]

That consolidated law was itself replaced in [2017](/history/iraq-narcotics-law-2017/).[@3] The history in [Iraq](/country/iq/) moved from professional licensing and records towards a system that combined those controls with more detailed criminal penalties and treatment procedures.[@1,3]
