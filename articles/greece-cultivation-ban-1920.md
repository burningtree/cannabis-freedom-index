In 1920 Greece — until then, with Turkey, one of the two main suppliers of hashish to Egypt and North Africa — forbade cannabis cultivation.[@1] The ban cut off Egypt's most abundant import channel and sent the trade looking for new fields.[@1,2]

## Egypt's ban created the Greek trade

Egyptians had produced and consumed hashish for centuries, until around 1880 the authorities outlawed cultivation, sale and consumption, burned the fields and raided the coffee houses that served it.[@2] Demand survived; smuggling systems grew to feed it, and across the Mediterranean Greece became the main exporter.[@2]

Greece's own politics then turned against the crop. The government prohibited production and burned cannabis fields, just as the Egyptians had done forty years earlier.[@2] Greek farmers lost their source of income, and Egypt lost its supply.[@2] The trade had reached far beyond Egypt: in French North Africa the smoked chira came mainly from Greece, with some consignments arriving from Istanbul.[@1]

## The ban leaked from the start

Greek hashish became rarer on the market but never disappeared.[@1] Large resin stocks had probably been hidden, and traffickers simply moved production to Bulgaria, Romania, Yugoslavia and Albania, whose farmers grew for Egypt through the 1920s.[@1,2] It took a second, more drastic Greek law in 1932 — providing for the destruction of stocks — to finish what the 1920 ban had started.[@1]

The pattern repeated across the region: each ban pushed growing onward, from Greece to the Balkans, then to Lebanese valleys worked for the same Egyptian demand.[@2] Bans never quite finished the job — the 1951 survey still found clandestine consumption among sections of the population in Turkey, Bulgaria and Greece.[@1]

At Geneva in 1925, Egypt's delegate El-Guindi tried to close the tap internationally, pushing hashish into the trade controls — eager, the historian notes, to leave his mark and demonstrate his country's sovereignty.[@2] The irony was visible across the empire: in British India cannabis stayed legal and taxed, while Britain enforced Egypt's prohibition with little enthusiasm.[@2]

For [Greece](/country/gr/), 1920 was the moment a supplier country chose prohibition — and learned that banning the fields was easier than finding the hidden resin.
