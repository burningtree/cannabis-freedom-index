Algeria's July 2025 amendment restored capital punishment for specified aggravated drug offences and expanded other sentencing rules.[@1] It also required the maximum legal penalty when examinations established that an offender was under the influence of drugs when committing an offence.[@1]

## Capital punishment returns to the drug law

The amendment attached death sentences to specified supply-related offences where drugs caused death or threatened serious harm to public health.[@1] The same penalty applied in listed circumstances including a transnational organised criminal group and using or threatening to use a firearm.[@1]

Other listed grounds concerned national security, creating insecurity or public disorder, and acting at the instigation or for the benefit of a foreign state.[@1] The clause referred to particular production, supply, import and precursor offences.[@1]

Earlier legislation had already contained a drug-law capital clause.[@2,3] The [1985 health law](/history/algeria-health-law-drug-framework-1985/) allowed it for specified trafficking and facilitation offences.[@2,3] The [2004 replacement law](/history/algeria-law-04-18-2004/) repealed those provisions and used prison terms extending to life.[@2,3]

## Recruitment and repeat offences face heavier penalties

The new law imposed twenty to thirty years for recruiting or using a minor, a person with particular needs, or a person receiving addiction treatment in listed drug activities.[@1] Higher penalties applied at specified locations, reaching death where the offence took place in or near educational and training establishments.[@1]

It also revised repeat-offence rules.[@1] Where an offence already carried life imprisonment, repetition could bring a death sentence.[@1] Other prison ranges were increased according to the schedule set out in the amendment.[@1]

## Tests can affect both punishment and care

The maximum-penalty rule applied when biological, hospital or medical examinations established drug influence during the offence.[@1] Investigators could order examinations in the circumstances stated in the law, and refusing them attracted the specified penalties.[@1]

A different rule concerned pupils’ periodic medical examinations.[@1] These could include early drug-use screening with the consent of their legal representatives or, where appropriate, the juvenile judge.[@1] Positive results led to the prescribed care measures, with protection from prosecution based on those results and restrictions on their further use.[@1]

The law also required negative drug-screening results in recruitment files for listed public and private-sector bodies, with implementing details left to regulation where needed.[@1]

## The amendment combines distinct approaches

Promulgated on 1 July and published on 13 July, the law paired more severe sentencing with further treatment and reintegration provisions.[@1] In [Algeria](/country/dz/), those rules concern different circumstances: aggravated supply, offending under drug influence, and people entering the prescribed care routes.[@1]
