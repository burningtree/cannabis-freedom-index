In July 1972 the anthropologist William Partridge began fieldwork in a municipality on Colombia's
north coast, at the foot of the Sierra Nevada de Santa Marta. He went to study how a community grew, sold
and smoked cannabis.

## Bananas, then war

The town had been built by bananas. From 1896 the United Fruit Company turned swamp into plantations,
drawing workers from all along the coast. As the company sold off its land in the 1950s and early
1960s, the banana economy around the town died with it. At the same time la Violencia, the civil war that began in
1948, drove Andean peasant families out of Santander, Norte de Santander and Cundinamarca. Between 1950 and
1953 many settled in the foothills of the Sierra Nevada, where locals called them *cachacos*, people from
the interior.

## A cash crop that takes guts

For these highland farmers cannabis was purely a cash crop: they did not smoke it themselves. Buyers from
the coastal towns arranged sales, often before planting, sometimes sweetening the deal with rum and money.

| | Price paid to the grower |
|---|---|
| A kilo of coffee, Colombia's best cash crop | 12.50 pesos |
| A pound of *la hoja*, the larger lower leaves | about 100 pesos |
| A pound of *la mona*, the resinous tops | 200 to 300 pesos |

One farmer told Partridge the business took *pateloncitos*, guts. Yet no grower in the area had been
investigated or arrested. The local police commissioner, recruited from among the peasants, grew cannabis
himself. Farmers knew that pressure from the United States had brought arrests in other regions.

## Two trades

Partridge found two separate markets. In the first, wealthy coastal buyers moved large quantities from the
highland growers to the cities of the coast, often for export to the United States; newspapers in 1972 and
1973 reported police seizures there. In the second, coastal peasants grew a few plants for the local
market, where day labourers bought for themselves and to resell. One plant could yield two pounds of *la
mona* and twenty of *la hoja*. Sellers told him that many policemen smoked and that the rest took bribes.

The consumers were the coastal day labourers, the landless poor of the town and countryside, who smoked
in groups at work and in the evenings. Locals called the drug *la mona*, *la amarilla* or simply *ella*,
"her"; *cáñamo*, the Spanish for hemp, meant only a lasso.

## Where it came from

Colombian historians cited by Partridge trace smoking to the Caribbean ports and the Magdalena valley, and
the country's [first marijuana law](/history/colombia-first-marijuana-law-1928/) to 1928. Clandestine
plantations made the news from about 1945. [Colombia](/country/co/) banned growing and trading marijuana
[in 1949](/history/colombia-decree-923-1949/), but Partridge's account shows how little the law reached a
remote mountain valley a generation later.
