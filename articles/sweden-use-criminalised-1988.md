On 1 July 1988 Sweden made it a crime to have used a drug. Possession had long been illegal; from that date the fact of consumption was an offence in itself, whether or not the person was found with anything.

## The law it was added to

The Narcotic Drugs (Punishments) Act came into force in 1968 and has been revised several times since, each time in a stricter direction. The 1988 change added personal use to the acts it punishes. In [1993](/history/sweden-prison-for-use-1993/) imprisonment was added to the penalties for it, a step that gave the police the power to require a urine or blood sample from a person they suspect.

## The scale of penalties

| Degree | Penalty |
|---|---|
| Minor | a fine, or up to six months in prison |
| Ordinary | 14 days to three years |
| Aggravated | two to seven years |
| Particularly aggravated | six to ten years |

Where an offence falls depends on the amount and on how dangerous the substance is judged to be. Driving has had its own zero-tolerance rule since 1999.

## What it means in practice

Most cases end in a fine. By 2009 more than half of all sanctions under the Act were fines, and in 2014 fines were 72% of the sanctions for minor offences.

A 2017 survey found about 4% of people aged 17 to 84 had used any narcotic in the previous year, with cannabis the most common.

Medical access is narrow. No regulator has approved herbal cannabis as a medicine. In 2018, 395 patients were being treated with the cannabis-based spray Sativex and 11 with dronabinol.

## Why the index moved

The index moved [Sweden](/country/se/) from 11 to 10 with the [1988 change](/history/sweden-use-criminalised-1988/). Consumption falls from 1 to 0, because use itself became an offence. The other segments do not change.
