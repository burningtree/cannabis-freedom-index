Medical cannabis in the United States began with a ballot measure written by
activists in San Francisco during the AIDS epidemic. It never said where the
cannabis would come from.

## From the AIDS wards

Dennis Peron conceived the measure in memory of his partner, Jonathan West, who had
used cannabis to ease the symptoms of AIDS. In 1991 San Francisco voters backed a
non-binding resolution for medical cannabis with 79 per cent. Bills to the same
effect passed the state legislature and were vetoed by Governor Pete Wilson.

So the campaigners went to the voters. The co-authors included a nurse, Anna Boyce,
and a psychiatrist, Tod Mikuriya. Three donors, among them George Soros, gave half a
million dollars each. Law enforcement and the state's attorney general opposed it.

On 5 November 1996 Proposition 215, the Compassionate Use Act, passed with 55.6 per
cent: 5.38 million votes to 4.30 million.

## What it said

- A patient with a doctor's recommendation, and the patient's caregiver, are exempt
  from the state's laws against possessing and growing cannabis.
- A doctor may not be punished for recommending it.

That was nearly all. The Act set no list of conditions, no quantities and no system
of supply. Guidelines of eight ounces and six mature plants came later, as did
identity cards, under a 2003 state law.

## Washington's answer

Cannabis remained illegal under federal law. On
[30 December 1996](/history/us-federal-warning-doctors-1996/) the Clinton
administration announced it would prosecute those involved in prescribing it.
Doctors sued and won the right to recommend.

Supply was another matter. In [2001](/history/us-oakland-cooperative-2001/) the
Supreme Court refused a defence of medical necessity, and in
[2005](/history/us-gonzales-v-raich-2005/) it held that Congress could criminalise
cannabis grown at home under a state law. Federal agents raided dispensaries for
years, more than fifty times in 2007 alone, until the Justice Department
[stepped back](/history/us-ogden-memo-2009/) in 2009.

## What grew from it

Because the law named no supplier, patients formed cooperatives and buyers' clubs,
and these became shops. One study describes the result as cannabis available "to
almost anyone who tells a willing physician that discomfort would be lessened", and
the "Californian model" as "something close to de facto legalization".

Other states followed with medical laws of their own, and voters grew used to legal
cannabis. That, more than anything in the text of Proposition 215, prepared the
ground for [Colorado and Washington](/history/us-colorado-washington-2012/) in 2012.
