New Zealand's Dangerous Drugs Act received assent on 11 November 1927. Its
Schedule included cannabis under the name Indian hemp, bringing it within a
wider system of drug controls. But the statute's title year was not its
commencement date: section 1 fixed [operation from 1 January 1928](/history/new-zealand-dangerous-drugs-commencement-1928/).

That distinction places the parliamentary decision and the start of the legal
regime on separate dates, rather than compressing both into a single year.

## International control shaped the measure

The Law Commission's historical review links the Act to international drug
agreements that extended controls to cannabis during the 1920s. It describes
the resulting legislation as a licensing scheme covering a range of drugs,
with offences for unauthorised import, export, production and dealing.

Jock Phillips' account for Te Ara likewise emphasises international alignment.
It records the minister's acknowledgement that there was no evidence of extensive
use of addictive drugs in New Zealand. The measure should therefore not be
explained simply as a response to an established domestic cannabis market.

## What Indian hemp covered

The original Schedule described dried flowering or fruiting tops of the female
cannabis plant from which resin had not been extracted. It also included the
resin itself, resin-based preparations, extracts and tinctures.

This definition matters because it identifies the material the legislature
actually listed. A modern shorthand such as cannabis can conceal the narrower
wording of an early statute and make later amendments appear redundant.

The [1963 amendment](/history/new-zealand-indian-hemp-definition-1963/) changed
that wording. Its separate timeline entry records the later expansion rather
than reading it back into the original Act.

## Controls retained medical channels

Section 9 prohibited production, manufacture, sale, distribution and other
dealings without a licence or another lawful authority. It also provided for
licence terms and conditions.

The framework did not amount to the disappearance of cannabis from medicine.
Te Ara describes controlled medical supply through doctors and chemists and
notes continuing restrictions and exceptions affecting cannabis treatments
under the later poisons regime. Medical availability and prohibition of
unauthorised dealings formed parts of the same system.

## A starting point for later legislation

The [Narcotics Act 1965](/history/new-zealand-narcotics-act-1965/) subsequently
implemented the Single Convention and distinguished dealing from simple
possession or use. The Law Commission identifies substantially higher penalties
for dealing under that legislation.

For [New Zealand's history](/country/nz/), the controls take effect on
[1 January 1928](/history/new-zealand-dangerous-drugs-commencement-1928/).
That is the date the index uses for the first national drop. The November 1927
assent, the product definition and the licensing structure stay on their own
terms, with later changes and evidence of enforcement recorded separately.
