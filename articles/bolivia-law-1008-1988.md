Bolivia’s drug law of 19 July 1988 sent people found with a minimal amount of cannabis for immediate personal use to compulsory treatment until considered rehabilitated. Possession above that amount fell under trafficking, exposing a user to ten to twenty-five years in prison plus a fine.

## Compulsory treatment already had a history

The [1962 narcotics law](/history/bolivia-narcotics-law-1962/) had explicitly covered Indian hemp, imposed prison sentences for unlawful possession and supply, and required institutional admission for drug misuse.

The 1988 legislation brought coca and controlled substances into a detailed law covering cultivation, manufacture, distribution and use. Cannabis and cannabis resin appeared in its controlled list alongside cocaine and other drugs.

For cannabis users, the distinction between a personal quantity and trafficking became especially consequential because the two routes led to very different forms of confinement.

## Doctors assessed the personal-use quantity

The personal-possession rule applied to dependent users and occasional consumers found with a minimal amount presumed to be for immediate personal consumption. Two specialists from a public drug-treatment institution were to assess that quantity.

The law gave them an assessment to make for the individual case. It specified no universal cannabis allowance in grams.

Someone within the assessed amount was to enter a public or private drug-treatment institution until the authorities were convinced of rehabilitation. The wording expressly included occasional consumers, extending this compulsory route beyond people described as dependent.

## Possession could lead to a trafficking sentence

Someone holding more than the assessed personal quantity fell under the trafficking offence. The prescribed punishment combined imprisonment with a fine.

The law’s definition of trafficking covered a broad range of conduct, including possessing intentionally, storing, transporting, delivering, supplying, buying, selling and donating controlled substances.

It also created separate offences. Unlawfully supplying another person carried eight to twelve years plus a fine, regardless of quantity. Unlawful cultivation carried one to two years in prison, rising to two to four for repeat offending. The charge therefore mattered alongside the amount involved.

## December rules measured two days of consumption

The [implementing regulations adopted in December](/history/bolivia-personal-use-assessment-1988/) defined immediate personal possession as an amount equivalent to 48 hours of consumption, assessed by two medical experts. Above it, trafficking was presumed.

The [2017 drug-control reform](/history/bolivia-ley-913-2017/) later replaced parts of the administrative system while retaining the personal-possession and trafficking offences. That combination of individually assessed quantities, compulsory treatment and severe criminal penalties continues to shape [Bolivia’s cannabis law](/country/bo/).
