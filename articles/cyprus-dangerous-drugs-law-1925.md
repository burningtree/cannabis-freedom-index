Cyprus controlled Indian hemp under its Dangerous Drugs Laws, 1925 to 1932 — and the proof sits inside a later amending order. Order in Council No. 2281, made under section 11(2) of those Laws on 5 March 1948 and deemed in force from 1 January that year, extended Part III of the Laws to two new synthetic drugs, amidone and Metopon.[@1]

## Why the order proves the 1925 coverage

Section 11(2) let the Governor bring further drugs under the same regime "in the same manner" as the drugs already listed in section 11(1).[@1] The order's preamble describes the newcomers as productive, if misused, of ill effects "substantially of the same character or nature as or analogous to those produced by Indian hemp, morphine or cocaine" — naming Indian hemp alongside morphine and cocaine as the benchmark drugs the Laws already covered.[@1] That wording only makes sense if Indian hemp stood in the section 11(1) base list from the 1925–1932 Laws themselves.[@1]

## A post-war synthetics update

The order did not touch cannabis directly: its schedule lists only amidone (with its salts and preparations) and methyldihydromorphinone, known as Metopon, applying Part III to them exactly as it applied to the listed drugs.[@1] It is a routine post-war update, folding new laboratory opiates into a colonial control framework built in the 1920s — the same wave that added pethidine, desomorphine and Indian-hemp preparations to sibling colony schedules in those years.[@2,3]

## Reported through London

The text reached the United Nations from the British government under article 21 of the 1931 Limitation Convention as amended by the 1946 protocol, with the Secretary-General circulating it to members and parties in 1949.[@1] One boundary stays: the filing reproduces the 1948 order, not the 1925–1932 Laws, so the exact cannabis provisions of the principal text remain unconfirmed here.[@1] The [country page](/country/cy/) continues the story from this colonial framework.
