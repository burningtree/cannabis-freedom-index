Somalia's 1970 narcotics law established a system of health-ministry authorisations, controlled
production and prescription dispensing. The original Italian text in the Official Gazette
contains twenty articles and a record-keeping schedule. Reading it alongside the later drug
offences decree gives a firmer historical basis than a general reference to a penal code.

Law 46 is dated 3 March 1970 and was published on 22 August. Its article 3 authorises the Health
Secretary to establish the controlled-substance list. Decree 227, dated 26 September and
published on 29 October, expressly lists Cannabis, with Hashish as a synonym, and Indian Hemp.
These dates identify verified controls; they do not establish Somalia's first cannabis ban.
The earlier restriction in the index remains an estimate.

## Cultivation, possession and transfers

Article 4 requires special Health Ministry authorisation for cultivating, harvesting or
processing plants yielding controlled substances. Breach carries six months to two years and
a fine. Article 5 requires registration and authorisation for narcotics possession, production
and trade, with two to seven years and a fine of 2,000–20,000 Somali shillings for unauthorised
activities.

Article 14 restricts transfers even when no payment is made. It also regulates delivery and
transport documentation. The law therefore does not provide a general exemption for gifts.

## Medicines and compulsory intervention

State medical establishments and pharmacies are entered in the authorised register under
article 6. Articles 15 and 16 regulate prescriptions, medicinal doses, patient identification
and dispensing records. Article 17 punishes prescriptions issued to encourage abuse without a
therapeutic need or in excessive quantities.

These are general narcotics medicines provisions. They do not establish that cannabis was
prescribed, that cannabis medicines are available today or that a patient may grow at home.

The law also takes a coercive approach to dependency. Article 18 requires health professionals
to report chronic narcotic intoxication within forty-eight hours. Article 19 permits medically
assessed detoxification treatment for habitual users whose serious mental disturbance makes
them dangerous to themselves or others. Neither provision demonstrates how often these powers
were used.

## The later 1984 decree

Decree-Law 5 of 18 May 1984 prescribes ten years and a 50,000-shilling fine for unlawful drug
manufacture, production, import, dealing or supply to another person. Article 2 prescribes five
years for allowing a public or private place to be used for consumption, and three years for
entering such a place to consume. These are specific premises-related offences; the wording
should not be extended into an identical sentence for every act of private consumption.

The decree assigned drug cases to the National Security Court and its regional branches. It
also set transitional rules for pending proceedings and earlier offences. Law 25 of 24 June
1984 subsequently converted it into statute, with its own commencement provision fifteen days
after publication on 19 July.

A complete later consolidation and the relationship between the cultivation provision and the
1984 production offence remain unresolved. These historical texts cannot establish a single
present-day sentencing practice across Somalia's different administrations and armed-group
territories. They strengthen the documented legal baseline while leaving that uncertainty
visible.
