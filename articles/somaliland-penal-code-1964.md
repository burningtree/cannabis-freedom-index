People in the northern Somali Republic came under a new penal code in April 1964, including prison terms for supplying narcotic drugs and allowing premises to be used for consumption.[@1,2] The code replaced the Indian Penal Code previously applied in Somaliland.[@2]

## One code replaced two earlier systems

The new code had been approved in December 1962 and entered into force on 3 April 1964.[@2] Somalilandlaw's editorial introduction explains that it replaced the Indian code in the north and the Italian code used in the former Italian territory.[@2]

The approving decree required copies to be displayed in regional and local administration offices so the public could inspect them.[@1] Its offences applied to citizens and foreigners in the territory, subject to exceptions under national or international law.[@1]

## Supplying drugs brought one to three years

The basic supply offence covered trading in narcotics, holding them for sale and supplying or obtaining them for another person outside cases allowed by law.[@1] Punishment was one to three years in prison and a fine.[@1]

The code required an increase when drugs were sold or delivered to someone under eighteen, a person with specified mental impairments or a person addicted to drugs.[@1] Its wording distinguished possession for sale from simply having a drug.[@1]

That distinction matters when tracing the later [1970 narcotics law](/history/somaliland-narcotics-law-1970/), which separately imposed authorisation requirements and prison terms for possession.[@3]

## Consumption venues had their own offences

Allowing public or private premises to become a meeting place for consuming drugs carried six months to two years in prison and a fine.[@1] Entering such premises to consume carried up to six months and a fine.[@1]

The code therefore reached both the organiser of a gathering and the person attending it, through different offences and penalties.[@1] These provisions concerned the use of premises, alongside the separate trading offence.[@1]

## Later laws made the controls more specific

A [1970 schedule](/history/somaliland-cannabis-schedule-1970/) expressly named cannabis, hashish and Indian hemp.[@4] The [1984 decree](/history/somaliland-drug-offences-decree-1984/) then raised supply and consumption-venue penalties and transferred drug cases to security courts.[@5]

After Somaliland re-established independence, its [2001 constitution](/history/somaliland-constitutional-legal-continuity-2001/) retained compatible existing laws until replacement.[@6] That continuity helps explain why an inherited Somali penal code remains part of [Somaliland's legal history](/country/xs/).[@2,6]
