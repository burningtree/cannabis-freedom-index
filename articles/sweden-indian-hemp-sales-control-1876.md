Sweden’s nineteenth-century poison legislation covered Indian hemp before the country adopted its later narcotics-control framework. Legal historian Olle Hoflund identifies a royal ordinance of 7 January 1876 extending liability to unauthorized sale or delivery of the substance named Cannabis indica.

## What the historical sources say

Hoflund discusses the measure on page 13 of the third edition of his study of narcotics offences. The Swedish government’s 2008 inquiry repeats the account in its historical chapter and cites that page.

The two publications therefore provide a traceable account, but they are not independent witnesses to the original law. The government review draws on the legal history. The original 1876 ordinance and its complete schedule have not been inspected for this article.

## Sale and delivery

The conduct identified is unauthorized selling or handing over Indian hemp. That wording supports a supply restriction. It does not establish that every person possessing the plant committed an offence, or that all cultivation and consumption were prohibited.

The distinction also limits how the plant’s name should be used. The historical sources identify Indian hemp and the botanical term Cannabis indica. They do not supply a basis here for extending the rule automatically to every fibre crop or every product now described legally as cannabis.

## A poison-law setting

The government inquiry places the provision within a history of controlling dangerous substances through rules governing their custody and sale. That setting matters when comparing it with later legislation directed specifically at narcotics.

A historical supply rule can precede a broader drug-control framework without containing all its later offences. Reading subsequent possession or cultivation prohibitions back into the 1876 provision would make the early record more comprehensive than the evidence supports.

## What the date means

The database uses the ordinance date given by Hoflund, 7 January 1876. It does not claim an independently verified commencement clause, cannabis-specific penalty or complete list of authorized suppliers. Those questions require the original text.
