In 1950, two years after Japan's Cannabis Control Act, about 25,000 farms still grew hemp.[@1] The law is often remembered as the moment the country's ancient hemp industry was banned. The record is slower and more interesting: cultivation was licensed, not abolished, and the crop survived long enough to reach an all-time peak after the ban.

## A law that regulated, not erased

The 1948 Act banned possession and made growing subject to a licence, but it left the licensed trade alive.[@1] In the months before it passed, Emperor Hirohito visited hemp farms in Tochigi prefecture. Junichi Takayasu, who runs Japan's cannabis museum, keeps a 1947 photograph of the visit and says the emperor came to assure growers that they and their livelihood would be protected.[@2]

The reassurance was not empty. Licensed farmers continued to grow hemp for rope, nets, cloth, paper and food.[@2] Hemp seed stayed in *shichimi*, the seven-spice blend, and hemp fibre stayed in the ropes at Shinto shrines.[@2]

## The peak came after the ban

The licensed system did not shrink the crop immediately. In 1952, 37,300 farmers grew hemp on about 5,000 hectares, the highest figures recorded.[@3] That is four years after the Act, and more farms than the 25,000 counted in 1950.[@1,3]

The collapse came later, as artificial fibres took the textile market and the cost of licences weighed on small farms.[@1]

## What was left

By 2015 Japan grew hemp on 7.6 hectares, and by March 2017 only 30 people held growing permits.[@3] The 2019 farm count was 35 growers on 9 hectares, about 90% of it in Tochigi.[@4] Tottori prefecture banned hemp production altogether in 2016 after the president of a licensed hemp company and two employees were arrested on cannabis possession charges.[@3]

The law still draws a careful line. "Cannabis" excludes the plant's seed and mature stalk, which is why hemp seed can be sold for food and why shrine ropes can be hemp.[@5] Since March 2025 a Type 1 grower must use seed from plants with no more than 0.3% delta-9 THC, and must treat seed before passing it on.[@5]

The 1948 Act did not end hemp in Japan. It narrowed who could grow it, then left the crop to compete with cheaper fibres. The 1950 farms are a reminder that prohibition and economic change did different parts of the work, and that the [licensed remnant](/country/jp/) is what survived both.
