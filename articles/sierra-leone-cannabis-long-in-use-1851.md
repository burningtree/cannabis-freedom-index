In 1851, Sierra Leone's colonial surgeon and apothecary R. O. Clarke published a short account of *diamba* in *Hooker's Journal of Botany*.[@1] The report described an established crop and social practice.[@1] Its publication records when that knowledge entered this botanical journal, rather than when cannabis first arrived in the colony.[@1]

## Seeds carried by displaced people

Clarke attributed the introduction of seeds to Central Africans removed from a slave ship by a British cruiser.[@1] He reported cultivation among their descendants and several other communities, including people he called Maroons, Settlers and Creoles.[@1]

The label “Congoes” in his account requires qualification.[@2] Duvall explains that such labels could group together captives embarked from western Central African ports.[@2] They did not necessarily identify a single language or pre-existing ethnic community.[@2]

British interception also did not mean freedom from further control.[@2] Duvall places these movements within a wider history in which formerly enslaved people remained subject to decisions about their residence and labour.[@2]

## More than a name for a plant

Clarke distinguished a preparation using flowers and seeds, called *Maconie*, from a leaf preparation called *Makiah*.[@1] He also described cultivation, drying, sale and gatherings at people's houses where a pipe was passed around.[@1]

Those details make the document evidence of agricultural and social knowledge, rather than merely a passing mention of an unfamiliar intoxicant.[@1] They also show why a general name for cannabis can conceal distinctions between preparations.[@1]

Duvall compares related vocabulary around the Atlantic.[@2] His argument combines words with documented population movements; a resemblance alone cannot identify the person who carried a seed or date a particular introduction.[@2]

## The author and the editor disagreed

The report contains more than one voice.[@1] Clarke called the plant *Cannabis sativa*, while the journal's editorial introduction compared it with Indian preparations and speculated about transmission from Egypt or Arabia.[@1] The editor's suggested route was not a demonstrated migration history.[@1]

Clarke's own attribution of the plant as indigenous to Central Africa is likewise a nineteenth-century claim, not the modern account of its botanical origin.[@1] His moralising descriptions and medicinal claims need the same historical attribution.[@1]

The document strengthens the history of [diamba](/culture/language/diamba/) in [Sierra Leone](/country/sl/), while leaving its earliest local chronology unresolved.[@3] The colony's later [1926 drug controls](/history/sierra-leone-dangerous-drugs-ordinance-1926/) reached practices already documented decades before.[@3]
