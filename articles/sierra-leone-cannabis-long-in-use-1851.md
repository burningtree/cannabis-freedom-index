In 1851, Sierra Leone's colonial surgeon and apothecary R. O. Clarke published a short account of *diamba* in *Hooker's Journal of Botany*. The report described an established crop and social practice. Its publication records when that knowledge entered this botanical journal, rather than when cannabis first arrived in the colony.

## Seeds carried by displaced people

Clarke attributed the introduction of seeds to Central Africans removed from a slave ship by a British cruiser. He reported cultivation among their descendants and several other communities, including people he called Maroons, Settlers and Creoles.

The label “Congoes” in his account requires qualification. Duvall explains that such labels could group together captives embarked from western Central African ports. They did not necessarily identify a single language or pre-existing ethnic community.

British interception also did not mean freedom from further control. Duvall places these movements within a wider history in which formerly enslaved people remained subject to decisions about their residence and labour.

## More than a name for a plant

Clarke distinguished a preparation using flowers and seeds, called *Maconie*, from a leaf preparation called *Makiah*. He also described cultivation, drying, sale and gatherings at people's houses where a pipe was passed around.

Those details make the document evidence of agricultural and social knowledge, rather than merely a passing mention of an unfamiliar intoxicant. They also show why a general name for cannabis can conceal distinctions between preparations.

Duvall compares related vocabulary around the Atlantic. His argument combines words with documented population movements; a resemblance alone cannot identify the person who carried a seed or date a particular introduction.

## The author and the editor disagreed

The report contains more than one voice. Clarke called the plant *Cannabis sativa*, while the journal's editorial introduction compared it with Indian preparations and speculated about transmission from Egypt or Arabia. The editor's suggested route was not a demonstrated migration history.

Clarke's own attribution of the plant as indigenous to Central Africa is likewise a nineteenth-century claim, not the modern account of its botanical origin. His moralising descriptions and medicinal claims need the same historical attribution.

The document strengthens the history of [diamba](/culture/language/diamba/) in [Sierra Leone](/country/sl/), while leaving its earliest local chronology unresolved. The colony's later [1920 prohibition](/history/sierra-leone-ban-1920/) belongs to a separate moment: a law imposed on practices already documented decades before.
