In 1970 the House of Assembly of Papua New Guinea made possessing marijuana a crime under the territory's drug law.[@1] The change closed a gap that a recent prosecution had exposed: people could hold or smoke cannabis grown locally because the old law reached imported supplies, not the plant in the garden.[@1] The offence carried three months to two years in prison.[@4]

## A law built for imported drugs

The Dangerous Drugs Ordinance 1952 controlled the import and supply of narcotic drugs for medicinal use.[@2] Its list of dangerous drugs included galenical preparations of Indian hemp—extracts and tinctures—but not the raw flowering tops that people smoked.[@2] Its possession offence applied to drugs on board a ship or imported without a licence, so locally grown marijuana fell outside it.[@2]

The UN report on the territory said narcotic drugs were neither manufactured nor produced in Papua New Guinea and were not exported; imports were controlled under the 1952 ordinance.[@1] An officer told an Australian Senate committee that trafficking was isolated and drug abuse very small.[@1] The report also said a recent prosecution had exposed the local possession gap.[@1]

## Possession becomes an offence

The House of Assembly adopted the Dangerous Drugs (Possession) Act 1970, recorded in the official index as No. 82 of 1970.[@1,5] It amended the 1952 ordinance and made knowing possession of a dangerous drug an offence.[@1,4] The Supreme Court later quoted the possession provision as section 7(1)(d), with a penalty of "Imprisonment for not less than three months or more than two years."[@4]

The old Act, as later consolidated, punished cultivation, making, export and conveyance on the same scale.[@3] Its text groups those activities in one subsection; a 1990 amendment was to add "knowingly" to all four paragraphs, though the consolidation notes that its commencement is unclear.[@3]

## The courts could still avoid prison

In 1973 the Supreme Court heard [the appeal of Henderson](/history/papua-new-guinea-henderson-ruling-1973/), convicted of possessing marijuana and sentenced to the three-month minimum.[@4] The court held that the minimum did not stop a magistrate from imposing a fine or a conditional discharge under the District Courts Act.[@4] It allowed the appeal and sent the case back for a further hearing.[@4]

## What came next

The old Act stayed in force for half a century. From 13 January 2022 its maximum briefly rose to 40 years or a K1,000,000 fine; on 31 March 2022 the [Controlled Substance Act](/history/papua-new-guinea-controlled-substance-act-2022/) replaced the whole law.[@6,7,8] Cannabis was listed in Schedule I, and the new sentencing table set a maximum of two years for 10 g or less and 16 years to life for more than 2 kg.[@7] The [country profile](/country/pg/) records the result.
