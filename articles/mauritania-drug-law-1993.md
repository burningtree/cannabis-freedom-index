Mauritania's drug law, signed on 20 July 1993, prescribed death for repeat high-risk drug production and trafficking offences and allowed it in specified aggravated cases. People accused of simple illicit use faced a different penalty: up to two years, a fine alone, or both, with treatment offering a route away from prosecution.

## Ministers and police already had drug-control roles

Mauritania had [joined the Single Convention](/history/mauritania-single-convention-1989/) in 1989 and created a [national drug-policy commission](/history/mauritania-drug-commission-1990/) the following year. Its later report to the UN children's-rights committee described twelve ministers coordinating education, enforcement and social reintegration. A central office charged with detecting traffickers and drug users followed in December 1992.

President Maaouya Ould Sid'Ahmed Taya signed the criminal law after adoption by the National Assembly and Senate. The signature came nineteen days after Mauritania [joined the UN convention against illicit drug trafficking](/history/mauritania-1988-convention-1993/).

## Repeat offences could bring death

Growing, producing, importing or exporting high-risk drugs carried fifteen to thirty years and a fine. A broad trafficking clause covered buying, possessing, transporting, distributing and giving drugs away without payment. Repeat offences under these clauses carried death.

Capital punishment was also possible where specified offences involved organised crime, violence or weapons, or drugs that caused someone's death. The law linked its high-risk category to domestic drug schedules set by the health minister.

People who helped another person use a high-risk drug, including by providing premises, faced two to ten years and a fine. This extended criminal responsibility to those who enabled consumption.

## Simple use could lead to a fine or treatment

The separate use chapter prohibited occasional and habitual illicit consumption, whether alone or in a group. Its penalty expressly allowed either imprisonment or a fine by itself.

Health authorities could assess a person's medical condition and family, working and social circumstances. Completing prescribed treatment prevented prosecution. Another clause protected people who had successfully undergone detoxification or medical supervision after the alleged use. Those voluntarily seeking hospital or dispensary treatment could request anonymity.

## Foreigners faced exclusion as well

The law required permanent territorial exclusion for foreigners convicted of listed offences, including illicit use. It also allowed courts to restrict travel, withdraw passports and suspend driving licences.

These criminal controls formed the background to [the Senate's 2008 debate](/history/mauritania-controlled-drug-market-senate-2008/) over authorised drug supplies for medicine and research. That debate turned attention toward who could handle controlled medicines within [Mauritania's](/country/mr/) prohibition system.
