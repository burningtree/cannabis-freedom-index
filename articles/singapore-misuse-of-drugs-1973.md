On 7 July 1973, Singapore's Misuse of Drugs Act replaced the 1951 Dangerous Drugs Ordinance and the 1969 Drugs (Prevention of Misuse) Act.[@2] Cannabis was placed in Class B. Possession or consumption could bring up to 10 years, a $20,000 fine, or both; trafficking carried 3 to 20 years, a $5,000 to $40,000 fine, or both, plus 2 to 10 strokes.

## Two laws before one

The 1951 ordinance had restricted Indian hemp, as the law called cannabis, to people with official authorisation; unauthorised possession could bring a $10,000 fine, five years in prison or both.[@6] The 1969 Act targeted amphetamines, lysergamide, mescaline and related substances, and its schedule did not list cannabis.[@3] Cannabis stayed under the 1951 ordinance until 1973.

Cannabis possession arrests rose in the late 1960s, customs seized 87.5 kg in the first half of 1970 against 27.7 kg a year earlier, and heroin began to appear. The government set up the Central Narcotics Bureau in 1971.[@4,5] The Bill's explanatory statement promised "more up-to-date and extensive provisions" and "greatly increased" penalties, with caning for serious offences.[@1]

## Cannabis in Class B

The Act divided controlled drugs into three classes. Cannabis and cannabis resin sat in Class B.[@1] The class mattered most for trafficking, where penalties varied; possession and consumption carried the same maximum for every class.

For possession or consumption, the maximum was 10 years or a $20,000 fine, or both; a second or later offence carried at least 2 years or a $3,000 fine, or both. Trafficking carried 3 to 20 years or a $5,000 to $40,000 fine, or both, plus 2 to 10 strokes. Trafficking to someone under 18 carried up to 30 years or a $50,000 fine, or both, plus 15 strokes. Growing cannabis carried 3 to 20 years or a $5,000 to $40,000 fine, or both.[@1]

Anyone with more than 15 g of cannabis or cannabis resin was presumed to possess it for trafficking. Possession of a container or keys could be enough to presume possession, and possession presumed knowledge of the drug.[@1]

## New police powers

CNB officers, police and customs officers could arrest without a warrant anyone reasonably suspected of an offence. They could search premises without a warrant, search people, and stop and search ships, aircraft, vehicles and trains. A CNB or immigration officer could require a urine sample from someone suspected of having a controlled drug in their body.[@1]

The Director of CNB could require a suspected addict to be examined and order treatment at an approved institution. A person could volunteer for treatment, and statements made for that purpose could not be used in a later prosecution.[@1]

## What came next

In December 1975, Parliament made death mandatory for trafficking more than 30 g of morphine or 15 g of diamorphine; cannabis was not yet capital. In 1977, Operation Ferret led to thousands of arrests.[@4] The death penalty reached cannabis in [1990](/history/singapore-cannabis-death-penalty-1990/).
