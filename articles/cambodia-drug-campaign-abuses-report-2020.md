Amnesty International called for Cambodia to stop punishing personal drug use, possession and cultivation after investigating the country's anti-drug campaign.[@1] Its May 2020 report described arbitrary detention, alleged torture and failures of care, and urged voluntary health services in place of compulsory detention.[@1]

## Interviews put people behind the arrest figures

The [campaign launched in January 2017](/history/cambodia-drug-campaign-2017/) had produced at least 55,770 arrests on suspicion of using or selling drugs by March 2020.[@1] Amnesty's researchers went to Cambodia in November and December 2019 to examine what happened after arrest.[@1]

They interviewed fifty-one people, including thirty-four who used or previously used drugs, as well as relatives, lawyers, activists and health specialists.[@1] Six of the people interviewed had been minors when arrested and detained.[@1]

The research included twenty people held in administrative detention centres and fifteen documented criminal-prosecution cases.[@1] It examined the wider drug campaign, with many interviewees drawn from poor and marginalised communities.[@1]

## A positive test could bring lasting consequences

Interviewees described arrests tied to the place they were found or the people around them.[@1] Some said they had stopped using drugs or had never used them; Amnesty recorded those accounts without deciding whether each interviewee had broken drug laws.[@1]

The report described coerced drug testing, failures of fair-trial rights and the influence of money on whether people could secure release.[@1] It also documented allegations of abuse and inadequate healthcare in prisons and centres labelled as rehabilitation facilities.[@1]

## The law promised care and safeguards

The [2012 drug law](/history/cambodia-drug-control-law-2012/) required the state to support treatment, rehabilitation and harm reduction.[@2] It provided voluntary-treatment consent rules, medical assessment for compulsory care and rights to appeal detention-related decisions.[@2]

Against those promises, the investigation described people being detained against their will and subjected to punishment instead of evidence-based care.[@1] The report argued that enforcement was harming the health and rights it was meant to protect.[@1]

## Reform meant changing both law and services

Amnesty called for decriminalising personal use, possession and cultivation, closing compulsory drug detention centres and expanding voluntary services.[@1] It also urged attention to groups particularly exposed to public-space policing, including homeless people.[@1]

In [Cambodia](/country/kh/), the proposed change would move the response away from arrest and detention towards care people could choose, while requiring authorities to address the abuses documented during the campaign.[@1]
