In *Cannabis and Culture* (1975), anthropologist Vera Rubin examined Jamaican accounts of a “ganja vision”: a small dancing person sometimes recalled from an early cannabis-smoking experience. Her question concerned the social meaning of those accounts, rather than whether cannabis invariably produced a particular image.

The chapter grew from a multidisciplinary research programme involving the Research Institute for the Study of Man and the University of the West Indies. It placed reported experience within working-class relationships, expectations and the distinction between becoming a smoker and deciding against it.

## Ten accounts within a selected sample

The clinical sample comprised sixty working-class men: thirty smokers and thirty non-smokers, aged twenty-three to fifty-three. Most had tried smoking at least once, including many who did not become regular users.

Ten smokers reported personally experiencing the vision in a recognisable form. Other participants repeated stories they had heard. Rubin distinguishes those accounts, and says more research would be needed to establish how widespread the phenomenon was.

The numbers do not measure Jamaican national prevalence. Nor do retrospective interviews establish exactly what happened during someone's first smoking experience.

## A little dancing figure

Some accounts described a small woman in coloured clothing, dancing or turning. Another described a group of little men. The figures were not identical in every detail, but Rubin considered the recurring pattern significant.

She interpreted a positive first experience as potentially confirming membership in a smoking circle. A negative or uneventful experience could provide an accepted explanation for not joining. Her argument therefore included a route out of smoking, as well as a story validating it.

## Comparison without a shared origin

Rubin compared the social function with accounts of vision quests among Indigenous peoples of the North American Plains. This was an analogy about belonging and changes in status, not evidence of contact, a shared historical origin or equivalent ceremonies.

She also distinguished culturally patterned visions from the assumption that every cannabis user sought or experienced hallucinations. Her stronger claims about the relative importance of culture and pharmacology remain interpretations of these data, not controlled experimental findings.

## What the publication records

The chapter documents narratives circulating in a particular research setting under Jamaican prohibition. It does not establish a universal initiation rite, a required Rastafari ceremony or a reproducible effect of ganja.

The broader everyday institutions appear in the account of [Jamaican herb camps and yards](/culture/traditions/jamaican-herb-camps/). Together, the chapters preserve different kinds of evidence: descriptions of venues and relationships, and participants' remembered experiences interpreted by a researcher.
