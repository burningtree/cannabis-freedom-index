In 1939 Colombia absolutely prohibited growing marijuana: existing plantations were to be destroyed, and anyone who defied the ban would be punished as a trafficker in "drastic drugs" under the penal code.[@1] Growing had been noticed and partly policed for years, but this was the first order to wipe the crop out entirely.[@1]

## The plant had been watched since the 1920s

Colombian authorities had already noticed marihuana-growing in 1925, along with its use by sailors, stevedores and prostitutes in the Caribbean ports.[@1] Measures against the plant existed through the 1920s, and historians date the country's [first marijuana law to 1928](/history/colombia-first-marijuana-law-1928/).[@1]

What changed the climate in the 1930s came largely from the north: the United States criminalised marihuana sale federally with the Marihuana Tax Act of 1937, and its drugs chief Harry Anslinger tied the plant to insanity and violent crime.[@1] At a Federal Bureau of Narcotics congress in December 1938, Kolb had described the marihuana consumer as a cause of many crimes.[@1] Colombia felt that pressure directly.[@1]

## A health ministry resolution made it absolute

In September 1939 the Ministry of Health's own journal, the Revista de Higiene, published an American article by Lawrence Kolb entitled "Marihuana: the weed which drives you mad."[@1] The same month, health ministry resolution 645 ordered the absolute prohibition: destroy the plantations, punish violators as traffickers.[@1] The resolution, dated 18 September 1939, appeared in the Diario Oficial on 6 October, and Colombia's own 1939 report on drug traffic recorded the step for the international record.[@1] That report survives in the national archives in Bogotá, and it is the paper trail historians rely on to date the absolute ban.[@1]

## The ban became the reference for decades

The 1939 resolution stayed on the books as the starting point: a decade later, [Decree 923 of 1949](/history/colombia-decree-923-1949/) again banned cultivation and trade across the republic while expressly recalling that growing had already been prohibited by the 1939 health ministry resolution.[@1,2]

The historian's larger point is that none of this began with the North American counterculture of the 1960s: by the time demand exploded abroad, Colombia already had a significant domestic market and, since 1939, a standing order to destroy the crop that supplied it.[@1] That tension runs through the whole later history of [Colombia](/country/co/).
