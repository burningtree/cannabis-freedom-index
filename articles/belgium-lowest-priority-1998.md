Belgium's first step away from prosecuting cannabis users was not a law. On 17 April 1998 the minister of justice, using his power under the 1997 act that set up the College of Prosecutors-General, issued a directive on prosecution policy for drugs. The [1921 drug law](/history/belgium-drug-law-1921/) was not touched. What follows is taken from a bill tabled in the Chamber two years later by Daniel Bacquelaine and Olivier Chastel, whose explanatory statement describes the directive and criticises it.

## Where it came from

A parliamentary working group on drugs had reported in 1997. It called for prevention, and it said in plain terms that penal policy towards users had to change: "repressive intervention towards the drug user is justified only if, in addition, the person has committed offences that disturb the social order". Addiction by itself, the bill's authors add, was therefore no reason for the criminal law to step in.

## What the directive said

| Point | Content |
|---|---|
| Priority | prosecuting possession of cannabis for personal use gets "the lowest priority" in prosecution policy |
| Hard and soft | it draws a clear distinction between soft and hard drugs, changing the policy of the earlier directive of 26 May 1993 |
| Record | people found with cannabis are registered by a simplified police report |

## The criticism

The bill's authors grant that the directive "brings about a certain de facto depenalisation". Their objection is to the third point. The simplified report, they write, is an entirely new instrument of control that keeps cannabis use "within the orbit of the criminal law": holders of cannabis are to be inventoried. It also hands the police something that had belonged to the prosecutor, the judgment of whether a prosecution is worthwhile.

They cite the justice ministry's own criminal policy service, whose 1998–1999 report found that two thirds of the unsuspended prison sentences passed as the main penalty in drug cases in 1994 rested solely on the criminalisation of use.

Their conclusion was that the matter had to be settled in statute. That came with the [law of 2003](/history/belgium-law-2003/).

## Why the index moved

Three scores rise by one point each. Enforcement goes from 2 to 3, because prosecutors were instructed to put personal cannabis possession at the bottom of their priorities. Possession goes from 1 to 2 and consumption from 1 to 2: the act stayed a crime in the statute and was still written up by the police, but it was now unlikely to be prosecuted. Cultivation and sharing are unchanged. The total moves from 11 to 17.
