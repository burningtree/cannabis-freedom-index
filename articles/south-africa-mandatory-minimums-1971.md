From 1 December 1971, anyone in South Africa convicted of possessing dagga for the first time
went to prison for at least two years. A dealer went for at least five. For fifteen years judges
could not give less, whoever stood before them.[@1]

## A law against a rising tide

Dagga had been banned nationally since [1922](/history/south-africa-national-ban-1922/), and the
police had counted ever more arrests: [5,762 in 1932](/history/south-africa-dagga-arrests-count-1932/),
over 23,000 in 1955, and [more than 30,000 a year](/history/south-africa-dagga-arrests-1968/) by
1967 and 1968.[@3] Until 1971 the most a court could impose was a fine of R1,000 or five years.[@2]

The bill came from the Minister of Social Welfare and Pensions, who told the House of Assembly
that dagga was "the precursor to stronger drugs". The opposition leader, Sir De Villiers Graaff,
supported minimum sentences for dealers and cited about five million pounds of dagga confiscated
the year before. Helen Suzman moved to reject the bill. She objected to the minimum sentences, to
making the accused prove their innocence and to detention for questioning, and argued that a dagga
smoker should not be treated like a user of hard drugs.[@2]

## What the Act did

| First conviction | Prison |
|---|---|
| Possession | 2 to 10 years |
| Dealing | 5 to 15 years |

Repeat offenders faced five to fifteen years for possession and ten to twenty-five for dealing.[@1]
A sentence could be partly suspended, until a 1973 amendment ruled out suspending any part of the
minimum.[@1] Anyone found with more than 115 grams was presumed to be a dealer unless they proved
otherwise, a rule carried over from [1954](/history/south-africa-dealing-presumption-1954/).[@2]
The same Act created rehabilitation centres to which users could be committed.[@2]

## Softened, then replaced

A 1978 amendment began to set dagga apart from more dangerous drugs in sentencing.[@1] In
[1986](/history/south-africa-sentencing-discretion-1986/) parliament abolished the mandatory
minimums altogether, made fines possible and let courts suspend sentences in full; a judge later
wrote that this let courts fit the punishment to the person.[@4] The
[1992 Drugs and Drug Trafficking Act](/history/south-africa-drugs-act-1992/) then replaced the
1971 law.

Its convictions outlived it. The [2024 Cannabis for Private Purposes Act](/history/south-africa-private-purposes-act-2024/)
lists convictions for using or possessing dagga under the 1971 Act among those to be expunged, once
the Act comes into force.[@5] See [South Africa](/country/za/) for the law today.
