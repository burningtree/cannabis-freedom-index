Congress of Micronesia adopted a scheduled drug-control law during its special
session of 23 July–9 August 1974.[@1] The law belongs to a longer territorial
history: a marijuana prosecution decided in July had used an earlier, different
system for deciding which drugs were prohibited.[@2]

## The old rules behind the court case

Mariano Bermudes faced possession and sale charges in the Mariana Islands.
The old law defined drugs through their effects on consciousness, thought, mood
and perception, then left the health director to publish the list. Its marijuana
penalty could reach a year in prison, a $1,000 fine, or both.[@2]

On 23 July, Chief Justice Burnett dismissed the charges. The definition gave the
director insufficient guidance and left people unable to know what conduct the
law prohibited. The judge also found no rational basis for penalties that could
punish marijuana more heavily than opium or heroin. He accepted the government's
power to control drugs and left the private-use question unresolved.[@2] The
[Bermudes entry](/history/cnmi-bermudes-marijuana-charges-1974/) follows that case.

## Congress keeps control of the schedules

The later code's controlled-substances chapter names Public Law 5-110 as its
source. It lists marijuana and THC in Schedule I. Its marijuana definition covers
the plant and resin broadly, while excluding specified mature-stalk products and
sterilised seeds incapable of germination.[@3]

The health director has a different role in this text. The director assesses
abuse, medical hazards and other factors, then recommends changes. Congress has
sole authority to add, remove or reschedule substances. Notice and an opportunity
for a hearing normally precede a recommendation, with an exception for specified
federal scheduling notices.[@3]

## Small amounts have their own penalties

The [1980 territorial code](/history/micronesia-criminal-code-1980/) records a
maximum $50 fine for an ounce or less and for an unpaid gift of that amount.
Larger holdings have separate prison-or-fine bands. At 2.2 pounds or more,
possession creates a rebuttable presumption of trafficking, which the defendant
can contest.[@3]

## The rules reach Chuuk's later history

A report published in 1991, based on exploratory fieldwork in 1985, described
Chuuk relying on the former territorial drug rules. Its police-record discussion
also traced local enforcement back to
[a plant uprooted on Moen in 1978](/history/micronesia-chuuk-police-cannabis-1978/).[@4]
The [Micronesia profile](/country/fm/) follows the subsequent national and state
rules; the territorial histories also continue under
[Palau](/history/palau-controlled-substances-act-1974/) and the
[Northern Marianas](/history/cnmi-controlled-substances-act-1974/).
