Victoria passed a law in December 1927 penalising unauthorised use of cannabis
and its resin. The government brought cannabis into state poison controls
after the Commonwealth asked states to follow international drug agreements.
Federal officials had already restricted imports; Victoria was extending the
controls people faced within the state.

## The request came from the Commonwealth

[Australia's federal import restrictions](/history/australia-ban-1926/) had
confined covered cannabis supplies from overseas to licensed trade for
medicine and research. State governments were responsible for further rules
within their borders.

In September 1927, Public Health Minister W. J. Beckett explained why Victoria
was changing its poison laws. He told parliament that the Commonwealth had
asked all states to align their narcotics controls with the international
agreement reached at Geneva in 1925.

Beckett also pointed to changes in Britain's dangerous-drug law. Victoria
already regulated narcotic sales under earlier legislation, and the government
wanted its definitions and restrictions brought up to date. The
[Geneva agreement](/history/geneva-opium-convention-1925/) helped turn that
general ambition into another round of state legislation.

## Cannabis entered a much wider poison-law debate

The bill covered several problems that the government treated as requiring
tighter control over dangerous substances. Beckett discussed poisoned baits
killing livestock and native animals, people drinking methylated spirits,
and the risk of household poisons being mistaken for medicines or food.

The narcotics changes formed a separate part of that wider proposal. Beckett
argued that the state should place “every possible obstacle” in the way of
illicit drug traffickers. His explanation tied the new rules to international
control and updating an existing system for selling drugs.

For cannabis users, the resulting law carried a more direct consequence:
unauthorised use of Indian hemp, the legal name used for cannabis, and its resin
was penalised. The Poisons Act received assent on 21 December 1927, after both
houses had agreed on the bill.

## Other states followed their own timetables

Cannabis had appeared in Australian poison legislation before this change.
[Western Australia listed it in 1899](/history/australia-western-australia-cannabis-poison-schedule-1899/),
placing covered sales under pharmacy controls.

Broader state prohibitions spread over the following decades: South Australia
acted in 1934, New South Wales in 1935 and Queensland in 1937. Western Australia
and Tasmania followed later. Together with federal restrictions at the border,
these decisions built the overlapping controls that shaped
[Australia's cannabis policy](/country/au/).
