In March 1884, Egyptian Customs was permitted to sell confiscated hashish for delivery abroad rather than destroy it. According to the Indian Hemp Drugs Commission’s later account, proceeds supported rewards for informers and officers involved in seizures. The measure changed enforcement finance without establishing a lawful domestic cannabis market.

## A different kind of regulation

Paragraph 559 of the commission’s 1894 report describes the disposal arrangement within a chronology of Egyptian restrictions. The account quotes customs director Caillard explaining why a reward fund was needed.

The evidence used here is that contemporary retrospective report. The original Egyptian instrument has not been recovered and inspected for this article. Its precise legal form and full procedural conditions consequently remain open questions.

## Revenue from confiscation

The reported rule allowed Customs to sell seized material subject to delivery abroad. It divided the resulting proceeds among informers and officials who had taken part in the seizure.

The export condition is essential. Permission to dispose of confiscated goods through foreign delivery does not mean that ordinary residents could buy them legally in Egyptian shops. The account concerns what the authorities could do with seized property, rather than a general permission for domestic retail trade.

## The administration’s explanation

Caillard described the absence of a fund from which rewards could be paid. He also contrasted that problem with smugglers’ profits and their capacity to secure silence or complicity from officials.

This is the customs director’s explanation of the measure. It establishes the stated administrative problem without proving how much corruption occurred, how every sale was conducted, or whether rewards improved enforcement. Those outcomes would require additional records.

## Keeping the chronology distinct

The same report describes [importation and cultivation prohibition in March 1879](/history/egypt-import-ban-1879/). Its March 1884 disposal policy should therefore be distinguished from a claim that cultivation first became prohibited in 1884.

The database preserves the older [1884 cultivation entry](/history/egypt-cultivation-ban-1884/) with an explicit uncertainty notice while the original instrument is sought. This separate, documented event concerns disposal and rewards.

Placed in the history of [Egypt](/country/eg/), the rule illustrates how prohibition could generate further regulations governing confiscated material. It is unscored context because a change in customs disposal arrangements does not, by itself, establish a new legal regime for personal possession, cultivation or consumption.
