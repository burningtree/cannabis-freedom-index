In 1973 Afghan cannabis prohibition got serious — twice. That year King Zahir Shah outlawed cannabis production and committed to eradication with American money behind him, put at $47 million in the retrospective history.[@1] Then on 17 July his cousin Daoud seized power, and the new president publicly took up the same cause: in his 23 August independence-day speech he called for the "uprooting of hashish and opium" and a campaign against smuggling.[@1,2]

## The hippies made it urgent

The pressure had built with the hippie trail. Western youths poured across the border — the first Lonely Planet guide, published that year, joked about finding the Islam Qala customs post manned by stoned Americans smoking dope with the officials.[@1] What had been small-scale trade became a commercial enterprise exporting resin to Europe and North America, making Afghanistan a major supplier alongside Pakistan and Lebanon.[@1] In Washington in October 1971 the Afghan foreign minister admitted the problem: hashish and opium were prohibited, but the bans went unenforced, foreign tourists' demand kept growing, and the remote mountains where the crops grew were nearly ungovernable — while pledging full cooperation with America.[@4] The [smuggling crackdown of 1972](/history/afghanistan-smuggling-crackdown-1972/) followed, and the American embassy deliberately fed alarming foreign press coverage to Afghan officials and editors to push Kabul toward enforcement.[@5]

## Daoud renews the pledge

Daoud's republic needed American goodwill, and narcotics was the currency. His deputy foreign minister Wahid Abdullah repeated the anti-narcotics commitment on 28 August, and the US embassy recast its entire narcotics programme around the new regime.[@2] Enforcement sat with the Interior Ministry — being purged and reorganised after the coup — while the Justice Ministry kept an undefined role; the Americans planned to work cautiously through the new men in charge.[@2] The courts were already busy: that spring the press counted fifteen drug arrests, almost all hashish, and nine hashish verdicts, including an Afghan fined 30,000 afghanis with four months in jail while foreign defendants paid fines.[@3]

## Eradication promised, exports booming

The difficulty showed the same year, when a US cable detailed the king's own personal valet trying to smuggle 40 kilos of hashish oil to America.[@1] The 1973 pledges did not hold back the trade: production kept climbing for decades, through war and revolution.[@1] The [Taliban banned hashish again](/history/afghanistan-taliban-radio-hashish-ban-1997/) in 1997, and [again after returning to power](/history/afghanistan-taliban-cultivation-ban-2023/). The [country page](/country/af/) traces that long arc from the 1973 uprooting calls to today.
