East Germany passed a law against cannabis in 1973 although almost none of its citizens
had ever seen any. The law was about the border.

## A country without hashish

"Hashish in the GDR," one East German recalled, "was as hard to get your hands on as
explosives." The state credited socialism. The real protection was the currency:
smugglers had no interest in being paid in East German marks, so there was nothing to
gain by selling there.

The party nonetheless treated cannabis as a threat. From the early 1950s the press
presented it as proof of the decay of capitalism, in language close to American
anti-marijuana films of the 1930s.

## The transit problem

West Berlin lay inside East German territory, and by the late 1960s it had a drug scene.
The routes to it crossed the GDR.

In 1968 one person was caught bringing hashish into East Germany. In
[1970](/history/germany-gdr-transit-hashish-seizures-1970/) there were 328 seizures,
142 kilograms in all, nearly all of it hashish bound for West Berlin. Agreements in
1971 and 1972 opened transit roads and made the traffic easier. Much of the organised
smuggling ran through Schönefeld airport, where the state airline flew in from the
Levant. Couriers with a few grams were fined; organised smugglers with 5 to 20
kilograms got one to nine years.

## The law

Until then the only drug law was the 1929 Opium Law, which the Soviet occupation
authority had [reinstated in 1945](/history/germany-soviet-zone-opium-law-1945/). In
1973 the People's Chamber passed the Narcotic Drugs Act.

- Producing, trafficking and consuming cannabis became crimes, along with every
  narcotic in the UN conventions.
- A Central Bureau for Addictive Substances was set up in the health ministry.
- The few addicts in the country were to be registered and could be confined.

East Germany [joined the UN conventions](/history/germany-gdr-conventions-1975/) in 1975.
With almost no domestic cases to study, its experts took their science from the West,
including the theory of cannabis as a gateway to heroin.

## The end

The Stasi folded drugs into its view of the border, linking hashish smugglers with
people who helped East Germans escape. When the Wall opened in November 1989, cannabis
arrived with everything else, and in early 1990 the interim government admitted that
drug imports had become a public health problem it had not prepared for. East Berlin
police created a [narcotics working group](/history/germany-east-berlin-narcotics-group-1990/)
that year.
