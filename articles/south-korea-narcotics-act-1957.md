South Korea put “Indian hemp” into its new narcotics law on 23 June 1957.
The definition named Cannabis sativa L., its seeds, resin and products,
while leaving mature stalks, products made from them and seeds that could
not germinate outside the definition.[@1] Cannabis had entered the drug-control
system, but the country's own hemp would remain at the centre of a dispute
over what the law actually covered.[@2]

## A law built around licensed handlers

The law replaced earlier American military-government drug rules. It
restricted the handling of listed narcotics to licensed people, with
specified exceptions, and prohibited unauthorised use and cultivation of
plants supplying narcotics. Unlicensed possession and prohibited cultivation
could bring up to ten years in prison; unauthorised use could bring up to
five.[@1]

Medicine was no general escape route. A separate provision prohibited
making medicines from Indian hemp and using, administering or supplying
those medicines for administration.[@1] At the same time, the mature-stalk
and non-germinating-seed exceptions preserved a distinction between the
plant's drug-bearing material and some ordinary products.[@1]

## Why Korean hemp kept circulating

The Constitutional Court later placed the law in the aftermath of colonial
opium production and the Korean War. Its account describes the 1957 Act as
replacing military-government controls with a framework based on
international drug agreements.[@3]

Historian Matt VanVolkenburg's account of contemporary newspapers describes
a gap between that framework and Korean hemp: the authorities treated the
law's “Indian marijuana” category as leaving local hemp alone. By the early
1960s it was sold around American bases as “happy smoke”. US military
authorities began asking Seoul to close the gap in 1966.[@2]

The statute itself used the plant name rather than a requirement that the
cannabis had been imported. The historical account describes how Korean
hemp was treated; the legal text shows a wider set of controls attached to
the category it named.[@1,2]

## The distinction breaks down

A bill addressing habit-forming drugs finally passed in July 1970 after
years of American pressure and reports about student marijuana smoking.[@2]
The [1970 entry](/history/south-korea-habit-forming-drugs-law-1970/)
follows that change, and the [1975 arrests](/history/south-korea-entertainers-arrested-1975/)
follow its extension beyond the camp towns.[@2] A separate
[Cannabis Control Act](/history/south-korea-cannabis-control-act-1976/)
followed in 1976, a sequence also recorded in the Constitutional Court's
legislative history.[@3] The [South Korea profile](/country/kr/) carries
the story forward.
