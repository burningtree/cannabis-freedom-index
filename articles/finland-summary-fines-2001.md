On 1 September 2001 Finland gave drug users an offence of their own. Law 654/2001 added section 2a to chapter 50 of the Penal Code: anyone who unlawfully uses a narcotic, or possesses or tries to obtain a small amount for personal use, faces a fine or at most six months in prison — half the ordinary drug offence's two-year maximum.[@1,2]

## A lesser crime, not a non-crime

The reform sorted rather than pardoned. Use and small-quantity possession moved from the ordinary narcotics offence into the new milder box, but criminal liability stayed: police still record the act, and the statute still allows imprisonment.[@1] Revised section 7 let prosecution or punishment be waived for minor cases, or where the person sought approved treatment.[@1]

## The waiver

Section 7 gave prosecutors and courts a way out: punishment can be waived for minor cases, and prosecution or punishment can be dropped where the offender sought approved treatment.[@1] The waiver is discretionary, not a right — the same act that qualifies for treatment-based leniency can equally end in a fine. That duality, help or punishment at the system's choice, has marked Finnish drug policy ever since.

## Why a separate box matters

The 2a offence carries its own procedural track. The drug-use offence can be dealt with by a summary fine, and the Police Board's tables set predictable day-fine bands — ordinarily 10 day-fines for hash or marijuana up to 10 grams.[@2] That made small cannabis cases routine paperwork instead of court cases, while keeping them convictions: the fine lands in police registers and can affect security clearances, some professions and a driving licence.

Critics note the box did not shrink the system so much as streamline it — more fines, fewer dropped cases. Supporters note it spared users the two-year shadow of the ordinary offence. The 2001 compromise still defines [Finland's](/country/fi/) cannabis law: use is a crime, but a small, standardised one.
