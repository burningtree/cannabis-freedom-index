Tajikistan’s Supreme Court told judges in June 2004 to apply a recent criminal-law reform to people already sentenced. Where the reform removed an offence, judges were to release the person; where it lowered a penalty, the sentence had to fit the new maximum.[@1]

## The reform applied to existing prisoners

The court’s guidance identified 22 May 2004 as the reform’s commencement date, following its adoption earlier that month. It told courts hearing cases and appeals to take the changed law into account.[@1]

For people already imprisoned, a review could begin through an application by the prisoner, a submission from the prison administration or a prosecutor’s submission. The guidance explained which court would handle the review, including cases involving people serving their sentences outside Tajikistan.[@1]

## A lighter maximum could shorten a sentence

The principle was that a change improving a convicted person’s legal position could affect an earlier judgment. The Supreme Court directed judges to reduce punishment to the new statutory maximum when the relevant penalty had been lowered.[@1]

It also required proceedings to end and prisoners to be released where the changed law removed criminal liability for their conduct. The public guidance separately addressed some juveniles convicted for the first time of lower-severity offences, directing a switch away from imprisonment.[@1]

## Drug offences used different quantity bands

In the later consolidated Criminal Code, personal possession and unauthorised cultivation were divided into quantity bands. The lowest band allowed a fine or up to two years in prison, while higher bands and aggravating circumstances brought longer terms. Those basic alternatives were already present in the earlier consolidation published through WIPO.[@2,3]

Drug control also involved the [general law adopted in 1999](/history/tajikistan-narcotics-law-1999/). Its later text reserved scientific cultivation for licensed research institutions and prohibited non-prescribed drug use. The criminal offences operated alongside those licensing and health rules.[@4]

## Later reviewers examined life beyond the sentence

A [2010 drug-policy review](/history/tajikistan-drug-policy-review-2010/) brought officials and civil society together to examine policing, treatment and patient protections. That review forms a later part of the country’s debate about how drug control affected people.[@5]

The [Tajikistan profile](/country/tj/) explains the later statutory penalties. The 2004 court guidance shows the route by which a person already sentenced could ask a judge to apply a more favourable law.
