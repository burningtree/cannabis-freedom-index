On 29 January 2004 cannabis became a Class C drug in the United Kingdom. It had been in Class B since the class system began.

## The order

The change was made by a short statutory instrument, the Misuse of Drugs Act 1971 (Modification) (No. 2) Order 2003, made on 10 December 2003.

| Substance | Before | From 29 January 2004 |
|---|---|---|
| Cannabis and cannabis resin | Class B | Class C |
| Cannabinol and its derivatives | Class A | Class C |

For possession, the maximum sentence fell from five years to two.

## How it came about

The Home Secretary, David Blunkett, announced the plan in 2001. The government's own Advisory Council on the Misuse of Drugs had recommended the lower class as early as 1979, and an independent inquiry, the Runciman Report, endorsed it in 1999. A poll at the time found 49% of adults in favour of decriminalising cannabis and 36% against.

The stated purpose was practical: to let the police concentrate on more serious offences and harder drugs. The transfer was held back until the penalties for supplying Class C drugs had been stiffened.

## What happened next

The government reported that arrests for cannabis possession fell by a third in the first year, saving an estimated 199,000 police hours. In January 2006 the Home Secretary, Charles Clarke, decided on the Advisory Council's advice to leave cannabis in Class C. Three years later a different Home Secretary [moved it back](/history/uk-class-b-2009/).

## Why the index moved

The index moved the [United Kingdom](/country/gb/) from 14 to 20 with the [2004 reclassification](/history/uk-class-c-2004/), the largest rise in its record.

| Segment | Before | After |
|---|---|---|
| Possession | 1 | 2 |
| Enforcement | 3 | 4 |
| Consumption | 1 | 2 |

Possession remained a crime, which is why the gain is modest. Cultivation and sharing stay at 1, since the penalties for growing and supplying did not fall.
