Ceylon’s Opium and Bhang Ordinance of 1878 combined licensing restrictions with an express exception for medicinal use. A contemporary court report preserves the relevant provisions and shows why the meaning of “medical practitioner” mattered to the law’s operation.

## Licensed trade and medicinal use

The report of *Jansz v. Usubu Lebbe* describes section 10 of Ordinance No. 4 of 1878 as penalizing possession or sale of opium or bhang without a licence. Section 4 included preparations containing either substance. The restriction therefore reached mixtures as well as the separately named drugs.

Section 13 preserved medicinal possession and retail sale by medical practitioners or druggists. Its requirement was that the activity be genuinely for medicinal purposes. The provision should not be read as general permission for every seller or every use of bhang.

## A decision about the practitioner

The reported appeal came from the Galle police court. The defendant had sold legium, described in the judgment as an opium preparation. The magistrate convicted him after construing the exception as applying only to qualified medical practitioners.

Chief Justice Burnside set the conviction aside on **17 June 1891**. Because the ordinance did not define the expression, he applied its ordinary meaning. A witness, identified as a vedarala, testified that the defendant practised medicine and that the preparation was used medicinally. Burnside regarded that evidence as sufficient for the exception and ordered an acquittal.

This was an opium prosecution, not a finding about the contents of a cannabis medicine. Its relevance to cannabis history lies in the common statutory exception, which the report expressly reproduces for both opium and bhang. It illustrates how a court interpreted access to that exception without requiring the qualification test applied by the magistrate.

## Limits of the record

The decision belongs to 1891, although volume I of *The Ceylon Law Reports* bears a publication date of 1892. The year of the later printed volume must not be substituted for the judgment date.

The original 1878 ordinance and its commencement proclamations were not retrieved. The [1897 legislation](/history/sri-lanka-import-ban-1897/) expressly extended the older ordinance to places outside its previous operation. This entry therefore records the documented licensing and medicinal provisions without assuming that they immediately applied throughout the island in 1878, or that this was Ceylon’s first cannabis restriction.
