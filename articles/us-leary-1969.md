The law that began federal cannabis prohibition in the United States was struck down by
the Supreme Court in 1969.[@2] The man who brought the case was the country's best-known
advocate of psychedelic drugs.[@1]

## The arrest

On 22 December 1965 Timothy Leary was driving back into the United States with his
family across the international bridge at Laredo, Texas. At the inspection point officers
searched the car. They found marijuana seeds and small amounts on the floor and in the
glove compartment, and a silver snuff box with some marijuana and three partly smoked
cigarettes on his daughter.[@1,2]

He was charged under the
[Marihuana Tax Act](/history/us-marihuana-tax-act-1937/) of 1937.[@2]

## The trap in the law

The Act did not forbid marijuana. It taxed it. Anyone acquiring cannabis was supposed
to register, obtain an order form and pay a tax, and it was failing to do so that was
the federal offence.[@2]

Leary's argument was that the scheme could not be obeyed without confessing. To comply,
he would have had to identify himself to the government as someone acquiring marijuana
without being registered, and so hand over evidence of a crime.[@2]

## The ruling

The case was argued in December 1968 and decided on 19 May 1969. Justice John Marshall
Harlan wrote the opinion. Compliance, the court held, created a "real and appreciable"
hazard of self-incrimination, contrary to the Fifth Amendment. Leary's conviction was
reversed.[@2]

The fiction that had made the Act possible in 1937, that it was a revenue measure and
not a ban, was the thing that undid it.[@1,2]

## What replaced it

The ruling left federal law with a hole in it, though every state still had its own
prohibition. Congress filled the hole within eighteen months. The
[Controlled Substances Act](/history/us-controlled-substances-act-1970/) of October 1970
dropped the pretence of taxation, banned the drug directly and placed it in Schedule I.

So the decision that freed Leary produced a stronger law than the one it removed. It
did bring one change for the better: the new Act abolished the mandatory minimum
sentences of the 1950s.[@1]

The index lists the ruling for context. Between May 1969 and October 1970 a person's
position under state law did not change, so it moved no score for the
[United States](/country/us/).
