Yugoslavia’s 1950 narcotics law prohibited growing Indian hemp, with an exception for specified scientific institutions working on their own experimental fields.[@1] The federal framework covered Macedonia as one of Yugoslavia’s republics.[@1] It placed narcotic cultivation, manufacture and trade under state control as part of the economic plan.[@1]

## Cannabis appeared as both a crop and a preparation

The law listed Indian hemp among controlled raw drugs and separately listed its extracts, tinctures and resins.[@1] Scientific growers needed to follow conditions and quantities prescribed by the federal public-health authority.[@1] The exception therefore concerned controlled institutional work, rather than an allowance for households to grow a few plants.[@1]

Manufacture and preparation were limited to medical and scientific purposes and reserved to appointed public undertakings.[@1] Retail distribution used pharmacies, dispensaries and specified veterinary outlets, while import and export required an authorised state undertaking and consignment permissions.[@1]

## Controls included records and criminal penalties

Businesses and dispensing outlets had to keep special registers of drugs received and supplied.[@1] Unlawful cultivation or manufacture for gain and unlawful drug trading could bring corrective labour or up to two years in prison, with aggravated cases receiving heavier punishment.[@1] Import and export offences had their own penalties, and drugs involved were subject to confiscation.[@1]

The promulgating decree was dated 23 January 1950, while the Act provided for commencement on publication in the federal gazette.[@1] The January date consequently identifies the decree rather than an independently verified gazette publication day.[@1]

## Later frameworks replaced the state-controlled model

A legal study traces the later federal framework to the [1964 narcotics act](/history/north-macedonia-yugoslav-narcotics-act-1964/), describing continued cannabis restrictions and a distinction between minor-offence possession and drug use itself.[@2] Macedonia’s [2008 replacement law](/history/north-macedonia-drug-control-law-2008/) subsequently repealed a republic authorities law from 1983 and ended application of the federal production-and-trade law from 1991.[@3]

The [2016 medical reform](/history/north-macedonia-medical-2016/) opened a corporate cultivation route requiring health ministry approval, government consent and agriculture ministry planting permission.[@3] Current application guidance asks for premises documentation, staff, a cultivation plan and continuous physical security.[@4] The [North Macedonia profile](/country/mk/) records that licensed route separately from the rules for personal possession and unlicensed growing.[@3,4]
