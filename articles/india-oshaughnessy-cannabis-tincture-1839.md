William Brooke O'Shaughnessy's 1839 paper gave British medicine its most
systematic early account of Indian cannabis preparations. Written in Calcutta,
it joined reports from Indian practice to experiments on animals and hospital
patients.

## Testing instead of repeating hearsay

O'Shaughnessy was an assistant surgeon in the Indian Medical Service and later
a professor at Calcutta's medical college. His paper, "On the Preparations of
the Indian Hemp or Gunjah (Cannabis Indica)", appeared in the *Transactions of
the Medical and Physical Society of Bengal*.

He first observed cannabis's effects across several animal species, then
administered preparations to people. Historian James H. Mills presents this as
an unusually comprehensive British scientific assessment for colonial India,
while also cautioning that O'Shaughnessy's professional ambitions may have
contributed to his enthusiasm.

The method combined descriptions of the plant and earlier medical writing with
O'Shaughnessy's own experiments. He also compared cannabis's adverse effects
with those associated with alcohol, opium and tobacco rather than treating the
drug in isolation.

## What the cases claimed

The clinical reports covered severe rheumatism, rabies symptoms, cholera,
tetanus, infantile convulsions and delirium tremens. O'Shaughnessy described
sedative and pain-relieving effects in rheumatism. Cannabis did not cure the
rabies case, but he reported relief from hydrophobia. In cholera cases he
observed reduced vomiting and diarrhoea and induced rest.

He was most confident about cannabis as an anticonvulsant. His tetanus cases
included recoveries, and he reported using the drug in an infant suffering
convulsions. The paper was not a modern controlled trial: it was a collection
of experiments and clinical observations, and Mills advises caution when
reading its conclusions.

## From paper to dispensatory

The work circulated beyond the original journal. O'Shaughnessy's *Bengal
Dispensatory* of 1842 devoted twenty-five pages to cannabis. His 1844 *Bengal
Pharmacopoeia* described indications and dosing and supplied a formula for a
tincture prepared from ganja tops and rectified spirit.

These later manuals turned the journal cases into material that doctors could
consult as a dispensatory entry, including a preparation method rather than
clinical anecdotes alone.

The journal paper and the two later manuals made O'Shaughnessy a central figure
in the nineteenth-century medical history of cannabis.
