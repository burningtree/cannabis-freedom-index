*Reefer Madness* turned a warning about cannabis into a melodrama of endangered youth. Originally titled *Tell Your Children*, it became one of the best-known images of American anti-cannabis campaigning, and later a film watched for the absurdity of its warnings.[@1,4]

Its dating needs care. The American Film Institute and British Film Institute catalogue it as 1938; Cornell Cinema uses the familiar 1936 date.[@1,2,3] This history entry follows AFI's catalogue year while preserving the disagreement. The title by which audiences now recognise it also belongs to its complicated distribution history.

## A warning performed as a story

AFI identifies Louis Gasnier as director and George Hirliman as producer.[@1] Its account records a foreword claiming that fictionalised scenes rest on research into marijuana addiction.[@1] The plot then joins youthful smoking to sexual danger, death and mental collapse, framed by a school principal's warning to parents.[@1]

The claim to research is part of the film's rhetoric. A dramatic plot supplies no evidence that the sequence it invents is a normal result of using cannabis. Its combination of authority, parental anxiety and sensational incident is what makes it useful as a propaganda artifact.

## Education and exploitation

The film also circulated as commercial entertainment. Later distribution and retitling placed its apparent moral lesson alongside the attraction of scandalous material. AFI records reissues as *The Burning Question* and *Reefer Madness*.[@1]

This is why it should not simply be described as a government documentary. A film can advance an anti-drug message while also serving an exploitation market. Calling the film propaganda describes its persuasive framing; it does not establish that a federal agency commissioned or financed it.

## Audiences reverse the lesson

The BFI describes the picture's later embrace as an underground classic.[@2] Accounts of its early-1970s revival link it to campus screenings and cannabis-law reform fundraising.[@4] The same exaggerated behaviour that was meant to frighten an audience could invite a later audience to laugh at the warning.

That reversal is part of the artifact's history. It is also different from proving that the original release caused a particular law. The [1937 Marihuana Tax Act](/history/us-marihuana-tax-act-1937/) needs its own documentary explanation, and is not explained by the film alone.

## The word outlives the warning

The title borrowed [reefer](/culture/language/reefer/), a word associated with cannabis cigarettes and jazz-era speech. Its later cultural life made that slang inseparable from a film in which users were represented through catastrophe. The poster remains evidence of how cannabis was advertised as a threat, rather than evidence that its pictured claims were true.
