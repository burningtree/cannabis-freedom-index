In 1925 Bulgaria prohibited growing cannabis for resin, closing a six-year detour in the hashish trade.[@1] Traffickers had brought resin cultivation into the country in 1919 and 1920, after Greece — Egypt's main supplier — banned it.[@1]

## Egypt's ban set the whole region moving

Egyptians had produced and consumed hashish for centuries, until around 1880 the authorities outlawed cultivation, sale and consumption, burned the fields and raided the coffee houses.[@2] Demand did not disappear; smuggling systems grew up to feed it.[@2]

Across the Mediterranean, Greece became Egypt's main supplier.[@2] Then Greek politics turned: the government prohibited production and burned cannabis fields, just as Egypt had done forty years earlier.[@2] Greek farmers lost their income, Egypt lost its most abundant channel, and the trade moved north.[@2]

## The Balkans farmed for Egypt for a decade

Through the 1920s, Bulgarian, Romanian and Yugoslav farmers grew hashish for the Egyptian market.[@1,2] A 1951 United Nations survey reconstructed the sequence from League records: traffickers introduced resin cultivation to Bulgaria in 1919–1920 and Bulgaria prohibited it in 1925; Romania prohibited cultivation in 1926; Yugoslavia took up growing around 1927 and prohibited it in 1932; Albania followed the same pattern.[@1]

Turkey played the swing role: it had always grown hashish, intensified production when the Greek supply dried up, and then passed its own 1933 law prohibiting cultivation, holding, use and trade.[@1] Greece itself had to legislate twice — the 1920 cultivation ban, then a more drastic 1932 law ordering hidden resin stocks destroyed.[@1]

At Geneva in 1925, Egypt's delegate El-Guindi pushed hashish into the international trade controls, seeking to choke the supply his country's ban could not stop alone.[@2]

## Lebanon inherited the trade

Each Balkan ban pushed production onward: after Greece fell out, whole Lebanese valleys were turned over to cannabis cultivation and Palestine became the corridor to Egypt.[@2] The trade even reached Paris, where clandestine hashish plots were found at Orly, Aubervilliers and Le Bourget.[@1]

Bans did not end use: the survey praised governments' efforts to trace and destroy secret plantations, but noted clandestine consumption persisting in Turkey, Bulgaria and Greece.[@1] The 1925 Bulgarian ban was one link in that chain — the moment [Bulgaria](/country/bg/) chose prohibition over supplying Egypt's demand.
