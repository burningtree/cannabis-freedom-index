Six years after Hungary introduced [diversion](/history/hungary-diversion-1993/), the choice of a treatment programme in place of prosecution, the first government of Viktor Orbán took it away from most of the people who used it. The change took effect on 1 March 1999 and lasted four years.

## The restriction

The Orbán government came to office in 1998 and tightened the drug provisions of the criminal code. From then on only a person diagnosed as drug-dependent by an expert forensic psychiatrist was offered the alternative to punishment. Everyone else caught with a small amount for personal use went before a criminal court.

## What followed

The overview of Hungarian diversion read here, by a Budapest drug policy organisation, records two effects.

- The number of people prosecuted and sentenced rose significantly, and with it the workload of prosecutors and judges.
- The assessment of dependence varied widely from one offender to the next and was regarded as chaotic by many professionals.

## The end of it

A Socialist and Liberal coalition won the 2002 election and abolished the restriction. From [2003](/history/hungary-diversion-widened-2003/) diversion was again open to anyone caught with drugs for personal use.

A study of one Budapest provider shows the swing. After diversion was reopened to non-dependent users, the share of its clients who had been sent by the criminal justice system rose from 24% to 72.6%.

## A second narrowing

The pattern returned in 2013, under the second Orbán government, with a new criminal code that offers diversion only to a person who has not re-offended within two years.

## Why the index moved

The index moved [Hungary](/country/hu/) from 12 to 8 with the [1999 restriction](/history/hungary-diversion-restricted-1999/). Enforcement falls from 3 to 1, back to where it stood before 1993, because an ordinary user caught with cannabis once more faced a criminal court and a record.
