British Guiana placed the sale of ganja and bhang under licence in 1861.
Ordinance No. 22 brought cannabis into colonial regulation decades before the
international drug-control treaties of the twentieth century.

Jamie Banks's history locates the measure alongside official concerns about
opium among Chinese immigrants. Cannabis was included before the colony's
government treated its use as a substantial problem in its own right.

## Migration and the colonial market

British Guiana, now [Guyana](/country/gy/), was a major destination for Indian
indentured workers after emancipation. Migrants brought cultural practices as
well as labour, including knowledge and use of cannabis.

Banks describes the movement of cannabis practices and colonial medical ideas
across the empire. Officials' judgments about intoxicants developed within a
society organised around plantation labour and racial distinctions. Their
reports therefore need to be read as evidence of colonial priorities as well
as accounts of consumption.

## Sales required permission

The ordinance placed ganja and bhang sales under a licensing system. It should
be distinguished from a complete prohibition of every cannabis-related act:
the chapter establishes regulated sale, without supplying grounds to claim
that all possession or consumption became criminal in 1861.

Banks draws on historian Peter Hanoomansingh's interpretation of the measure
as anticipatory. The government acted before cannabis had acquired the status
of a major local concern. The immediate policy setting included opium, rather
than a cannabis crisis demonstrated by the evidence cited in the chapter.

## Later medical arguments had a different reception

Banks contrasts British Guiana with Trinidad, where asylum officials were more
successful in mobilising support for restrictions. In British Guiana, Robert
Grieve's later claims connecting cannabis with mental illness met a less
enthusiastic governmental response.

That comparison helps explain the earlier licensing measure. A colony could
regulate cannabis without accepting every subsequent claim that it produced
violence or mental disorder. Banks treats those claims as historically situated
medical arguments, shaped by professional ambitions and racial assumptions.
They are not presented here as established clinical findings.
