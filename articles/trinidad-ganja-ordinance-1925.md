In 1925 Trinidad and Tobago banned ganja. The colony had spent the previous ten years
licensing and taxing it.

## From licence to ban

Under the [Ganja Ordinance of 1915](/history/trinidad-ganja-licensing-1915/) cannabis
passed through bonded warehouses to licensed sellers, on the model of Bengal. In 1925
the colonial legislature replaced the licence with a prohibition.

The date is not a coincidence of the calendar. In February 1925 the
[Geneva convention](/history/geneva-opium-convention-1925/) put Indian hemp under
international control for the first time, and across the British Empire colonial
governments began rewriting their drug laws around it. The sources read for this
article do not record the debate in Trinidad itself.

## Who it fell on

Ganja had come to the islands with indentured workers from India, and its use was
associated with them. Colonial doctors had argued since the 1880s that it filled the
asylum with Indian patients, a claim that officials in
[Mauritius](/history/mauritius-officials-find-no-harm-1894/) and the
[Indian Hemp Drugs Commission](/history/india-hemp-drugs-commission-1894/) had found
little evidence for.

## Ninety-four years

The ban outlasted the empire that imposed it. It was carried into the law of
independent [Trinidad and Tobago](/country/tt/), where possession could mean years in
prison, until [December 2019](/history/trinidad-decriminalisation-2019/). Since then
adults may hold 30 grams and grow four plants. No legal market was created, and in
[October 2026](/history/trinidad-age-limit-bill-2026/) the Senate voted to raise the
minimum age to 25.

In the index the 1925 ordinance took Trinidad from a licensed system to prohibition, a
fall on every score.
