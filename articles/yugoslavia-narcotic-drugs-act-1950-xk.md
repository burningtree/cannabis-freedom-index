People in Kosovo could no longer grow Indian hemp under Yugoslavia’s 1950 narcotics law, except through specified scientific institutions working on their own experimental fields.[@1] The federal law put drug cultivation, manufacture and trade under state control as part of the economic plan.[@1]

## Scientific institutions had a narrow exception

The act listed Indian hemp among controlled raw drugs, while placing its extracts, tinctures and resins in a separate group of preparations.[@1] Growing Indian hemp was prohibited, with the public-health authority setting the conditions and quantities for the scientific exception.[@1] Manufacturing and preparing narcotic drugs was reserved to appointed public undertakings for medical and scientific purposes.[@1]

The decree was dated 23 January 1950, after the federal councils’ meeting on 21 January.[@1] The law itself specified commencement on publication in the federal official gazette.[@1]

## Trading could bring imprisonment

Unlawful drug production for gain and unlawful selling, purchasing or transferring drugs could bring correctional labour or up to two years in prison.[@1] Import and export offences carried a separate maximum of three years, with higher penalties for the aggravated cases specified in the act.[@1] Drugs involved in those offences had to be confiscated.[@1]

## Kosovo later adopted its own restrictions

The [2008 narcotics law](/history/kosovo-narcotics-law-2008/) placed cannabis, resin, extracts and tinctures in Table I.[@2] Activities involving that table were restricted to scientific research, education and police purposes.[@2] The law also prohibited cultivation of plants from which narcotics could be obtained.[@2]

Its definition of growing plants for narcotic production included obtaining and holding seeds, sowing, raising plants and receiving or possessing the plant parts needed to obtain drugs.[@2] Research institutions needed a licence to acquire, possess or use the listed plants and substances.[@2] For Table I, the Interior Ministry issued that licence after approval by the narcotics committee.[@2]

## Small possession still reached the criminal courts

Under the code that [took effect in 2013](/history/kosovo-criminal-code-2013/), a first offender holding less than three grams of cannabis for personal use faced a fine or up to one year in prison.[@3] Ordinary unauthorised possession carried a fine and one to three years, and growing cannabis to produce drugs carried one to ten years.[@3]

The [Kosovo profile](/country/xk/) describes the present rules, while the history traces the earlier federal restrictions and the later laws governing users, growers and researchers.[@1,2,3]
