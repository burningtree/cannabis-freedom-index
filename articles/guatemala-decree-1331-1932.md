In February 1932 Guatemala's Decree 1331 set penalties for cultivating
"lethal plants" and preparing their soporific principles, and for
introducing, transporting or trafficking them, expressly naming Indian hemp
and marihuana alongside poppy and opium.[@1] It was the country's first
cannabis ban, and it punished the trade end to end, from the field to the
sale.[@1]

## Penalties on paper

The decree appears in a 1935 national police memoir that lists the era's
laws, a sign of how freshly the ban sat in the force's own
paperwork.[@1] The wording was broad: growing the plants, preparing their
soporific principles, and every step of moving the product counted as
punishable.[@1] Indian hemp and marihuana were named in the same breath as
the poppy and opium poppy products the police already knew.[@1]

## Growers pursued

The same memoir shows the ban being used. David Muñoz Salazar was held for
growing marihuana and preparing its derivatives, running the traffic through
two accomplices.[@1] Another man, Enrique Gándara, was detained in the
capital's Central Park.[@1] The cases sit in the memoir's crime reports as
ordinary police work, not as novelties.[@1]

## Enforcement on a large scale

A 1973 penal code punished sowing and cultivating plants and trafficking
drugs, and the 1992 narcoactivity law widened those concepts before the
civil war ended.[@3] Seizures ran into the millions of plants a year: 14
million cannabis plants confiscated in 2011, after 10.8 million in 2008 and
4.3 million in 2009.[@3] A 2005 survey put general-population cannabis use
at 0.13 percent.[@3]

## A ban that outlasted its challengers

The prohibition survived every later challenge. A 1992 drug-trafficking law
set separate prison terms for personal cannabis possession.[@2] In 2012
President Otto Pérez Molina called for decriminalising drugs, with 41
percent of Guatemalans backing the idea in a poll, but no law
followed.[@2] In 2016 a congressional committee rejected a bill to regulate
growing, sale and medical and recreational use as unfeasible, inopportune
and unconstitutional, though its sponsor vowed to keep trying.[@2] The
timeline runs through the [1992 narcoactivity law](/history/guatemala-ley-contra-la-narcoactividad-1992/),
the [2012 decriminalisation call](/history/guatemala-decriminalisation-call-2012/)
and the [rejected 2016 regulation](/history/guatemala-regulation-rejected-2016/);
the [Guatemala profile](/country/gt/) follows the later history.[@2]
