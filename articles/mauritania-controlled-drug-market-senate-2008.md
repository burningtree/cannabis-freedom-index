Mauritania's Senate voted 23 to five on 9 January 2008 for a bill controlling the legal supply of narcotic and psychotropic drugs. Opposition senators feared it would permit drug cultivation and international trade, while the government argued that medical supplies and treaty obligations required clear controls.

## A severe criminal law stood behind the debate

The [1993 drug law](/history/mauritania-drug-law-1993/) already prescribed fifteen to thirty years and a fine for high-risk drug production and trafficking. Repeat offences carried death, and specified aggravating circumstances could also lead to capital punishment.

Simple illicit use had its own maximum of two years, a fine alone, or both. Treatment could prevent prosecution. That law's categories depended on schedules set by the health minister, making the classification and authorised handling of drugs central to the system.

## Senators disagreed over authorised cultivation

The government brought an 87-article bill with three annexes before a sitting chaired by Senate president Bâ Mamadou, known as M'baré. It covered classification, medical and scientific activities, chemicals used in illegal manufacture, and penalties for breaches.

Some opposition senators asked for a delay and revision because they feared permission to grow, import and export drugs. Others pressed for tighter pharmacy licensing and safeguards against misuse of authorised substances.

## Treaty pressure shaped the government's case

Health Minister Mohamed Lemine Ould Raghani pointed to the UN drug conventions of 1961, 1971 and 1988. He said failure to implement them had nearly caused a rupture with the international drug-control board. He promised to address senators' recommendations when preparing implementing rules.

The [1961 convention, which Mauritania joined in 1989](/history/mauritania-single-convention-1989/), limits controlled drugs to medical and scientific purposes. The debate concerned how to organise that authorised supply alongside criminal prohibition.

## Deputies then pressed for prescription controls

At the [National Assembly's vote on 21 February](/history/mauritania-controlled-drug-market-assembly-2008/), the minister described strict oversight of narcotic medicines, from manufacturing and imports to prescriptions and pharmacy dispensing.

Deputies wanted uncontrolled medicines brought under supervision while protecting pharmacy outlets. The minister pledged rigorous enforcement. For doctors, pharmacists and medicine suppliers in [Mauritania](/country/mr/), authorised handling of controlled drugs was becoming a focus of parliamentary scrutiny alongside the existing criminal penalties.
