Timor-Leste’s own drug law took effect on 26 January 2017. It kept personal cannabis use, possession and growing as criminal offences, with up to one year in prison or a fine in the lower quantity band. It also allowed punishment to be waived for an occasional user in that band and protected the confidentiality of people seeking treatment.[@1]

## Parliament replaced an inherited legal regime

The [final parliamentary vote](/history/timor-leste-drug-bill-passed-2016/) took place on 18 October 2016. The government presented the bill as part of its public-safety programme, while describing drug use as a health problem requiring treatment and assistance.[@2]

The published law explained why a domestic replacement was needed. Courts had relied on Indonesian legislation carried over under Timor-Leste’s constitutional continuity rules. The preamble criticised that inherited regime’s incompatibility with the country’s constitutional principles, including its absolute prohibition of the death penalty.[@1]

The new law was promulgated on 23 January, published on 25 January and commenced the following day.[@1]

## Personal use had its own penalty bands

The personal-use offence covered consumption and activities solely for personal consumption, including growing, producing, buying and possessing controlled drugs. Its lower band allowed up to one year in prison or a fine of up to 120 daily units.[@1]

Above five times the daily reference quantity, the ceiling rose to two years or a fine of up to 240 daily units. The annex listed daily references of one gram for herbal cannabis, half a gram for resin and a quarter gram for oil. Those figures determined penalty bands within a criminal offence.[@1]

Unauthorised trafficking outside the personal-use offence carried five to fifteen years. A considerably less serious trafficking case had a separate one-to-five-year range.[@1]

## Seeking help carried confidentiality protections

People asking a doctor or health institution for help were guaranteed anonymity and individual treatment. Medical staff had professional-secrecy duties and were not required to report the patient’s identity or treatment to the criminal authorities.[@1]

The law required the Health Ministry to arrange free assistance for people presenting voluntarily. It also allowed prison to be suspended when a person with drug dependence voluntarily accepted treatment under court supervision.[@1]

## Courts and politicians continued to shape the debate

Later in 2017, Lusa reported a [three-and-a-half-year sentence for Tony Crean](https://tracker.cannabisfreedom.fyi/report/timor-leste-cannabis-plants-conviction-2017/) in a cannabis-plant case, with his defence announcing an appeal.[@3]

The [2022 debate over cannabis policy](/history/timor-leste-ramos-horta-speech-2022/) returned to the relationship between punishment, health and policing. The [Timor-Leste profile](/country/tl/) explains the law’s personal-use rules, trafficking penalties and treatment provisions.[@1]
