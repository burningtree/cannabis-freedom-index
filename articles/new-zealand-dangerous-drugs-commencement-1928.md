On 1 January 1928 cannabis came under New Zealand drug law for the first time: producing, manufacturing, selling, distributing or otherwise dealing in the plant became an offence unless done under a licence or some other lawful authority.[@1,2] The plant entered the statute book under the name Indian hemp, grouped with opium, morphine and cocaine as a dangerous drug.[@1,2]

## An international treaty set the timetable

The Geneva convention on narcotics had extended its controls to cannabis, then known as Indian hemp, in 1925.[@3] New Zealand met that obligation by passing the Dangerous Drugs Act 1927, which set up a licensing scheme across a wide range of drugs and made it an offence to import, export, produce or deal in them except under a licence or other lawful authority.[@3]

[Assent came on 11 November 1927](/history/new-zealand-dangerous-drugs-1927/), but section 1 held the whole Act back from operating until the new year.[@1,2] The entry into force, not the assent, is what changed the position on the ground.[@1]

## The schedule defined the plant precisely

The schedule caught Indian hemp defined as the dried flowering or fruiting tops of the pistillate Cannabis sativa plant from which the resin had not been extracted.[@1,2] It added the resin itself, preparations based on that resin, and extracts and tinctures of Indian hemp.[@1,2]

Section 9 then closed the circle: no one could produce, manufacture, sell, distribute or otherwise deal in any dangerous drug except under a licence from the Director-General or some other lawful authority.[@1,2] Licences could carry conditions and fees, and anyone previously convicted under the Act needed ministerial approval before a new licence could issue.[@1,2]

## Licensed dealing stayed open

The ban was aimed at uncontrolled trade, not at every handling of the drug: licensed manufacturers and sellers, and supply through lawful medical channels, remained permitted.[@1,2] Enforcement tools came with the regime, including search warrants for premises and documents where an offence was suspected.[@1,2]

## The 1975 Act took over as the governing statute

The licensing regime lasted until drug law was recast in the [Misuse of Drugs Act 1975](/history/new-zealand-misuse-of-drugs-1975/), which the Law Commission describes as the statute regulating the recreational use of illegal drugs including cannabis.[@3] That Act placed cannabis in Class C, the lowest class, with a maximum of three months in prison or a $500 fine for possession.[@3]

The 1928 commencement thus opened nearly five decades of dangerous-drugs control in [New Zealand](/country/nz/), moving from a treaty-driven licensing system to the classification scheme that still shapes the country's cannabis penalties.
