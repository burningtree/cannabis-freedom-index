On 17 February 1908 Suriname's colonial government issued the opium ordinance (G.B. no. 13) that first banned growing cannabis in the colony — and gave the ganja import trade ten years to wind down, until 1 March 1918.[@1,2]

## The immigrants' intoxicant

The contemporary encyclopedia defines ganja as the resin-bound tops of the female Cannabis sativa plant, and calls it an intoxicant much used in Suriname by the British-Indian immigrants.[@1] A taxed import trade served them: 726 kg entered in 1907, then 432, 717, 791, 761, 718 and 689 kg through 1913, paying ƒ40 duty per kilo.[@1]

## Growing banned, imports licensed away

The 1908 ordinance prohibited cultivating both the opium poppy and Cannabis sativa outright, while regulating import and sale instead of banning them at once.[@2] Import was reserved to holders of the governor's permit to trade wholesale in opium, and sale to licensed wholesale and retail opium traders; a December 1908 decision granted those permits only for Paramaribo, Nieuw Nickerie and Albina.[@2] Nobody else was allowed to hold more than 0.05 kg of opium, and violations carried heavy penalties.[@2]

Pharmacists stood outside the system: those importing and dispensing opium purely for medical purposes were exempt, with the governor setting how much each could keep in stock.[@2] A wholesale trader paid ƒ1,000 a year for the privilege, a retailer ƒ120.[@2]

## 1 March 1918: the deadline arrives

From 1 March 1918, the ordinance said, nobody was to cultivate, import or possess either plant anymore — while the pharmacy rules for pharmacists stayed in force.[@2] [The import ban duly took effect on that date](/history/suriname-ganja-import-ban-1918/), and [the 1922 colonial report](/history/suriname-opium-ganja-convictions-1922/) still counted fourteen possession convictions for opium or ganja, with no cannabis-growing offences detected. Three years after the deadline, [immigrants petitioned to lift the ban](/history/suriname-ganja-petition-1921/) and asked for an import-and-sale concession.

## What came next

The 1908 system lasted for decades: [the 1955 Opium Act](/history/suriname-opium-order-1955/) kept four-year prison ceilings for deliberate cannabis offences, and [the 1998 Drugs Act](/history/suriname-narcotics-act-1998/) replaced it in turn. Suriname's current law is on [its profile](/country/sr/).
