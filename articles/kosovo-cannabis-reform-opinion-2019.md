Six months after Kosovo's new criminal code kept prison on the table for cannabis possession, a Pristina consultant argued the country would do better to legalise and regulate.[@1,2] Kushtrim Shaipi's October 2019 Kosovo 2.0 piece made the enforcement-first case against enforcement.[@1]

## Checkpoints and raids that yielded little

Shaipi's target was method, not intent. Kosovo Police tactics — special-forces presence at checkpoints, raids in bars and clubs — had yielded little, he wrote.[@1] Around one in ten of more than 5,500 Kosovars surveyed knew someone using cannabis: the market the raids were meant to close was ordinary and visible.[@1]

## Regulation instead of penalisation

His proposal was regulated legalisation, which he argued could serve Kosovo better than penalisation.[@1] The context was global: more countries were rethinking drug policy, with cannabis leading.[@1] And it was local: the April 2019 code had just retained prison exposure for possession, with only a lower tier — a fine or up to one year — for first-time offenders holding less than three grams.[@2]

## A debate that stayed a debate

The piece changed no law. Six years later possession is still criminal at every amount, the 2008 drug law still limits cannabis to research, education and police work, and the police still measure success in seizures — 25.2 kg and four arrests in Pristina in January 2025.[@3] Shaipi's opinion stands as the clearest domestic call for a regulated market, and a marker of how little the argument has moved [Kosovo's](/country/xk/) law since.
