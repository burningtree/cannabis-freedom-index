In June 1938 two policemen disagreed in Geneva about what cannabis does to people. One
ran the American narcotics bureau. The other had policed hashish in Egypt for a
generation, and he thought the American was talking nonsense.

## The setting

Cannabis had been under international control since the
[convention of 1925](/history/geneva-opium-convention-1925/), adopted after Egypt's
delegate told the League of Nations that hashish caused most of his country's
insanity. A decade later the controls were judged unsatisfactory, and in 1934 the
League set up a [sub-committee](/history/league-cannabis-subcommittee-1935/) of experts
to form a considered view.

## The American

The United States was not a member of the League, but Harry Anslinger attended its
opium committee as an observer and pressed for strict prohibition. In 1937, the year of
the [Marihuana Tax Act](/history/us-marihuana-tax-act-1937/), he reported violent crimes
"directly traceable" to cannabis. In 1938 he told the committee that marijuana
"maintains its ancient, worldwide tradition of murder, assault, rape, physical and
mental deterioration".

## The Egyptian policeman

Thomas Russell, known as Russell Pasha, had served in Egypt since 1902, as Cairo's chief
of police and as head of the country's narcotics bureau. He had an international
reputation for the drug war he had waged there, and he represented the Egyptian government.

He had already told the League, in
[1930](/history/egypt-russell-hashish-report-1930/), that hashish was the vice of the
city slums and did comparatively little harm. Now he said it again. His impression was
that hashish addiction "was not leading to any particular crime wave". The addict was
"a tiresome type of individual", not a dangerous criminal, and it would not be true to
say that much serious crime was due to it.

He answered Anslinger with sarcasm: hashish could be extremely dangerous, he allowed,
though in most cases its users lapsed into joviality.

## What came of it

Nothing. The sub-committee stopped work in 1939 when war broke out. After the war the
question passed to the United Nations, where the American view prevailed and cannabis
was classed with heroin in the
[Single Convention of 1961](/history/un-single-convention-1961/).

The exchange matters because of who was speaking. Egypt had put cannabis into
international law in 1925 on the strength of one delegate's speech. Thirteen years on,
the man actually responsible for enforcing that law in Egypt was telling the same body
that the premise was wrong.
