In 1988 the United States drug agency's own judge examined whether marijuana belonged
among the most dangerous drugs. He ruled that it did not. His agency overruled him.

## Sixteen years to a hearing

Marijuana was placed in Schedule I of the
[Controlled Substances Act](/history/us-controlled-substances-act-1970/) in 1970, the
category for drugs with no accepted medical use. In 1972 the reform group NORML
petitioned to have it moved to Schedule II, which would have allowed doctors to
prescribe it.

The agency refused even to consider the petition. It took three court orders, in 1974,
1977 and 1980, to make the government process it and carry out the scientific and
medical evaluation the law requires. Hearings finally opened in the summer of 1986 and
lasted about two years.

## The ruling

On 6 September 1988 the Drug Enforcement Administration's chief administrative law
judge, Francis L. Young, gave his decision. Marijuana, he wrote, was

> one of the safest therapeutically active substances known to man.

It did not meet the criteria for Schedule I, and the Act required that it be moved to
Schedule II.

## The answer

The ruling was a recommendation to the agency's administrator, John Lawn, who rejected
it. In 1994 an appeals court confirmed that the administrator had the power to overrule
his judge, and the petition, twenty-two years old, was dead.

## What followed

Later petitions, filed in 1995, 2002, 2009 and 2011, were all denied, the last of them
in 2016. Change came by another route. Federal health officials
[recommended Schedule III](/history/us-hhs-schedule-iii-2023/) in 2023, and in
[2026](/history/us-medical-schedule-iii-2026/) state-licensed medical cannabis was
moved there.

The index lists Judge Young's ruling for context. It changed no law, but it is the
clearest case of the evidence being heard, in the forum the law provided, and set
aside.
