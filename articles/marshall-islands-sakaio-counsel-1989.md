George Sakaio received two years in prison after pleading guilty to marijuana possession without a lawyer. On 18 October 1989, the Marshall Islands Supreme Court reversed his conviction, finding failures to protect his right to legal assistance and ensure he understood his plea. The case went back to the High Court for further proceedings.[@1]

## A guilty plea brought an immediate prison sentence

Sakaio had been charged under the [national narcotics law that commenced in 1987](/history/marshall-islands-narcotics-act-1987/). At his arraignment, when the charge was put to him, he had no lawyer. He pleaded guilty and the High Court sentenced him to two years.[@1,2]

He immediately asked to withdraw his plea. The court refused. Five days later, at another hearing concerning the marijuana, the Public Defender tried to represent him. The judge rejected that request too, reasoning that Sakaio had already been convicted and sentenced.[@1]

## Police and judges had duties to explain his rights

The Supreme Court treated access to a lawyer as fundamental to a fair criminal trial. Police had to advise an arrested person about legal assistance, including the availability of the Public Defender. Court officials also had duties to explain the right to counsel and allow an opportunity to obtain representation.[@1]

The appeal record did not show that Sakaio had received those explanations before pleading. There was no transcript of the arraignment and sentencing. The Supreme Court also stressed that making a proper record was a duty, rather than something the trial judge could choose to omit.[@1]

## A court had to check that the plea was understood

Accepting a guilty plea required more than hearing the word guilty. The trial court had to establish that the defendant acted voluntarily and understood the charge and the plea’s general consequences.[@1]

Giving up the right to a lawyer also had to be a knowing decision, shown in the record. Silence could not establish that Sakaio understood and waived that right. His immediate request to withdraw the plea and subsequent attempt to obtain a lawyer reinforced the problems with the conviction.[@1]

## The conviction was reversed and the case returned

The Supreme Court concluded that it could not regard Sakaio’s plea as properly informed. It reversed the judgment and returned the matter for further action consistent with its ruling, requiring the criminal process to respect those rights.[@1]

Later [drug-law amendments in 2020](/history/marshall-islands-drug-asset-seizure-2020/) expanded asset-seizure powers and republished the marijuana penalty ladder.[@3] The [Marshall Islands profile](/country/mh/) explains those penalties and the legal exceptions alongside the history of the country’s cannabis controls.[@2]
