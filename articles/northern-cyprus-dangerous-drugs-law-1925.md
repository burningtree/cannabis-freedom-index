Cyprus prohibited cannabis imports and exports under a drug law dated 22 December 1925.[@1] The law also gave the colonial governor power to control growing, possession and sale through regulations.[@1] These rules covered the island as a whole, including the territory now governed as Northern Cyprus.[@1]

## Cannabis crossed the border illegally

The Dangerous Drugs Law grouped Indian hemp with raw opium and coca leaves, but its border rule treated them differently.[@1] Opium and coca leaves could enter under a medical or scientific licence; the cannabis clause prohibited bringing Indian hemp into Cyprus or sending it out.[@1]

Indian hemp meant the dried flowering or fruiting tops of the female cannabis plant, together with its resin.[@1] A separate power extended to the whole plant, allowing regulations to restrict or prohibit its cultivation, production, possession, sale and distribution.[@1]

## Offences could bring a year in prison

An offence under the law could lead to up to one year in prison, a fine of up to £100, or both.[@1] The court could also confiscate the goods involved.[@1] Attempting an offence, or encouraging someone else to commit one, carried the same punishment.[@1]

## A later law covered smoking directly

The [1956 consolidation](/history/northern-cyprus-dangerous-drugs-law-1956/) retained controls on cannabis and its resin, while providing a licensing route for imports and exports through approved ports.[@2] It also authorised regulations over cultivation and distribution throughout the colony.[@2]

For cannabis prepared for smoking, the law set out direct offences covering manufacture, sale, possession and use.[@2] Someone who occupied premises could also commit an offence by permitting preparation, sale or smoking there.[@2] Pipes and other utensils used for smoking came within the same part of the law, extending its reach beyond the cannabis itself.[@2]

## Northern legislation retained the controls

The later [1972 northern drug law](/history/northern-cyprus-drug-law-1972/) continued to distinguish cannabis flowering tops from resin.[@3] It subjected imports to licensing and criminalised cannabis-smoking premises and cultivation.[@3] Its cultivation offence covered a Cannabis sativa plant without requiring proof that flowering or fruiting tops had developed.[@3]

That later law forms the basis of the offences described in the [Northern Cyprus profile](/country/xn/).[@1,2,3] Reading the original 1925 text alongside its successors shows how border controls and powers to regulate the plant developed into explicit offences affecting growers, users and the people providing places to smoke.[@1,2,3]
