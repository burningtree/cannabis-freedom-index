From 13 June 1949, drugs confiscated by Iraqi courts had to go to the health authorities, which could order their destruction or their use for medicine and science in government health institutions.[@1] Cannabis fell within the drug law to which the regulation applied.[@2] The rule placed the handling of confiscated stocks under a named public authority.[@1]

## Cannabis was already inside the drug-control system

The [1938 drug law](/history/iraq-dangerous-drugs-law-1938/) covered Indian hemp, resin and preparations made from them.[@2] It restricted possession to authorised professionals and people obtaining drugs through lawful prescription or pharmacy channels.[@2]

Health officials also controlled manufacturing licences and import and export permits.[@2] Pharmacists and dealers had to enter receipts and supplies in official registers within twenty-four hours, and retain controlled-drug prescriptions for at least three years.[@2] The law therefore followed drugs through authorised dealings as well as prohibiting unauthorised possession.[@2]

## A court order sent the drugs to the health directorate

The 1949 regulation concerned drugs that a court had ordered confiscated under that existing law.[@1] They had to be delivered to the Directorate General of Health.[@1] The directorate then decided between destruction and medical or scientific use within government health institutions.[@1]

Regulation 36 was proposed by the minister of social affairs and approved by the Council of Ministers.[@1] Although made on 22 May, it took effect on publication in the official Gazette on 13 June.[@1]

## Doctors, police and customs supervised destruction

Destruction required oversight by a committee with two doctors representing the health directorate, one police representative and one from customs and excise.[@1] The committee had to send its report and certificate to the Ministry of Social Affairs.[@1]

For a confiscated stock, the regulation thus specified both who received it and who certified its destruction.[@1] Medical and scientific reuse went through government institutions under the health directorate's decision.[@1]

## Confiscation continued alongside criminal penalties

The [1955 amendment](/history/iraq-drug-penalties-1955/) paired a replacement fine-or-prison penalty with confiscation of materials seized in the offender's actual use.[@3] A decade later, the [1965 narcotics law](/history/iraq-narcotics-law-1965/) replaced the 1938 law and again required illegally held drugs to be confiscated.[@4]

The replacement preserved existing regulations and instructions until new ones took their place.[@4] That continuity connected the earlier administrative machinery with the later criminal law in [Iraq](/country/iq/).[@4]
