Trinidad’s cannabis controls moved beyond cultivation licensing in 1894. Jamie Banks’s archival research identifies Ordinance No. 22 of that year as a prohibition of ganja sale, cultivation and possession. A later parliamentary account also describes a medical exception. Together they establish a missing stage between the colony’s 1885 licensing measure and its 1899 revision.

## From licensing to prohibition

The [1885 ordinance](/history/trinidad-cultivation-licence-1885/) imposed heavy licence duties on cultivation. Banks explains that its effect was intended to be restrictive, even though a licensing requirement differs legally from an outright prohibition. Contemporary observers sometimes confused these positions when describing Trinidad to officials elsewhere in the empire.

The 1894 instrument represents a separate development. Banks cites it in the Colonial Office records as Ordinance No. 22, concerning prohibition of sale, cultivation and possession of ganja, in file CO 295/356. This archival reference supports recording the later measure independently rather than attaching all nineteenth-century restrictions to 1885.

## The medical exception

During the House of Representatives debate on 11 December 2019, Attorney General Faris Al-Rawi described the 1894 legislation as prohibiting cultivation, sale and keeping of ganja while allowing licensed medical practitioners to keep and sell it. His account appears on pages 23–24 of the unrevised parliamentary record.

That is an official retrospective description, rather than the original statute. It supports mentioning the reported exception, but cannot establish its exact wording, the licensing procedure or the penalties for breaches. Those details remain open until an original or reliable consolidated text is read.

## Colonial concerns behind the measure

Banks relates regulation to medical officials’ claims about cannabis and insanity among Indian immigrants. He also identifies practical concerns: domestic cultivation, particularly in Cedros, and supplies arriving from nearby Venezuela. The law emerged from this combination of labour administration, medical campaigning and concern over cross-border trade.

These explanations concern the political setting of prohibition. They do not establish that cannabis caused the illnesses attributed to it by colonial officials, or that the new law successfully eliminated supply.

The event uses year precision because enactment and commencement dates were not verified from the instrument. Its [1899 revision](/history/trinidad-repeal-1899/) also needs careful treatment: a report of repeal does not by itself establish that every remaining cannabis control disappeared.
