[Kumina](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kumina) is a religion that took shape in Jamaica in the nineteenth century, drawing above all on the religion of the Kongo peoples of Central Africa. Its ceremonies are for healing: drumming, singing and dancing call down ancestral spirits, who possess the dancers. Cannabis was smoked to invite that possession. Kumina is one of the three routes by which scholars suggest the plant entered the [Rastafari](/culture/traditions/rastafari-sacrament/) sacrament.

## Who brought it

Kumina came with people from the Kongo region, brought to Jamaica in the years after the abolition of slavery as indentured labourers. They settled above all in the parish of St Thomas, in the east of the island, and from there the practice spread to Portland, St Mary, St Catherine and Kingston. In the second half of the nineteenth century it blended with Myalism, an older Afro-Jamaican religion.

Its communities are small and based on family, organised in "nations" named after African peoples: Mondongo, Moyenge, Machunde, Kongo, Igbo, Yoruba. Members of Kumina families are called Bongo. One joins by marrying into a Bongo family or by initiation. Each nation is led by a King and a Queen, and the Queens are the keepers of the religion's knowledge, respected, in one description, as "psychologists, doctors, priests, and judges". Imogene "Queenie" Kennedy, who died in 1998, was the best known of the twentieth century.

## The ceremony

| Element | |
|---|---|
| Purpose | Mainly healing: the spirits are summoned to cure the sick |
| Means | Singing, dancing, drumming, animal sacrifice, spirit possession |
| Cannabis | Smoked to help bring on possession by the ancestors |
| Authority | Not the Bible; this sets Kumina apart from the Revival churches |

Outsiders also called it Pukkumina or Pocomania, a term now regarded as largely pejorative.

## Ganja, Kumina and Rastafari

Cannabis reached Jamaica in the [1850s](/history/jamaica-ganja-arrives-1855/), imported from Bengal for Indian indentured workers, and with it the Indian word [ganja](/culture/language/ganja/). Kumina also took ideas from the indentured labourers of the years after emancipation.

When Rastafari arose in the 1930s, it made ganja its sacrament. Scholars offer three possible sources: Hindu ritual, through the Indian labourers; the general use of cannabis among poor Afro-Jamaicans; and Kumina, in which smoking was already a religious act. They are not exclusive.

The connection is clearer in music. The Kumina drumming style, developed from the music of the ceremonies, shaped the Nyabinghi drumming of the Rastafari, and through the drummer Count Ossie, who played at Rasta gatherings, it passed into ska, reggae and dancehall.

## The law

Kumina's ganja was never protected. Jamaica [banned the plant in 1913](/history/jamaica-ganja-law-1913/), under pressure from the churches. The [reform of 2015](/history/jamaica-ganja-reform-2015/) protects sacramental use by Rastafari.
