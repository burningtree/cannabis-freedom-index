*Siemieniotka*, also called *konopiotka* or *siemieniatka*, is a traditional hemp-seed soup from Silesia, Poland, served at the Christmas Eve supper, *Wigilia*. It carries on the centuries-old culinary use of hemp as food rather than as an intoxicant, from a time when hemp was an ordinary crop.

[Śląskie Smaki](https://slaskiesmaki.pl/staticcontent/141243/siemieniatka-siemieniotka-konopiotka), the region’s culinary tourism project, records descriptions of the soup in Silesian accounts from 1887 and 1888. The dish is still served at Christmas Eve suppers, with recipes passed from one generation to the next.

Sula Benet’s 1975 survey adds a different kind of evidence. She records hemp-seed Christmas Eve soup, spelling it *semieniatka*, in Poland and Lithuania and formerly Russia, and interprets the meal in relation to beliefs about deceased relatives visiting their families. This is her comparative folklore account, rather than proof that every contemporary Silesian household gives the soup the same meaning.

## The dish

The name comes from *siemię*, seed. The seeds are boiled, rubbed free of their shells, and ground to a paste. The paste is thinned with milk and sweetened with honey.

Hemp seed is a food in its own right. Hulled, it is about half fat and a third protein, and it can be eaten raw, ground into meal or pressed for oil.

## The occasion

[Wigilia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wigilia) is the Polish Christmas Eve supper. It excludes meat, and by custom it has twelve dishes, for the twelve Apostles. It opens with a soup, which in most of Poland is beetroot borscht or a clear mushroom broth. In Silesia the soup may be siemieniotka.

| Element of Wigilia | What it is |
|---|---|
| The soup | Borscht with small dumplings, mushroom broth, or in Silesia siemieniotka |
| Fish | Herring, and carp in several forms: the main dish everywhere |
| Sides | Cabbage, sauerkraut |
| Twelve dishes | One for each Apostle |

## A crop that stopped being ordinary

Hemp was for centuries a common field crop across Europe, grown for rope, cloth, oil and seed. The first drug laws did not touch it. Poland's [Act of 1923](/history/poland-narcotics-act-1923/) named hashish beside opium, morphine and cocaine, and did not mention the plant.

That changed by stages.

| Year | Polish law |
|---|---|
| [1923](/history/poland-narcotics-act-1923/) | Hashish is controlled; the plant is not named |
| [1951](/history/poland-narcotic-1951/) | Cannabis herb is listed as a narcotic |
| [1985](/history/poland-drug-prevention-act-1985/) | A drug act that does not punish possession |
| [1997](/history/poland-drug-law-1997/), 2000 | Possession becomes a crime |
| [2022](/history/poland-hemp-act-2022/) | State research institutes may grow cannabis for medicine |

These laws were written about the herb and the resin. The soup is made from the seed.

## Relatives

Dishes of the same family exist elsewhere on the old hemp-growing map of Europe and Asia.

| Dish | Where | What it is |
|---|---|---|
| Siemieniotka | Silesia, Poland | Hemp-seed soup with milk and honey |
| *Bhang ko achar* | Nepal | A pickle of ground hemp seed with potato |
| Bhang chutney | Kumaon, India | Seeds ground with mint, tomato and spices |
| [Shichimi](/culture/traditions/hemp-seed-in-shichimi/) | Japan | A seven-spice mixture that includes hemp seed |

They have in common that they use the seed, and that they belong to regions where hemp was a household crop.

Latvian [hemp-seed butter](/culture/traditions/latvian-hemp-seed-butter/) offers another Baltic food tradition in which cannabis seeds are prepared for nourishment.
