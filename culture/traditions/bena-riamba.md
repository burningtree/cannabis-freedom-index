By the 1880s a people of the Kasai, in the south of what is now the Democratic Republic of the Congo, reorganised their common life around cannabis. They called themselves *Bena Riamba*, the sons of hemp. The pipe took the place of older rites, and smoking together became the sign of belonging and of peace.

## The only witnesses

What is known comes from outsiders. The German explorers [Paul Pogge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paul_Pogge) and [Hermann Wissmann](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hermann_von_Wissmann) reached the Bashilenge in 1881, and their accounts, written on the eve of colonial rule, are the main source. The pipe-maker Alfred Dunhill, writing in 1924, added that by 1884 a king in the region had set up a "riamba" cult "in place of fetish-worship". No account by a member survives in the sources used here, and everything below should be read with that in mind.

## How it began

Cannabis was new to the region. It may have been brought to the Congo basin only in the 1850s, by Swahili traders from Zanzibar; the word *riamba* is of the same family as the Angolan [diamba](/culture/language/diamba/).

According to the explorers, it began with small clubs of smokers bound to one another by friendship. The clubs grew into a movement and the movement into something like a religion. The Bashilenge took a new name for themselves, Bena Riamba, and a new name for their country: *Lubuku*, friendship. They greeted one another with the word *moio*, which meant both hemp and life.

## What was done

| Occasion | The place of hemp |
|---|---|
| Joining | New members were initiated with the pipe, usually before a war or a long journey |
| Daily life | Each man was expected to take part and to show his devotion by smoking often |
| Travel and war | Taken before setting out; it was held to protect against every kind of harm |
| Trade and peace | No holiday, no trade agreement and no peace treaty was concluded without it |

The pipe was a great gourd. Pogge and Wissmann found large plots of hemp planted round the villages to supply it.

The explorers reported that the Bena Riamba attributed universal powers to the plant. Later writers compared the pipe to the peace pipe of North America, and it seems to have worked in a similar way: to smoke with someone was to be at peace with him.

## The law that followed

The Bashilenge country was absorbed into King Leopold's Congo Free State within a few years of the explorers' visit. The new state took a different view. An order of 1899 dealt with the destruction of confiscated hemp, and in 1903 an [ordinance "against the use of smoking hemp"](/history/congo-free-state-hemp-ordinance-1903/) prohibited growing, selling, carrying, possessing and using it. Offenders faced a fine of 100 to 1,000 francs and from fifteen days to a year of penal servitude. Village chiefs were made jointly liable for the fines.

Nothing in the sources used here says how the movement ended.

## Now

The Democratic Republic of the Congo still prohibits cannabis for anything but medicine, industry and science, which a directive of 2021 allows under licence. In short histories of cannabis in the country the movement survives as a single line: in the 1880s the "People of Cannabis" popularised its ritual use in the south-west.
