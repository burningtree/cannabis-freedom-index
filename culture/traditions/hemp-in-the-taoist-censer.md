China's early classics mention hemp constantly, as cloth, rope and food, and say nothing about its power to intoxicate. There is one body of writing that breaks the silence. From about the fourth century, Taoist texts speak of burning cannabis as incense and of eating it in order to see spirits. They are the main evidence that the plant had a ritual use in China at all.

## The silence

Hemp, [*má*](/culture/language/ma/), has been grown in China since the Neolithic. The *Book of Odes* has it woven into clothing. The classics do not mention what the flowers do.

The word itself knows. The same character means numb, and is the root of the Chinese words for anaesthetic and narcotic. The botanist Li Hui-lin argued that the plant's stupefying effect was known "from extremely early times", and that shamans of north-east Asia had passed its medical and spiritual uses to the *wu*, the spirit-mediums of ancient China, whose calling declined after the Han dynasty.

Some researchers explain the silence of the classics by that association with shamanism. After the rise of Confucianism, the ritual taking of cannabis is thought to have been suppressed.

## What the Taoists wrote

| Date | Text | What it says |
|---|---|---|
| 4th century | Texts attributed to Ge Hong | Those beginning the Way need not go into the mountains; "with purifying incense and sprinkling and sweeping" some can call down the immortals |
| About 500 | Tao Hongjing, *Mingyi bielu* | Hemp seeds "are very little used in medicine, but the magician-technicians say that if one consumes them with ginseng it will give one preternatural knowledge of events in the future" |
| 6th century | *Wuzangjing*, a Taoist medical work | "If you wish to command demonic apparitions to present themselves you should constantly eat the inflorescences of the hemp plant" |
| About 570 | *Wushang Biyao*, a Taoist encyclopaedia | Cannabis is added to ritual incense-burners |
| About 730 | *Shiliao bencao*, on diet | "Those who wish to see demons should take it … for up to a hundred days" |
| About 1100 | *Zhenglei bencao* | "If taken in excess it produces hallucinations and a staggering gait. If taken over a long term, it causes one to communicate with spirits and lightens one's body" |

The founders of the Shangqing school of Taoism, Wei Huacun and Xu Mi, are among those said to have called down the immortals with incense. Its scriptures were dictated to the medium Yang Xi in nightly visions in the fourth century, "aided almost certainly by cannabis" in the judgment of the historian of science [Joseph Needham](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joseph_Needham).

Needham's general conclusion was that the hallucinogenic properties of hemp "were common knowledge in Chinese medical and Taoist circles for two millennia or more", and that the early Taoists experimented systematically with "hallucinogenic smokes".

## What the ground adds

Archaeology has confirmed the practice from outside the texts, at China's western edge. Wooden braziers from the [Jirzankal cemetery in the Pamirs](/history/china-jirzankal-study-2019/), about 2,500 years old, held the burnt residue of cannabis rich in THC. In the Turpan basin, graves of the same age contained people thought to be shamans, buried with sacks of cannabis by their heads, and one man laid under thirteen whole plants.

## The law

The pharmacopoeias kept the plant: hemp seed has never been dropped from the official Chinese list of medicines. The modern state [classed cannabis as a narcotic in 1985](/history/china-narcotic-1985/), and use is punished with detention of ten to fifteen days and a fine under the [public security law](/history/china-public-security-law-2006/).

Growing hemp for fibre was never prohibited.
