*Kanapinis*, the hemp man, is a character in Lithuania's *Užgavėnės*, the carnival held before Lent. Lean Kanapinis confronts *Lašininis*, the fat or pork man, in a staged struggle. The hemp man normally wins. The contest gives human form to the departure of winter, the arrival of spring and the change from rich food to the leaner meals of the fasting season.

This is a cannabis tradition expressed through a folk character. It does not document ceremonial smoking or require an intoxicating preparation. Hemp belongs to the story as a familiar crop and food, which is precisely what gives the character's name its force.

## Two bodies, two seasons

Accounts of [Užgavėnės](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/U%C5%BEgav%C4%97n%C4%97s) identify Lašininis with winter and Kanapinis with spring. Their contrasting bodies also make the contrast in diet visible. The fat man's defeat comes at the point when the pre-Lenten feast gives way to abstinence.

J. Kudirka's description, published by the [Lithuanian Folk Culture Centre](https://www.lnkc.lt/eknygos/thelt/node18.html), connects the lean character directly to hemp seed. Children were told that Kanapinis had carried away the hanging stores of pork, leaving the household to eat lean food flavoured with hemp seed or herring until Easter. The crop therefore represents more than a convenient name for a costume: it has a place in the seasonal food economy behind the drama.

## Noise, masks and pancakes

The carnival traditionally falls on the Tuesday before Ash Wednesday. Celebrations can also be arranged on a nearby weekend. Participants wear masks, make noise, sing, dance, play jokes and eat pancakes. A winter effigy, usually called *Morė*, is burnt as part of sending the season away.

Kudirka describes the custom as particularly strong in Žemaitija, or Samogitia. It also includes animals, devils and death among its masked characters. The surviving descriptions contain caricatures of Jewish and Roma people; those reflect historical stereotypes and should not be treated as neutral portraits of the communities depicted.

## A custom carried into town

The [Klaipėda Ethnocultural Centre](https://www.etnocentras.lt/shrove-tuesday/) explains that this masquerading tradition was not originally characteristic of its own region, but is now central to the city's celebrations. Its account describes improvisation, songs and pranks, with Lašininis and Kanapinis also organising games and relay races. Communities adapt the form while retaining its recognisable figures.

The [Kaukynės festival](https://kaukynes.mjr.lt/en/) likewise advertises the pair's contest as part of a modern celebration combining traditional masks and music. These accounts show a living, organised custom, rather than evidence that every detail has remained unchanged since an identifiable ancient date. The sources used here do not establish a date when the hemp man first appeared.

## The crop and its modern regulation

In [Lithuania](/country/lt/), hemp's place in folk culture sits beside a separate history of regulating the plant. The country [lifted its industrial-hemp cultivation ban in 2013](/history/lithuania-hemp-2013/) and [expanded permitted hemp products in 2021](/history/lithuania-hemp-products-2021/). Those changes concern cultivation and products, not permission to portray a carnival character.

Kanapinis belongs beside food customs such as Silesian [siemieniotka](/culture/traditions/siemieniotka/) in the atlas. Both preserve the memory of cannabis as nourishment. The carnival continues through community performances, and the hemp man's victory remains legible even to spectators who have never grown the crop.

Latvian [hemp-seed butter](/culture/traditions/latvian-hemp-seed-butter/) offers another Baltic food tradition in which cannabis seeds are prepared for nourishment.
