*Freak Street* is the travellers' name for Jhochhen in Kathmandu. During the 1960s and early 1970s, its lodges, cafés and licensed cannabis shops became a destination on the overland hippie trail. People came to buy hashish, stay cheaply and meet other travellers. Nepali shopkeepers and café owners built businesses around that demand.

The nickname can make the neighbourhood sound like a foreign enclave invented from nothing. Jhochhen was also a place where residents worked, served food and adapted to a changing tourist economy. Its cannabis market ended as a lawful business, but its social life did not end with it.

## A shop and a meeting place

Devi Dutta Sharma's Eden Hashish Centre became one of the district's best-known businesses. The [Kathmandu Post's history of Eden](https://kathmandupost.com/miscellaneous/2018/09/29/the-garden-of-eden) describes a showroom, spaces for customers to smoke and rest, and a business aimed at foreigners. Lodging and cannabis sales occupied parts of the same commercial world.

Its marketing also travelled. English-language signs, business cards and calendars presented Kathmandu as an exotic destination and Eden as an established, trustworthy seller. Religious imagery helped sell that imagined experience. The surviving posters later became collectors' objects. That afterlife belongs to the history of advertising as much as to the history of cannabis.

Legal retail did not make every part of the business lawful. The same account distinguishes licensed domestic sales from the export of hashish, which was prohibited. Romantic accounts of an unrestricted market lose that distinction.

## The café around the corner

[Snowman Café](https://kathmandupost.com/miscellaneous/2014/12/12/still-around), founded by Ram Prasad Manandhar in 1965, offers another view of the district. Its founder recalled customers smoking charas inside and outside while the surrounding streets filled with travellers. Food, conversation and hanging about were part of the neighbourhood's identity alongside buying drugs.

Those recollections are accounts of a particular era. They do not describe a custom followed by everyone in Kathmandu, nor do they establish that Nepali cannabis traditions began with Western visitors. [Shivaratri at Pashupatinath](/culture/traditions/shivaratri-at-pashupatinath/) belongs to a different religious setting in the same city.

## Licences cancelled, then a wider ban

Nepal [revoked cannabis cultivation and trading licences on July 16, 1973](/history/nepal-shops-closed-1973/). The [1976 Narcotic Drugs (Control) Act](/history/nepal-narcotics-act-1976/) followed with a broader prohibition. Closing the licensed market and criminalising personal conduct were separate steps.

That sequence distinguishes Freak Street from Copenhagen's [Pusher Street](/culture/traditions/pusher-street/). Both became internationally recognised cannabis places, but Kathmandu's shops had operated under licences; Christiania's stalls operated despite prohibition. Visibility alone does not tell us the legal position of a market.

## After the travellers moved on

In its [2019 return to Snowman](https://kathmandupost.com/21/2019/11/21/snowman-cafe-the-final-bastion-of-freak-street), the Kathmandu Post found a business increasingly serving local customers, with the founder's son working in a café that had become principally a cake shop. The contrast with the remembered crowds of foreign travellers was part of the story.

That reporting supports an account of adaptation, rather than a claim that the district remains a legal cannabis destination today. The name Freak Street survives as a reminder of a particular encounter between tourism, local enterprise and a market that [Nepal](/country/np/) closed.
