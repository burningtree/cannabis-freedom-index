The Heddawa were followers of Sidi Heddi, a Moroccan holy man whose brotherhood incorporated *kif* into religious devotion. Historical accounts describe wandering, alms-seeking, voluntary poverty and cannabis smoking alongside chanting and rhythmic movement. This was a particular community, distinct from both ordinary café smoking and religious brotherhoods that condemned cannabis.

In *The African Roots of Marijuana*, Chris S. Duvall connects its history with cannabis cultivation in northern Morocco. His account also shows why stories about sacred cannabis need careful reading: saints' legends, hostile descriptions and observations of actual practice became entangled in the surviving literature.

## Sidi Heddi and the stories told about him

Duvall places the formation of the order near the end of the eighteenth century and dates Sidi Heddi's death approximately to 1805. Accounts of the holy man's earlier life and conversion to kif are partly legendary.

One story has him rebuke another marabout for smoking, then agree to try the substance himself. Successive puffs bring confusion and finally spiritual understanding, after which he establishes his own order. This is a devotional origin story, not evidence that cannabis reliably produces revelation.

The distinction leaves room for the historical person without treating every miracle or anecdote as biography. His tomb became a focus of pilgrimage, and later followers remembered him as a patron of kif smokers.

## Devotion outside respectable society

The brotherhood embraced practices that drew criticism from religious and secular authorities. Duvall describes followers accepting poverty and celibacy while using cannabis in morning and evening devotions. Chanting and movement were part of the attempt to attain a trance-like state.

Observers disagreed about the people they encountered. Some portrayed the brethren as beggars, idle dependants or threatening outsiders; others described shepherds and people capable of enduring severe hunger. Hostile language reveals the writer's social judgments as well as the circumstances being described.

Those accounts do not make cannabis a defining practice of Moroccan Islam as a whole. The Heddawa occupied a contested place within it. Roger Joseph's later observations of disapproving religious brotherhoods in Rif villages, discussed in [Kif and the sebsi](/culture/traditions/kif-and-the-sebsi/), describe a different setting rather than refuting the existence of this order.

## A religious demand for a farm crop

The Heddawa preferred kif from Ketama. Duvall describes their relationship with growers through accounts of cultivation and donations to the monastery. Cannabis could be a gift to a religious institution as well as a commodity sold in town.

That relationship helps explain the crop's regional value. Religious acceptance among some marabouts gave farmers one constituency for their harvest, while secular consumption expanded more widely during the nineteenth century. Duvall does not reduce every Rif farmer or smoker to membership in the brotherhood.

The material was also different from the export resin that later made Morocco internationally famous. [Kif](/culture/language/kif/) commonly meant herbal cannabis prepared for smoking. Treating all older references as accounts of modern pressed hashish erases changes in preparation and trade.

## The photograph and the later record

The photograph here belongs to a gallery explicitly attributed to Ramón Touceda Fontenla's 1955 study of the Heddawa. It preserves a smoking room, a long pipe and the publication's original caption. The date of publication is established by that attribution; the moment when the photograph was taken is not.

Its caption's interpretation of the seated man's condition belongs to the original writer. A still photograph cannot independently establish his mental state. The image documents a representation of the brotherhood and its material setting, rather than an independently observed sequence of intoxication.

Duvall follows the Heddawa into twentieth-century accounts while tracing the Rif's growing dependence on cannabis markets. His historical evidence does not establish the brotherhood's present membership or the survival of every described practice.
