Anyone who has watched people share a strong pipe knows the cough. In much of nineteenth-century
Africa it was more than a side effect. Smokers coughed loudly, together and at length, and several
observers suspected they were doing it on purpose. The geographer Chris Duvall, who gathered the
accounts, notes that coughing is "amusingly prominent" in the historical record, and that it was
probably sometimes a performance.

The record is almost entirely written by outsiders, many of them irritated, some amused. Read
together, their complaints describe a social habit: a cough that announced, to anyone in earshot,
that a group was smoking.

## What the visitors heard

| Year | Place | Observer, as quoted |
|---|---|---|
| 1798 | Delagoa Bay, [Mozambique](/country/mz/) | The horn pipe "makes them cough exceedingly, and they do it in a very affected manner, but they enjoy it vastly" (William White) |
| 1850s | Unyamwezi, [Tanzania](/country/tz/) | The noise is "probably not wholly natural", a way of "renowning it" (Richard Burton) |
| 1870s | Angola | Smokers cough "as if their lungs were coming out of their mouths" (J. J. Monteiro) |
| 1874 | Lower Congo | "Mostly the cough is an affectation" (Burton, who had smoked it himself) |
| 1880s | Congo Basin | The more the white man laughs, "the more the black's cough grows" (a Belgian) |

William White, an officer of the ship *Lion*, gave the earliest of these in his journal of 1798.
At [Delagoa Bay](/history/mozambique-delagoa-bay-bang-1798/) the men sat in circles round a fire,
smoking tobacco and bang through a four-foot bamboo set in a cow's horn of water, and drew the smoke
through a gap between the horn and the crook of the arm. The engraving in his book shows exactly
that.

## Renowning it

Richard Burton, travelling through what is now [Tanzania](/country/tz/) in the 1850s, gave the
fullest explanation. The "grotesque sounds", he thought, were "a fashion of 'renowning it'": an
announcement to the public that "the fast youths are smoking bhang". Twenty years later, on the
lower Congo in today's [DR Congo](/country/cd/), he went further and said that, having used it
himself "for months together", he had concluded that the cough was mostly put on.

French visitors to the Nyamwezi complained of "savage explosions of the voice" that could last all
night, and others in Zambia that "the hemp smokers keep us awake an hour or two". A Belgian found an
entire group squatting and coughing "an extremely comic spectacle", and noticed that the cough grew
louder the more he laughed.

## A cough worth hearing

Some of the evidence comes from the smokers' side. Duvall notes that Nyamwezi speakers had specific
words for cannabis coughing. An oral history recorded at Lake Malawi, in today's
[Malawi](/country/mw/), before 1922, tells that people discovered an island was inhabited by
"hearing the cough of a man who was smoking hemp". In the late 1800s Zulu smokers in
[South Africa](/country/za/) sang praise songs between coughs. A century earlier the traveller Anders
Sparrman concluded that for San smokers the fits of coughing were probably "a very desirable consequence" of
the pipe.

In [Angola](/country/ao/) the same period produced the sharpest complaint quoted above, and further
inland, in today's DR Congo, the [Bena Riamba](/culture/traditions/bena-riamba/) made cannabis smoking the centre of a whole
movement.

## After the bans

The habit described here belonged to open, shared smoking. Colonial laws, such as
[South Africa's national ban](/history/south-africa-national-ban-1922/) of 1922 and Tanganyika's
[Dangerous Drugs Ordinance](/history/tanganyika-dangerous-drugs-ordinance-1935/) of 1935, made a
loud public cough a risk rather than a boast. The accounts collected by Duvall stop with the
colonial period; none of the sources read for this entry describes the custom today. The sound
itself did survive in one place, as Duvall points out in a footnote: it opens Black Sabbath's 1971
song "Sweet Leaf".
