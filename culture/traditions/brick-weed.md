*Brick weed* is cannabis plant material compressed into dense blocks. The English name describes its shape. In [Brazil](/country/br/) it is commonly called *prensado* or *maconha prensada*; Spanish accounts use *marihuana prensada* and *ladrillo*, meaning brick. These names describe a processed form, rather than a botanical variety or strain.

Its cultural importance lies in the market that made the block familiar. Many accounts of cannabis celebrate distinctive flowers and named varieties. Brick weed instead records the experience of receiving an anonymous, compacted product through an illicit supply chain, with limited knowledge of its cultivation or handling.

## A block of plant material

Compression makes a bulky crop easier to handle and transport. The [DEA Museum](https://museum.dea.gov/exhibits/online-exhibits/cannabis-coca-and-poppy-natures-addictive-plants/cannabis) documents cannabis bricks as a form used in bulk trafficking across borders and within the United States. Its exhibit identifies the physical object, although its wider account is written from a law-enforcement perspective.

The block should not be confused with [hashish](/culture/language/hashish/). Hashish is made from collected resin; brick weed retains plant material. Both can arrive as rectangular blocks, but their appearance in a package does not make them the same preparation. Nor does pressing establish a particular THC concentration.

## Paraguay and neighbouring markets

[Guillermo Garat's TNI research](https://www.tni.org/files/publication-downloads/drug_policy_briefing_46.pdf) describes [Paraguay](/country/py/)'s characteristic bricked cannabis entering markets in Brazil, Argentina, Chile and Uruguay. It places production within a rural economy in which young growers and labourers work through intermediaries who connect them to buyers.

The report follows cultivation, harvesting, drying, pressing and packaging as parts of one supply chain. It also describes unequal returns: the existence of a valuable illicit export does not mean the people growing it receive most of the money. The brick can conceal that labour history as effectively as it conceals the appearance of the original flowers.

The [development of cultivation around Amambay](/history/paraguay-amambay-cannabis-1969/) belongs to that regional history. Paraguay's [personal-possession exception](/history/paraguay-ten-grams-1988/) was a separate legal development, not permission for the cross-border trade.

## Quality and what the evidence can show

A [forensic study by Macedo, Kosmann and Pujol-Luz](https://www.scielo.br/j/rbent/a/sPFt7YTnRc5KHhpSLnz7dHc/?lang=en) examined seven kilograms of pressed cannabis from two seizures in central Brazil. The researchers found insect fragments within the compacted material and used their species distributions to investigate possible geographical origins.

That evidence documents particular seized samples. It does not establish that every brick contains the same contaminants or comes from Paraguay. A photograph likewise cannot determine potency, additives or microbiological quality. The term's association with low quality should be distinguished from a laboratory finding about a specific batch.

## A point of comparison for growers

[Research by Marco Castro and Paulo Fraga](https://lifescienceglobal.com/pms/index.php/ijcs/article/view/9102) examines Brazilian home-growers through observation and interviews, documenting how they explain their practices and organise mutual assistance under prohibition. That social world offers a different relationship to the plant from anonymous purchased material: cultivation becomes something participants know, discuss and defend.

## Possession and supply under the law

In the [United States](/country/us/), the [1970 Controlled Substances Act](/history/us-controlled-substances-act-1970/) placed cannabis within federal drug control. Compressing plant material into a brick did not change its identity under that law. The museum's transport account concerns an illicit market, not a separate legal category for a pressed product.

Brazil's [2006 removal of prison penalties for users](/history/brazil-no-prison-for-users-2006/) did not establish a regulated retail supply. Its [2024 Supreme Court ruling](/history/brazil-supreme-court-2024/) subsequently made personal cannabis possession non-criminal while retaining prohibition and seizure of the material. Distinguishing a consumer from a trafficker is therefore a separate question from recognising the physical form of the cannabis.
