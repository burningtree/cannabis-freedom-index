Ruigoord is an artists' village in the Port of Amsterdam, where a former church and surviving houses became places for studios, poetry, music and festivals. Cannabis belongs to its countercultural history through documented gatherings and community connections. It is also part of a wider European network of *vrijplaatsen*: spaces organised around artistic and social freedom.

Its closest comparison in this atlas is Copenhagen's [Christiania and Pusher Street](/culture/traditions/pusher-street/). The connection is more than a resemblance between two hippie settlements. Christiania's cultural association calls Ruigoord its sister community and has sent delegates to its gatherings.

## A village saved from the harbour

The regional heritage project Oneindig Noord-Holland traces Ruigoord from an island settlement to a village on reclaimed land. Harbour expansion threatened its buildings after the municipality bought out residents. Artists arrived in the early 1970s and [prevented demolition in 1973](/history/netherlands-ruigoord-saved-1973/).

Preserving the village did not stop the port developing around it. Industrial facilities and shipping infrastructure became its immediate neighbours. A later settlement preserved space for artistic work; that arrangement should not be confused with recognition as an independent town. The Christiania delegation describes the remaining houses as studios, with residence not formally permitted.

## The church as a meeting place

Ruigoord's own programme describes recurring gatherings rather than a single annual cannabis event. *Landjuweel* combines several days of art and performance. *Vurige Tongen* brings together poetry, spoken word and music; *Openbare Werken* opens artists' studios to visitors. Dance events and other performances give the former church a continuing communal role.

This matters to its cannabis history: the plant was encountered within an established cultural environment, with its own venues and recurring audiences. A freetown is sustained by organising space, making art and negotiating its survival as well as by what participants consume there.

## A cannabis competition and its boundaries

A firsthand report in *Soft Secrets* documents the [Highlife Cup at Ruigoord on 24 June 2011](/history/netherlands-ruigoord-highlife-cup-2011/). It describes cannabis awards, exhibitors and festivities at the church, and identifies this as the third time Ruigoord hosted the competition. Highlife is a separate competition from the [High Times Cannabis Cup](/culture/traditions/the-cannabis-cup/).

The published rules for Landjuweel 2026 explicitly tolerate soft drugs for personal use up to five grams per person, prohibit trading and exclude pre-rolled joints. They also prohibit indoor smoking. These are the organiser's rules for that festival. They neither create a statutory exemption nor establish a permanent village-wide permission to sell cannabis. Dutch [tolerance policy](/history/netherlands-toleration-1976/) remains a separate legal history.

## Connections between free spaces

Christiania's account of Ruigoord's fiftieth anniversary describes a Free Cultural Spaces symposium, discussion of threatened cultural sites and the exchange of declarations and performances. Earlier meetings had taken place in Christiania. Participants shared concerns about land, autonomy and the future of places outside conventional commercial development.

Ruigoord therefore offers a documented European connection between cannabis culture and the freetown movement, without reducing that movement to a drug market. Its history includes cannabis competitions and personal-use rules alongside poetry, studios and campaigns to preserve a collective place.
