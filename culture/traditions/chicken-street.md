Chicken Street, in Kabul's Shahre-Naw district, became a familiar stop for overland travellers during the 1960s and early 1970s. Cheap accommodation, contact with other travellers and ready access to [hashish](/culture/language/hashish/) helped make the quarter part of the hippie trail. Its social world joined Afghan businesses to a foreign demand that increasingly extended beyond tourism.

Historian James Bradford places the street within a much older Afghan cannabis economy. Visitors encountered an existing product and trade; they helped change their scale and destinations. Like [Freak Street](/culture/traditions/freak-street/) in Kathmandu, it became famous through travellers' accounts, but its legal circumstances were different.

## Hotels and an overland meeting place

Bradford identifies Chicken Street as a place where foreigners could easily find hashish and names the Noor and Mustafa hotels as popular meeting points. People on the overland route exchanged information and encountered sellers within the same urban setting.

He reports almost sixty thousand tourists arriving in Kabul in 1971, many interested in the hashish trade. That figure concerns the city's tourism, not a count of Chicken Street customers or proof that every visitor used drugs.

Travellers often imagined Afghanistan as a place where hashish belonged as naturally as wine in France. Bradford criticises the simplification: foreign enthusiasm could ignore local customs, legal restrictions and Afghan disagreement about consumption.

## A local culture beyond the visitors

Afghan smoking traditions were not confined to the tourist quarter. Bradford describes hashish tea-houses, or *saqikhanas*, and recounts the traditions associated with Baba Qu in Balkh. The story of an ancient healer bringing cannabis is a legend, rather than a verified introduction date.

The Afghanistan Analysts Network's 2019 account describes communal pipe-smoking at Baba Qu's tomb alongside the stigma attached to regular smokers. Hospitality and disapproval could coexist. Neither that account nor the Kabul tourist trade represents the views of all Afghans.

This distinction also separates Chicken Street's commercial encounters from a religious smoking gathering. A traveller buying hashish in Kabul was not necessarily participating in an Afghan devotional custom.

## From purchases to an international trade

Some visitors carried cannabis home; organised traffickers later sought much larger quantities. Bradford traces how those connections linked Afghan producers with European and North American markets, alongside older regional commerce.

Demand affected production and rural income. His chapter describes access to machinery and changes in processing as suppliers pursued greater volume. It also records concerns about adulteration and declining quality. The reputation of Afghan hashish was a commercial claim as well as an attraction for travellers.

Afghan growers and traders made choices within that market. Bradford connects their participation to economic opportunities that state development programmes had failed to provide, rather than treating them as passive suppliers to foreigners.

## Tolerance was an unstable arrangement

The [1924–1925 penal code](/history/afghanistan-penal-code-1924/) had already restricted domestic cannabis use. Open sales during the tourist years did not establish a legally protected enclave.

In a [February 1972 telegram](/history/afghanistan-embassy-press-campaign-1972/), the US embassy described circulating alarming foreign press coverage to influence Afghan officials. The [1972 enforcement drive](/history/afghanistan-smuggling-crackdown-1972/) and [1973 cultivation ban](/history/afghanistan-ban-1973/) belong to the changing political conditions around the trade. The street's hippie-trail reputation records a particular historical period, not its present legal status.
