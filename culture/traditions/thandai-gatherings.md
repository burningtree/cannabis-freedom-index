Preparing *bhang thandai* could make a gathering before anyone drank it. In Khwaja A. Hasan’s account of north Indian social life, relatives, neighbours or friends met at someone’s home, collected ingredients and talked while grinding them for a sweet milk drink containing [bhang](/culture/language/bhang/). The work could take about an hour.

His chapter in *Cannabis and Culture* (1975) describes more than a festival refreshment. It records food preparation as an occasion for company, conversation and the expression of social boundaries. The chapter draws partly on research in Chinaura, near Lucknow, in 1959–60. Its descriptions should not be treated as rules followed by every Indian household or as evidence of the practice’s present extent.

## Company around the grinding stone

Hasan describes a *silauti*, a toothed stone grinding surface, as a central tool. Nuts, rose petals and spices contributed to the drink, with milk and sweetening among its other ingredients. This is a description of shared culinary work, rather than a fixed recipe: his account does not establish one preparation used everywhere.

Conversation continued through the work. Family matters, friends, prices and other everyday concerns passed around the room. Preparing the drink provided time for discussion as well as something to serve at its end.

The distinction between the drink and its cannabis ingredient matters. *Bhang* names the cannabis preparation; *thandai* names the cooling drink. The subject here is Hasan’s account of gatherings where the two were combined, not a claim that every glass called thandai contains cannabis.

## Familiar company, separate circles

The gatherings could follow ties of family, friendship, neighbourhood or caste. They were not necessarily open to anyone who wished to join. Hasan specifically says that people classified in the lower ranks of the caste order generally held their own parties rather than joining those of the twice-born castes.

That exclusion belongs to the historical account. It should neither be concealed beneath a picture of universal hospitality nor turned into a timeless rule about Indian social life.

Hasan compares these gatherings with smoking circles where everyday conversation also accompanied cannabis. But a shared clay pipe and a drink prepared at home involved different acts, materials and rules. Their common feature in his analysis was the social occasion, rather than simply the consumption of an intoxicant.

## Devotion without a requirement to consume

The chapter places cannabis within religious as well as household life. Hasan describes offerings to Shiva and *Bhagats*, devotees who adopted particular dietary and personal restrictions. In the setting he studied, abstaining from alcohol did not necessarily mean abstaining from cannabis.

He also explicitly says that becoming a Bhagat did not require using hemp drugs. Religious acceptance, permission and obligation were different things. The larger festival setting is described in [Bhang at Holi](/culture/traditions/bhang-at-holi/); Hasan’s domestic gatherings show why the preparation cannot be understood through the festival alone.

## From licensed supply to a legal distinction

Hasan wrote of bhang being available through licensed dealers. That is evidence for the period he describes, rather than a statement that supply was unrestricted.

The later legal history distinguishes leaves from other cannabis materials. India [secured a leaf exclusion in the 1961 treaty definition](/history/india-bhang-exemption-1961/), and its [1985 narcotics act](/history/india-ndps-act-1985/) excluded seeds and leaves when not accompanied by flowering tops. State regulation still matters; the name of a drink does not establish its legal position throughout [India](/country/in/).
