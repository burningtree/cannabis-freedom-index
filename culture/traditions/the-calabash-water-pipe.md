A calabash water pipe uses a hollow gourd as its water container and smoke chamber. In nineteenth-century Angola, such vessels belonged to a wider African repertoire of smoking technologies made from gourds, horns, bamboo, wood and other available materials. The pipe pictured here was published as a *mutopa* by the Portuguese traveller Henrique Dias de Carvalho in 1890.

Its importance is both social and technical. Shared pipes accompanied conversation, hospitality and political allegiance, while their makers developed ways to control airflow. Geographer Chris S. Duvall uses these designs to challenge accounts that recognise Asian and American water pipes while overlooking African examples.

## A material that could travel

Duvall identifies an 1832 Angolan account as the earliest description in his sources of calabash-based water pipes in sub-Saharan Africa. By the late nineteenth century, accounts placed related vessels across a much wider region. This is a history of surviving descriptions, rather than a date for the technology's invention.

Gourds supplied a light container that could be combined with a bowl and stem. Many historical African water pipes were portable, unlike the large floor-standing vessels familiar from some Middle Eastern settings. Travellers carried pipes and smoking material between settlements; some carried only selected components and found replacements along the way.

Much of that material culture decays. Archaeological pipe bowls may survive after their gourd, reed or wooden parts disappear, making a complete technological history difficult to reconstruct.

## Shared smoking in the Kasai

In the Kasai region of present-day Democratic Republic of the Congo, the [Bena Riamba](/culture/traditions/bena-riamba/) gave communal cannabis smoking a prominent place in religious and political life. The [1881 encounter recorded by Pogge and Wissmann](/history/dr-congo-bena-riamba-1881/) is one source for that history. Their published imagery includes Mukenge holding a large calabash pipe.

Shared smoking could express trust or belonging. It could also accompany demands for allegiance and coercive ordeals. The vessel should not be treated as a universal African peace pipe: its meanings depended on the people, occasion and political relations involved.

Nor did every pipe carry the same substance. Duvall records a Vili proverb distinguishing tobacco for the *timba*, a dry pipe, from cannabis for the *nkondo*, a calabash water pipe. Such distinctions are local evidence, rather than a rule for the entire continent.

## An opening above the water

The engraving shows a small opening near the base of the gourd's neck. Duvall compares this air-control feature with what modern smokers call a carb hole. His cited nineteenth-century accounts describe using an opening to regulate the passage of air and smoke.

The distinction became important in his comparison with a [United States water-pipe patent issued in 1980](/history/us-water-pipe-patent-1980/). That patent added a separate tube admitting air below the water. The Angolan design admitted air directly into the chamber above it.

This comparison identifies a documented earlier solution to a related problem. It does not establish that the patent's inventors copied an African pipe, or amount to a legal judgment about their claims.

## Reading the surviving image

The illustration is an engraving of an identified Angolan vessel, reproduced from an 1890 publication. It preserves the gourd's shape, decorative bands and projecting stem. It does not show a ceremony or establish what was inside this particular pipe.

Duvall reads the object alongside historical descriptions and vocabulary. Together they make African craftsmanship visible within the history of cannabis paraphernalia, while leaving the technology's exact beginnings and paths of transmission open to further research.
