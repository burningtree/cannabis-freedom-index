The sebsi is Morocco's cannabis pipe: a long thin stem of wood with a clay bowl no bigger than a thimble. It belongs to a way of smoking that is slow, sociable and mild, and that is older than the hashish trade for which the country is now known.

## The pipe

A sebsi has two parts. The stem is hardwood and may be as long as 46 centimetres. The bowl, called a *skuff* or *shkaff*, is narrow, made of clay, and fitted with a fine metal screen. It holds very little, about 25 milligrams, far less than the [chillum](/culture/traditions/sadhus-and-the-chillum/) of India and Jamaica, and gives a small serving at a low temperature.

What goes in it is [kif](/culture/language/kif/): the best parts of the plant, chopped very fine and mixed with tobacco or other herbs. Traditionally the cannabis was the unpollinated female flower and the tobacco a local black one.

## Smoked and eaten

Before the 1960s, cannabis in Morocco was grown in small quantities and used at home. It was smoked in the sebsi, and it was eaten.

| Form | What it is |
|---|---|
| Kif in the sebsi | Chopped flower and tobacco, a pinch at a time |
| [Majoun](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Majoun) | A sweet paste or ball of cannabis with honey, nuts and dried fruit. The name is Arabic for "kneaded". A report of 1957 mentions versions with opium and datura seed as well |
| In food | Mixed into ordinary dishes |

Kif also had an occasional place in Sufi religious ritual.

Hashish, the pressed resin, is a late arrival. Before the first Western travellers came, only small pieces of Lebanese hashish were to be found in the country. It is widely believed that Moroccans began making it during the years of the visitors' influence, and there are competing legends about who taught whom.

## A habit the state once sold

The sebsi and its kif were legal for most of their history. [Sultan Hassan I](/history/morocco-hassan-regulations-1890/) regulated the trade in 1890 and gave certain tribes of the Rif the right to grow. From 1906 a monopoly company sold kif alongside tobacco, and continued to under the French and Spanish protectorates.

| Zone | End of legal kif |
|---|---|
| French zone | [1954](/history/morocco-french-zone-ban-1954/), by a dahir banning all dealings in cannabis |
| Spanish zone | Still licensed in [1954](/history/morocco-spanish-zone-dahir-1954/), with a five-kilogram limit on possession; ended by the national ban |
| The whole kingdom | [1956](/history/morocco-kif-ban-1956/), after independence |

## What is left of it

The law has not changed for the smoker: kif has been illegal since 1956, and [the reform of 2021](/history/morocco-law-13-21-2021/) licenses growing for medicine and industry only. But growing in the Rif and the Jebala was never suppressed, and the old way of smoking has survived with it.

The custom now shares the mountains with an export industry and with tourism. Ketama has become a destination for visitors who come for the hashish, and tours of the farms are sold by word of mouth. In 2009 a senior politician proposed that cannabis be recognised as a traditional Moroccan herbal remedy and not as a dangerous drug, an argument that leans on exactly this history.
