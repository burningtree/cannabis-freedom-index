*La santa rosa*, the sacred rose, is a name for cannabis in a Mexican religious practice documented by anthropologist Roberto Williams-Garcia. In his account, the plant belonged on an altar with incense, bells, Catholic images and objects understood as ancient sacred presences. It received offerings and care, rather than being treated simply as a substance to consume.

His chapter in *Cannabis and Culture* (1975) includes observations from San Pedro in 1953 and later accounts from ritual specialists. It describes a particular community near the Gulf of Mexico, not a practice shared by all Mexicans or all Indigenous peoples. The chapter does not establish its present geographical extent.

## An altar and its caretakers

The plant rested in wrapped bundles near objects described as ancient sacred things, including crystals and archaeological fragments. Daily offerings included water, refreshments and incense. When a ritual specialist travelled, another caretaker could be entrusted with maintaining those obligations.

Women and men could hold religious authority. Williams-Garcia describes a priest and his wife as specialists in their own right, rather than treating the woman as an assistant by definition. Other participants served as *padrinos* and *madrinas*, godparents of the sacred rose.

Musicians also belonged to the ceremony. Their work was understood as part of communication with the plant and sacred presences, not simply entertainment accompanying consumption.

## Shared ceremonies and different experiences

The documented occasions included harvest celebrations, the fulfilment of vows and entry into religious office. In one account, purification with branches, prayers, candles and incense preceded the sharing of the plant. Participants chewed it; the chapter does not provide eyewitness evidence of smoking in these rites.

People described different experiences. Some associated the plant with fluent speech about ceremonial obligations, others with dancing or visions. One experienced woman openly criticised conspicuous speaking performances and favoured quiet thought instead.

That disagreement matters. The account preserves debate within the community, rather than a single response supposedly produced in everyone by cannabis. Williams-Garcia also acknowledges that he could not adequately record and translate everything said during the ceremonies.

## A sacred plant with a story

An origin narrative links the sacred rose with maize, a mother and child, the sun and a heavenly dwelling guarded by Saint Peter. The narrative helps explain why the plant receives ongoing care. It should be read as a religious account, rather than botanical evidence about where cannabis originated.

The Hill of Gold appears as a place associated with sacred beings and the deceased. The plant also had a role in purification around death. This connects its meaning to relationships and obligations extending beyond a single ceremony.

The familiar Spanish saintly name is therefore part of a particular religious vocabulary. It is distinct from the ordinary slang [mota](/culture/language/mota/), and this Mexican account should not be merged with the Brazilian [Santa Maria tradition](/culture/traditions/santa-maria-in-santo-daime/) merely because both use sacred names for cannabis.

## A record made under prohibition

The observations postdate Mexico’s [1920 marijuana ban](/history/mexico-ban-1920/). They show that a sacred use existed within the era of prohibition, but the chapter does not establish a legal exemption, immunity from prosecution or the survival of the ceremony today.

Its strongest evidence is the documented relationship between a named plant, ritual specialists, music and communal care. The 1953 observations provide a historical record; later legal debates about personal cannabis use belong to a different question.
