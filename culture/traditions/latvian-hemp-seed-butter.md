Latvian hemp-seed butter is a spread or relish made from ground hemp seeds, commonly roasted before grinding. It belongs to the country's food culture, especially in descriptions of Vidzeme and rural farm cooking. Spread on dense slices of rye bread, the seeds make cannabis part of an ordinary meal at the household table.

The name *kaņepju sviests* is often translated as hemp butter, but recipes differ. A preparation can contain added fat or consist of the ground seeds themselves. The English word “butter” should not be read as a guarantee that every version contains dairy, or confused with cannabis-infused butter made for intoxication.

## Seeds ground into a spread

The rural tourism association Lauku Ceļotājs describes the hemp preparation at Adzelvieši farm as made without added butter, vegetable fat or salt. Its account includes roasted seeds and traditional processing. This is a useful example of how a familiar English label can conceal a difference in ingredients.

A contemporary Latvian manufacturer's ingredient list gives another version: ground hemp seeds with rapeseed oil and salt. It suggests the spread with bread and several everyday foods. That commercial example documents one recipe in circulation; it does not establish a mandatory formula for the tradition as a whole.

Both preparations begin with the seed as food. Neither should be described as an edible made by extracting intoxicating resin from cannabis flowers.

## A farm keeps the practice visible

Adzelvieši, near Lake Burtnieki, is a documented place where cultivation, processing and tasting are presented together. Visit Valmiera describes hemp grown for seed, relish made according to an older cooking tradition, and hosts introducing visitors to the farm and its produce.

The culinary-route manual places the farm within a regional food itinerary. Demonstrations preserve practical knowledge alongside the finished product: visitors can learn what is grown and how it becomes something to eat. These accounts show a tradition being maintained and interpreted for visitors. They do not establish a precise date when the dish was invented.

Latvia's national tourism site also lists hemp oil, butter and flour among foods found in local markets. That wider context matters: the farm is a particularly well-documented example within a continuing food culture, rather than evidence that one business invented Latvian hemp cuisine.

## The crop and the police

The country's [2009 hemp revival](/history/latvia-hemp-revival-2009/) brought renewed attention to an agricultural crop with older uses. The contemporary account also described growers' fear of being required to prove their plants were non-narcotic.

The distinction became painfully concrete when police [removed a pensioner's kitchen-garden hemp in 2018](/history/latvia-hemp-woman-2018/). Tests found no narcotic, the case was closed and police apologised. Later compensation addressed the harm caused. That case belongs beside the food history because recognition of a familiar crop did not automatically protect its grower from suspicion.

Latvian hemp-seed butter joins Silesian [siemieniotka](/culture/traditions/siemieniotka/) and Lithuania's [hemp-man carnival](/culture/traditions/kanapinis-the-hemp-man/) in documenting European associations between cannabis, nourishment and rural life. Each has its own regional form; together they show why the plant's cultural history reaches beyond modern recreational use.
