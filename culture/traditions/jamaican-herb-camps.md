In the Jamaican communities described by anthropologist Lambros Comitas, a *herb camp* was more than a place to buy [ganja](/culture/language/ganja/). A vendor might provide dominoes, recorded music and drinks near his home. A *herb yard* was simpler: somewhere people could gather to smoke, without the additional facilities.

Comitas documented these settings in an early-1970s research programme, published in *Cannabis and Culture* (1975). They belong to his wider account of working-class social life under prohibition. They should not be confused with today's licensed dispensaries, or treated as religious institutions merely because cannabis was present.

## A distinction within informal trade

Comitas describes larger vendors serving roughly fifty to a hundred smoker-clients. A major vendor might run a camp close to his house and sell beer, ale or stout alongside cannabis. A record player, dominoes and occasionally a television made the setting a place to spend time.

The yard offered less. Someone might buy a small quantity from its operator or bring their own cannabis to smoke there. The distinction concerned the social facilities as well as the scale of the business. Comitas credits fellow researcher Claudia Rogers for distinguishing the two terms.

These descriptions identify particular informal venues. They do not establish an island-wide classification, a licensing category or an unchanged institution surviving everywhere in Jamaica.

## Trust beyond the yard

The venues existed within a broader network of small sellers and intermediaries. Comitas describes part-time vendors with a limited circle of trusted customers. New buyers might enter through an existing customer's recommendation. Someone could also ask a friend to obtain ganja, and that friend might ask another person in turn.

These indirect transactions could connect a seller with more people than the seller personally knew. The account shows why simply counting a vendor's direct acquaintances would miss part of the market.

Comitas contrasts distribution with cultivation. He found growers generally working cannabis plots individually, whereas lawful farming often involved labour exchange. He interpreted the solitary approach as a response to the legal and social risks of a crop being raided, stolen or neglected by a partner.

## Company was broader than a sacrament

Adult smoking networks in the chapter often centred on workmates and trusted neighbours. People might bring their own [spliffs](/culture/language/spliff/) or take turns supplying an evening's cannabis. This wider sociability should not be collapsed into the distinct [Rastafari sacrament](/culture/traditions/rastafari-sacrament/).

Comitas also records beliefs that ganja helped people undertake difficult work. Those are participants' explanations of their conduct, not measurements establishing improved performance. His subject was the relationship between a plant, everyday obligations and membership in a social circle.

The same conduct could mean belonging within one group and loss of respect within another. He describes people seeking higher social status distancing themselves from smoking, even when it remained accepted among former companions.

## An informal venue is not a legal exemption

The account postdates [Jamaica's 1913 Ganja Law](/history/jamaica-ganja-law-1913/). Familiarity within a neighbourhood did not make the trade lawful.

The [2015 reform](/history/jamaica-ganja-reform-2015/) changed possession, household cultivation and sacramental use, and created a regulated medical industry. A [licensed herb house opened in 2018](/history/jamaica-first-herb-house-2018/). Similar names do not establish continuity of legal status: Comitas's informal camps and yards were not the later licensing system. His chapter documents a historical social setting in [Jamaica](/country/jm/), without establishing the present extent of those venues.
