In a Scottish Halloween courtship charm recorded by Robert Burns, a person scattered hemp seed in the hope of seeing a future lover. The imagined figure would follow behind, gathering the hemp. Cannabis appears here through agricultural work and expectations of marriage, rather than through a preparation to smoke or eat.

Burns's poem *Halloween* and its explanatory notes are direct literary evidence for the custom. They are also a particular author's presentation of rural life, with jokes, scepticism and a carefully constructed scene. They do not prove that everyone in Scotland observed the practice, or that it survives widely today.

## A future partner in the hemp field

The author's note describes secret sowing, an imitation of harrowing and a spoken appeal to the person destined to become the participant's sweetheart. Looking over the left shoulder was supposed to reveal the figure. The imagined work of harvesting answered the real action of sowing.

That pairing is what makes hemp distinctive within the charm. Seed and future crop provided a way to picture an uncertain future relationship. The source describes an expectation of an apparition; it does not describe a drug-induced vision. No ingestion, smoke or intoxicating preparation is involved.

## Burns makes a joke of fear

In the poem, Jamie Fleck initially dismisses frightening stories. When he ventures out with the hemp seed, his confidence gives way to alarm. A pig supplies the comic explanation for what frightens him.

Reading the episode alongside the note preserves both parts of the evidence: Burns records the belief and turns a character's response to it into comedy. Treating the episode as proof of a hidden cannabis intoxication ritual would lose the distinction the text itself makes.

The poem appeared in the [1786 Kilmarnock collection](/history/uk-burns-halloween-1786/), digitised by the University of Glasgow. Publication gives a secure point by which the custom was in print, rather than a date when it began.

## A familiar crop before prohibition

The charm belongs to a different cultural setting from Britain's later [cannabis prohibition](/history/uk-ban-1928/). Modern legislation also distinguishes the seed from the controlled plant material: section 37 of the Misuse of Drugs Act excludes seed from its definition of cannabis. That distinction does not create general permission to cultivate the plant.

Like Lithuania's [Kanapinis](/culture/traditions/kanapinis-the-hemp-man/), the Scottish charm gives hemp a role in seasonal folklore. Their meanings differ: a staged contest welcomes spring in one, while sowing promises knowledge of a future partner in the other. Both document a cultural imagination shaped by a familiar agricultural plant.
