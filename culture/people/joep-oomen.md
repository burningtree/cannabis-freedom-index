Joep Oomen built a legal argument out of a single plant. Belgian law tolerated an adult growing one
cannabis plant for personal use. "If you're allowed to grow one plant," he reasoned, "you could also
have that plant cared for by someone else." On that idea he [founded Trekt Uw Plant](/history/belgium-trekt-uw-plant-2006/), "pull your
plant", in Antwerp in 2006: the first [cannabis social club](/culture/traditions/cannabis-social-clubs/)
in Belgium.

He was born in the Netherlands and moved to Antwerp in the 1990s. In 1993 he was one of the founders
of ENCOD, the European Coalition for Just and Effective Drug Policies, and as its coordinator ran
its office in Antwerp, the driving force, in the words of Dutch colleagues, behind a coalition that
reached into almost every country in Europe. He spoke Spanish, and was a link between the Belgian
movement and Spain, where cannabis clubs were already common.

He told grow! in 2013 that he had smoked cannabis since he was thirteen, and that it had never been
a problem for him in the Netherlands, with its coffeeshops. What changed his mind about drug policy
was a journey through South America in the 1980s, where he saw the violence, the abuses and the
poverty that the war on drugs brought to the producing countries.

## One plant, many owners

The opening came from the law. In [2003](/history/belgium-law-2003/) Belgium set cannabis apart from
other drugs, and a [directive of 2005](/history/belgium-three-grams-2005/) told prosecutors that an
adult holding up to 3 grams or one plant, without nuisance, should get only a simplified police
record. Trekt Uw Plant turned that individual tolerance into a collective arrangement: if one plant per
adult was the lowest priority for prosecutors, members could have their single plants grown
together, each tagged with a deed of ownership and a copy of the member's identity card. The
idea came, he said, from Spain, where clubs had worked on the same principle since 1995 and which
he knew through ENCOD. It was a deliberate test of the law. The directive told prosecutors what to
ignore; it said nothing about growing for others, and nothing in it authorised a club.

| How the club works | |
|---|---|
| Each member | Has one plant, grown for them in a shared grow room, with a name tag |
| Every two or three months | Members collect the harvest at an exchange market, the *ruilbeurs* |
| What members pay | A membership fee and the cost of growing, about €7 a gram |
| Members | About 400 |

## In court

The authorities did not accept the reasoning at once. Oomen was arrested in December 2006, at the
attempt to found the club. In May 2008 its board members each planted a single seed in a pot in
public, and were convicted of inciting drug use; on 25 February 2010 the appeal court in Antwerp
acquitted them, holding that the club did not set out to persuade others to use drugs. It was
prosecuted twice, and twice acquitted, and it has worked undisturbed since. But the rulings
settled only the charges in front of the court. Belgium never passed a law allowing collective
cultivation, and the clubs stayed in a grey zone.

Oomen's advice to the club's growers was to meet a police raid with an open door, a smile and a
pot of coffee, and an explanation of how the system worked. By 2013 there were about 250 members
and nineteen growers. Others who
copied it fared worse. A member, Michel Degens, started Mambo Social Club in Hasselt. It was
raided, and its organisers, though acquitted of inciting drug use, were convicted of possession,
production and sale, the court refusing to stretch personal possession to a shared grow room.

## Elsewhere

Through ENCOD he carried the model from country to country, in meetings, conferences and
interviews: a non-profit club, growing only for its own adult members, open about what it did and
ready to explain it to the police. Clubs on the Spanish and Belgian pattern were set up by
activists in several European countries in the years that followed.

In the Netherlands he co-founded the VOC, the Union for the Abolition of Cannabis Prohibition, and
organised a Cannabis Tribunal in The Hague. In Antwerp he also organised shelter for homeless people
for years. He died unexpectedly at home on 18 March 2016, aged 54; the police ruled out a non-natural
cause. In 2020 Trekt Uw Plant was still the largest of Belgium's five registered clubs.
