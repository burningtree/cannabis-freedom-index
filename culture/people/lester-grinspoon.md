Lester Grinspoon was a Harvard psychiatrist who questioned the scientific justification for cannabis prohibition. His [1971 book, *Marihuana Reconsidered*](/history/us-grinspoon-marihuana-reconsidered-1971/), emerged from an investigation that had changed his own assumptions. He later took his arguments into legislative hearings, criminal proceedings and the organised campaign for reform in the [United States](/country/us/).

His role differed from that of a chemist identifying cannabinoids or a patient seeking an individual exemption. Grinspoon reviewed evidence and asked what it justified the state doing to people. That question connected psychiatric scholarship with the penalties imposed by drug law.

## An investigation changes its author

In his [introduction to the book’s later reprint](https://www.rxmarijuana.com/FURTHER_RECONSIDERATION.htm), Grinspoon recalled beginning his research in 1967 with the expectation that cannabis was dangerous. Reading medical, scientific and other published accounts led him to reconsider that expectation. The title recorded the change rather than presenting his position as one he had always held.

Harvard University Press published the book in 1971, shortly after the [Controlled Substances Act of 1970](/history/us-controlled-substances-act-1970/) established the federal scheduling framework. Grinspoon argued that the harms of criminal enforcement needed to be assessed alongside the effects of the substance itself. A judgement about drug policy could not, in his view, be reduced to a list of pharmacological properties.

He also remembered predicting that adult-use legalisation would follow within a decade. That expectation proved wrong. His retrospective account makes the gap between an argument gaining attention and a law actually changing unusually clear.

## Patients and the forbidden medicine

Grinspoon’s interest expanded into the medical uses of cannabis. He and his wife reported that it relieved their son Danny’s chemotherapy-related nausea during treatment for cancer. That family experience helped shape his advocacy, but it remained a personal account rather than a clinical trial establishing a general treatment recommendation.

With James B. Bakalar, he published *Marijuana: The Forbidden Medicine* in 1993. The book argued that prohibition obstructed access to a substance with therapeutic uses. It belonged to the wider movement in which [Robert Randall](/culture/people/robert-randall/) sought federal patient access and [Dennis Peron](/culture/people/dennis-peron/) organised supply and electoral campaigns in California.

These approaches addressed different obstacles. A physician could present evidence, a patient could describe an unmet need, and a campaign could seek a legal exemption. California’s [1996 medical-cannabis law](/history/us-california-medical-1996/) was a state political outcome, not the automatic consequence of any single book or medical argument.

## Evidence inside a criminal proceeding

Grinspoon served as an expert witness before legislative committees and in legal proceedings. His [affidavit in the Boston cases involving Richard Cusick and Russell Stroup](https://norml.org/wp-content/uploads/pdf_files/brief_bank/AFFIDAVIT_OF_LESTER_GRINSPOON_MD.pdf) gives a concrete example. It supported a request to examine the rationality of the cannabis statute through an evidentiary hearing.

The document reviewed reasons courts had accepted for criminal regulation, including mental health, progression to other drugs and road safety. Grinspoon disputed those rationales using his reading of the research. His affidavit was an argument submitted for defendants; it was not itself a judicial finding that the statute was invalid.

Some of its assertions were categorical. They should be understood as his position in that proceeding, not reproduced as current medical consensus. The historical point is that prohibition’s factual premises became questions an expert asked a court to examine.

## Scholarship and organised reform

Grinspoon also chaired NORML’s board and remained an adviser to the organisation. In its [2020 memorial](https://norml.org/blog/2020/06/25/norml-remembers-dr-lester-grinspoon/), founder Keith Stroup emphasised his influence on the movement’s intellectual arguments. NORML is a reform organisation, so its tribute records the judgement of colleagues sharing that cause.

He died in 2020, after decades of publication and testimony. His lasting contribution to the debate was the insistence that evidence about cannabis and evidence about punishment belonged in the same discussion. His books and legal submissions challenged prohibition, while legislative change still depended on courts, voters and lawmakers taking separate decisions.
