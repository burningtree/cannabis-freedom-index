Dennis Peron helped make medical cannabis a question of patient access in the [United States](/country/us/). A cannabis seller and gay-rights activist in San Francisco, he organised a buyers’ club and helped write the initiative that became California’s [1996 medical-cannabis law](/history/us-california-medical-1996/). His campaign grew out of the AIDS crisis, when people seeking relief were also facing isolation and criminal penalties.

Peron worked with other campaigners, including [Mary Jane “Brownie Mary” Rathbun](/culture/people/brownie-mary/). Their work joined practical supply, public testimony and electoral organising. The law was not the achievement of one person, but Peron supplied a particularly visible place where patients and the prohibition they confronted could be seen together.

## From the Castro to patient access

Peron settled in San Francisco after serving in the US Air Force in Vietnam. In the Castro district he sold cannabis, organised protests and supported Harvey Milk. His partner Jonathan West died of AIDS in 1990. Peron’s experience of illness within his community helped focus his campaigning on access to cannabis for medical purposes.

The San Francisco Cannabis Buyers Club emerged in the early 1990s, before California had a medical-cannabis exemption. It operated openly rather than treating supply as an entirely private arrangement. That visibility was politically useful, but it also exposed the organisation and its members to enforcement.

## A club as a social institution

[Rose Ann Fuhrman’s 1995 visit](https://www.cannabisculture.com/content/1995/11/05/1049/) described membership checks, staff helping people enter the building, and customers spending time together around small tables. Cannabis was available in several preparations. The report appeared in an openly sympathetic cannabis magazine; its descriptions of the premises are contemporary observations, while its medical claims and praise should be read in that context.

Peron told the reporter that companionship mattered alongside the substance. People with serious illnesses could withdraw from others, and the club offered a place to meet. The institution therefore combined a supply function with social support. Calling it a dispensary alone misses some of what its participants thought they were building.

## Two propositions with different powers

San Francisco voters approved [Proposition P in November 1991](/history/us-san-francisco-proposition-p-1991/). Its [actual text](https://files.amlegal.com/pdffiles/sanfran/1991-11-05-PropP.pdf) recommended that California restore hemp medical preparations to the medicines available to patients and protect physicians who prescribed them. It was a declaration of policy, not a city exemption from state or federal criminal law.

Peron then helped draft and campaign for Proposition 215. Its [text](https://vigarchive.sos.ca.gov/1996/general/pamphlet/215text.htm) did something more concrete: specified state possession and cultivation offences would not apply to qualifying patients or their primary caregivers with a physician’s recommendation or approval. It also protected physicians for making recommendations and called for safe, affordable distribution.

That last aim did not itself create a comprehensive licensing system for buyers’ clubs. The measure’s protection for personal medical possession and cultivation should not be confused with an unrestricted right to sell cannabis. The gap between patient protection and organised supply remained a source of conflict.

## After the medical victory

Federal officials [threatened action against recommending doctors](/history/us-federal-warning-doctors-1996/) after the California vote. State reform therefore did not end the clash with federal prohibition. Peron’s club also faced raids and closure proceedings despite its medical purpose.

He later opposed California’s 2010 and 2016 adult-use initiatives, arguing that cannabis use was inherently medicinal. That was his political position, rather than an agreed medical definition. His opposition shows that campaigners for access could disagree over the market and legal categories that should follow reform. Peron died in 2018; the patient-centred law he helped secure had already become a foundation for a much larger and differently organised cannabis industry.
