Robert Randall had glaucoma and used marijuana to treat it. When he was charged with growing it, he answered that he had no choice: he needed it for his eyes. A federal court accepted that defence in 1976, and the government that had prosecuted him began to supply him with cannabis. He was the first person in the United States to receive it legally since the [Marihuana Tax Act of 1937](/history/us-marihuana-tax-act-1937/).

## Necessity

The case, *United States v. Randall*, turned on the old legal defence of necessity: that breaking the law is excused when it is the only way to avoid a greater harm. It had never before been extended to possessing or growing marijuana. On [24 November 1976](/history/us-randall-ruling-1976/) the judge ruled in Randall's favour. He then sued the federal agencies, and the settlement gave him supplies of the cannabis grown for government research at the University of Mississippi, provided through a doctor at Howard University Hospital.

His settlement became the model for a federal programme, the Compassionate Investigational New Drug program, set up in 1978. Under it the government itself supplied a small number of patients with cannabis from the same Mississippi farm.

## Other patients

With his wife, Alice O'Leary, Randall spent the following years helping other patients through the same process. In 1981 they founded the Alliance for Cannabis Therapeutics, which led the petition to move cannabis out of the strictest federal schedule. In [1988](/history/us-dea-judge-young-1988/) the DEA's own administrative law judge, Francis Young, recommended exactly that, calling cannabis one of the safest therapeutically active substances known. The DEA administrator rejected the recommendation.

In the early 1990s Randall set up the Marijuana AIDS Research Service to help people with AIDS apply to the federal programme. In [1992](/history/us-compassionate-program-closed-1992/) the government closed it to new patients. Forty-three people had been approved; at most fifteen were ever supplied at one time, and four were still receiving government cannabis in 2011.

## Afterwards

The closure turned campaigners away from federal agencies and towards state ballot initiatives. Randall wrote about his case and the campaign, with O'Leary, in *Marijuana Rx: The Patients' Fight for Medical Pot*. He died in 2001.
