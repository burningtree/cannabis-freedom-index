Sangula Meta held religious authority within the [Bena Riamba](/culture/traditions/bena-riamba/), a cannabis-centred movement in the Lulua Valley of present-day Democratic Republic of the Congo. In Chris S. Duvall's reconstruction, the movement's political leaders depended on her to sustain its theology. Her story places a woman within a history often reduced to male rulers, European explorers and spectacular smoking pipes.

She belonged to the turbulent late nineteenth century, when commerce, firearms and colonial expansion transformed the Kasai region. Her birth and death dates are not established in the sources used here.

## Religious authority in a political movement

The movement's early leader, Chishimbi, linked cannabis smoking with friendship and access to new trading relationships. Following his death, rival leaders including Mukenge and Chingenge competed for power. Duvall distinguishes their political leadership from Sangula Meta's religious responsibilities: she maintained the theology associated with Chishimbi's reforms.

This distinction does not make her role separate from government. Shared smoking expressed commitment to a new social order, while older religious practices were suppressed. Religious authority could help enforce allegiance as well as create belonging. Cannabis was embedded in that system of rule.

## A family connection left uncertain

Duvall considers Sangula Meta probably Mukenge's sister, but his notes preserve a disagreement. German accounts presented a sibling relationship, whereas the Portuguese traveller Henrique Dias de Carvalho suggested she was Chingenge's wife.

Those alternatives matter because the observers favoured different political leaders. Treating one family connection as settled would conceal the uncertainty of the surviving record. The same problem affects names and titles elsewhere in the movement: European writers used different spellings and did not consistently distinguish personal names from positions of authority.

## Admiration and coercion in the record

European observers described Sangula Meta as forceful and intellectually capable. Their praise accompanied reports of coercion. Duvall recounts an episode in which she caned reluctant guards or porters into action for Hermann von Wissmann, and another attributing a severe punishment using chilli-pepper juice to her.

These are reports transmitted by outsiders, including men who benefited from the movement's labour and protection. They establish how those writers portrayed her authority; they cannot supply her own explanation of it. Their account gives little access to how the people she governed understood her actions.

The movement itself combined ceremonial cannabis, hospitality and communal life with warfare, unequal power and participation in trading captives. Sangula Meta's prominence should be read within that setting, without turning female leadership into evidence of an egalitarian society.

## The portrait that survives

The engraving reproduced here appeared as “Sangula Meta” in the third edition of *Im Innern Afrikas* in 1891. Duvall reproduces it as figure 6.3. It is a nineteenth-century representation, mediated by the expedition's publication, rather than a photograph or a record of cannabis consumption.

Its value is specific: it identifies a named person who exercised authority in a cannabis-centred movement. Alongside the [1881 encounter recorded by Pogge and Wissmann](/history/dr-congo-bena-riamba-1881/), it helps recover a figure whose role disappears when the Bena Riamba are described simply as a peaceful brotherhood of hemp smokers.
