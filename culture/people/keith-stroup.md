Keith Stroup started from a simple idea: people who smoked marijuana were consumers, and consumers
could organise. In 1970, two years out of law school, he founded the
[National Organization for the Reform of Marijuana Laws](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NORML), NORML.[@1,2]
It still campaigns for the removal of all penalties for adults who possess, grow or casually share
small amounts.[@2]

Stroup studied political science at the University of Illinois, graduating in 1965, and law at
Georgetown, while working in the office of Senator Everett Dirksen. His first job after law school,
at the federal commission on product safety, brought him into contact with the consumer campaigner
Ralph Nader, whose methods he set out to copy.[@1]

## Repeal, then reform

He [founded the organisation](/history/us-norml-founded-1970/) with 5,000 dollars of seed money from
the Playboy Foundation.[@1,2] At first the R stood for "Repeal". The former Attorney General Ramsey Clark
advised him that "Reform" would make it more approachable, and he changed it.[@1] By the middle of the
1970s the backing of Playboy's owner, Hugh Hefner, at one point 100,000 dollars a year, had made
NORML the leading group campaigning to decriminalise marijuana, and in 1974 it absorbed
Amorphia, the Californian group that had run the state's
[failed legalisation initiative](/history/us-california-legalisation-vote-1972/) of 1972.[@2]

The years suited the argument. A national commission had
[recommended decriminalisation](/history/us-shafer-commission-1972/) in 1972. In 1973
[Oregon](/history/us-oregon-decriminalisation-1973/) made possessing an ounce a violation with a
fine, and while Stroup ran NORML, until 1979, eleven states adopted decriminalisation laws.[@1]

He found an unlikely fundraiser in an old scare film. In 1972 he learned that *Reefer Madness*, the 1930s melodrama of teenagers ruined by marijuana, was in the public domain, and [bought a print at the Library of Congress for 297 dollars](/history/us-reefer-madness-campus-revival-1972/). He showed it on college campuses after his lectures.[@3] Students who were meant to be frightened laughed, and the film became a joke about prohibition itself; its story is told in the exhibition [Reefer Madness](/reefer-madness/).[@3]

## The paraquat quarrel

The decade ended in a misjudgement. President Jimmy Carter's administration favoured reform, and in
1977 Carter [asked Congress](/history/us-carter-decriminalisation-message-1977/) to support state
decriminalisation. But Stroup fell out with Carter's drug adviser, Peter Bourne, over the spraying
of Mexican marijuana fields with the herbicide paraquat, which Stroup wanted ended.[@1] In
retaliation Stroup told a reporter that Bourne had taken cocaine at NORML's Christmas party in 1977.
Bourne was fired, and Stroup soon lost his own job: by one account, the people at NORML did not
like informers.[@1]

## The return

He spent the next years lobbying for family farmers and for artists, and from 1989 to 1994 ran the
National Association of Criminal Defense Lawyers. In 1995 he came back as NORML's executive
director, and for ten years he was the organisation's chief spokesman. The year he rejoined its
board, 1994, [Willie Nelson](/culture/people/willie-nelson/) became
[co-chair of its advisory board](/history/us-willie-nelson-norml-cochair-1994/).[@1]

He stepped down in January 2005, saying the cause needed a younger generation of activists, and
stayed on as NORML's legal counsel. He told the story of the organisation in a book, *It's NORML to
Smoke Pot: The 40-Year Fight for Marijuana Smokers' Rights*.[@1]
