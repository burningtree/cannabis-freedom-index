Thomas Adeoye Lambo (1923–2004) was a pioneering Nigerian psychiatrist whose work helped bring cannabis onto the country's medical and political agenda. His [1965 study of addiction](/history/nigeria-lambo-addiction-study-1965/) considered social circumstances, patients' experiences and the meaning of dependence. Within a year, [Nigeria](/country/ng/)'s military government had adopted a very different response: the [Indian Hemp Decree](/history/nigeria-indian-hemp-decree-1966/), which made cultivation punishable by death.

The contrast is central to historian Gernot Klantschnig's account of early Nigerian cannabis policy. Lambo helped establish a field of medical expertise, but expertise did not determine the law that followed. His research should not be read as advocacy of cannabis legalization or as the direct cause of the 1966 prohibition. His own assumptions about class also shaped what he saw in the clinic.

## Psychiatry in its social setting

Born in Abeokuta in 1923, Lambo became a leading figure in Nigerian psychiatry and later a deputy director-general of the World Health Organization. His wider research examined the relationship between culture, social change and mental health. In Abeokuta, he established the Aro Village System, integrating psychiatric treatment with community and family life. The Aro Hospital and its associated therapeutic village blended traditional and modern approaches to mental healing.

His cannabis research belonged to this broader project. Klantschnig describes him as one of the first West African psychiatrists to investigate cannabis-using patients and their possible dependence. He discussed African cannabis use in the 1950s, before newspapers made a supposed hemp boom a regular story.

His 1965 paper, *Medical and Social Problems of Drug Addiction in West Africa (With Special Emphasis on Psychiatric Aspects)*, appeared in the *West African Medical Journal*, volume 14, issue 6, pages 236–254. His work also appeared in the United Nations' *Bulletin on Narcotics*. Those publications carried the debate beyond an individual hospital and into international discussions of drug use. The PubMed citation provides a bibliographic record; the account of his cannabis research here follows Klantschnig's historical analysis rather than an independent reading of the original paper.

## What counted as addiction

Lambo questioned whether the cannabis users he treated met the accepted addiction criteria of his time. Klantschnig records his uncertainty about applying definitions centred on dependence, increasing doses and withdrawal to those patients.

He instead included disruption of social and working life in his account. Migration, rapid economic change, group identification and access to drugs all mattered. Cannabis use could not, in this approach, be explained simply by criminal character or by the existence of a supply.

He also resisted an automatic equation between prolonged use and permanent mental disturbance, while describing impairment in some patients. These were historical clinical judgments. They should not be read as a present-day assessment of cannabis risks or as a representative survey of all users.

## The limits of his categories

Lambo's emphasis on people he called “marginal Africans” reflected contemporary assumptions about migrant workers, former soldiers and political thugs. Klantschnig challenges that picture: students and other socially respectable groups accounted for more than a third of Lambo's own patients by 1965.

That criticism matters because a clinical classification could reinforce a public stereotype even when its author sought a more complicated explanation. Klantschnig also argues that press accounts turned psychiatrists' public interventions into apparent confirmation that cannabis caused madness. Medical discussion and newspaper alarm did not carry the same claims.

## A medical voice without control of policy

Lambo's proposed response combined treatment, education, law and longer-term social change. The military rulers of 1966 pursued an immediate demonstration of discipline and authority. Their decree drew on anxieties about youth, political violence and cannabis use among soldiers.

The distinction does not make historical psychiatric care uniformly humane. Klantschnig specifically notes coercive admission among its repressive features. Lambo's importance lies in the questions he raised about patients, social conditions and definitions of addiction, and in the limited influence those questions had on the punitive law adopted after his study.
