João Goulão is a general practitioner who spent his career treating people dependent on drugs, and
the official most associated with what other countries call the Portuguese model. Since 2001,
possessing a small amount of any drug for personal use in Portugal, cannabis included, has not been
a crime: it is a matter for a dissuasion commission rather than a court.

He was born in 1954 in Cernache do Bonjardim, studied medicine at the University of Lisbon, and
became a general practitioner in 1983. From 1987 he specialised in treating addiction, first at the
Taipas Centre in Lisbon, which the health ministry had opened for "the treatment, recovery and social
reintegration of drug addicts", and from 1997 as national director of the country's network of
treatment centres.

## Ill, not criminal

At the end of the 1990s Portugal had an estimated 50,000 to 100,000 heroin users and the highest
rate of HIV among people who inject drugs in the European Union. In 1998 Goulão was one of eleven
members of a committee asked what to do about it. Its report became the basis of the
[national strategy of 1999](/history/portugal-national-strategy-1999/), which expanded harm
reduction, doubled public spending on treatment and prevention and changed the law on minor drug
offences. "One of our premises," he said of the committee's work, "is that drug abusers are ill, not
criminals, and that they need help."

The change in the law came in [July 2001](/history/portugal-decriminalisation-2001/).

| Under the 2001 law | |
|---|---|
| A ten-day supply of any drug | No longer a crime |
| For cannabis flower | 25 g |
| What the police do | Confiscate it and refer the person to a dissuasion commission |
| What the commission can do | Suspend the case or impose a fine |
| Growing and selling | Still crimes |

## Why it could pass

Goulão has offered one explanation for why Portugal could do this when others could not: the drug
problem there could not be blamed on any one ethnic or economic group, so other prejudices could be
set aside. Right-wing politicians opposed the policy at first, warning that it would turn Portugal
into a narco-state.

## The coordinator

Goulão became head of the state's drug and addiction services in 2005, and national drug
coordinator. From 2009 to 2015 he chaired the European
Monitoring Centre for Drugs and Drug Addiction, the EU's drugs agency, and he has represented
Portugal at the UN Commission on Narcotic Drugs. In 2013 the American Drug Policy Alliance gave him
its Norman E. Zinberg award for medicine.

Decriminalisation in [Portugal](/country/pt/) covered possession, not supply. Growing and selling
cannabis remain crimes there.
