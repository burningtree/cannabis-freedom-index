Mary Jane Rathbun became known as “Brownie Mary” for supplying cannabis brownies to people with AIDS in San Francisco. A hospital volunteer rather than a physician, she joined everyday care to a campaign against the criminal penalties attached to cannabis. Her arrests brought public attention to patients who said it helped them eat and endure treatment.

With [Dennis Peron](/culture/people/dennis-peron/), she supported the local campaign for medical cannabis and California’s later statewide initiative. Her significance lies in that work: the brownies were a means of access and a public symbol, not simply an early version of a commercial edible.

## Baking becomes a criminal case

Rathbun initially sold cannabis brownies to supplement her income. Printed advertisements and an undercover investigation led to her [first arrest in 1981](/history/us-brownie-mary-arrest-1981/). She pleaded guilty and received probation and community service. The punishment also placed her in organisations providing practical support to vulnerable people.

As the AIDS crisis developed, many people she knew in the Castro became ill. She began giving brownies to patients rather than treating every batch as merchandise. Donations of cannabis helped sustain the work. This change from selling to giving matters to the history: the activity for which she became best known was embedded in care for a community experiencing severe illness and loss.

## The work beyond the brownies

At San Francisco General Hospital’s Ward 86, Rathbun spent years volunteering with people with AIDS. Her [contemporary obituary](https://www.sfgate.com/news/article/Mary-Jane-Rathbun-Popularly-Known-As-Brownie-Mary-2936526.php) describes thousands of hours of service over more than a decade. Patients became “her kids,” a phrase reflecting the personal relationships behind her public campaigning.

Accounts describe patients reporting relief from nausea and difficulty eating. Those reports explain why the brownies mattered to them, but Rathbun’s observations were not controlled clinical trials. Her contribution was patient support and advocacy. It should not be turned into a claim that her particular preparation had established safety or efficacy for everyone with AIDS or cancer.

[Gastro Obscura’s account](https://www.atlasobscura.com/articles/brownie-mary) also describes ordinary cookies, companionship and encouragement among her activities. The cannabis attracted headlines; much of the volunteering was less dramatic. Keeping both in view explains why a hospital volunteer became a persuasive figure in arguments about criminalisation.

## From arrests to a ballot campaign

Rathbun was arrested three times. Coverage increasingly presented her as a caregiver campaigning for sick people, rather than only as a seller breaking drug law. Her age and appearance helped unsettle the familiar image of the cannabis offender, although those press portrayals are part of the campaign’s reception rather than a measure of medical effectiveness.

She worked on San Francisco’s [1991 Proposition P](/history/us-san-francisco-proposition-p-1991/). The [declaration’s text](https://files.amlegal.com/pdffiles/sanfran/1991-11-05-PropP.pdf) urged California and its medical association to restore access to hemp medicines and protect prescribing physicians. It expressed the city’s position; it could not independently remove state or federal penalties.

Rathbun also contributed to the buyers’ club movement and campaigned for the statewide measure that followed. The organising connected patients, caregivers and voters. Public sympathy did not itself make the distribution lawful, but it helped put the question of medical access before the electorate.

## What the California law changed

California voters approved [Proposition 215 in 1996](/history/us-california-medical-1996/), during Rathbun’s lifetime. Its [text](https://vigarchive.sos.ca.gov/1996/general/pamphlet/215text.htm) protected qualifying patients and primary caregivers from specified state possession and cultivation offences when a physician recommended or approved the medical use. It protected physicians for recommendations and encouraged a plan for affordable supply.

The law did not repeal federal prohibition or authorise every form of commercial distribution. Rathbun died in 1999, after seeing the medical campaign win a substantial state-law protection. Her story records how informal care helped change that law, while the original distinction between a patient exemption and a complete legal supply system remained unresolved.
