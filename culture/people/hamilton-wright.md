Hamilton Wright was a doctor who became the first in a long line of American drug crusaders. As
the country's opium commissioner from 1908 he shaped both its drug diplomacy abroad and its drug
laws at home, and he wanted cannabis included in both, at a time when cannabis medicines were
still widely sold.

He was born in Cleveland, Ohio, in 1867, and qualified in medicine at McGill University in
Montreal in 1895. He spent years in Asia: two in China and Japan, then a mission to India, and
from 1900 the directorship of the new Pathological Institute in Kuala Lumpur, in the Federated
Malay States. He made his name by claiming to have found the germ that caused beriberi, a disease
later shown to come from a vitamin deficiency.

## Opium commissioner

President Theodore Roosevelt appointed him United States Opium Commissioner on 1 July 1908. He was
a delegate to the International Opium Commission at Shanghai in 1909 and to the conference that
followed at The Hague. At home he made the case in the press. "Of all the nations of the world,"
he told *The New York Times* in 1911, "the United States consumes most habit-forming drugs per
capita."

## The hydraulic model

In [1910](/history/us-wright-cannabis-bill-1910/) Wright persuaded David Foster, chairman of the
House Committee on Foreign Affairs, to sponsor a bill controlling opiates, cocaine, chloral and
cannabis. His reasoning, as the Transnational Institute describes it, was that if one dangerous
drug were effectively prohibited, habitual users would simply switch to another, so as many
substances as possible should be banned. The institute calls it "a hydraulic model of drug
appetites", a reverse gateway theory. The bill was defeated in 1911, mainly by the pharmaceutical industry. A revised
version, without cannabis, became the Harrison Act, in force from 1 March 1915. Cannabis would not
be banned federally until 1937.

## The Hague

At The Hague the subject came from Italy, worried about hashish smuggled into its new North
African colony. Many delegates were bewildered: they had no statistics, no scientific definition
and no instructions. The Dutch chairman suggested that countries deal with cannabis at home. The
United States alone supported Italy, whose delegation had already left after the first day, and
the [convention signed in 1912](/history/hague-opium-convention-1912/) said nothing about
cannabis. It was brought under international control thirteen years later.

Wright died in 1917. His wife, Elizabeth Washburne, carried on his work as an assessor to the
League of Nations' opium committee into the 1920s. Federal prohibition came in
[1937](/history/us-marihuana-tax-act-1937/), under
[Harry Anslinger](/culture/people/harry-anslinger/).
