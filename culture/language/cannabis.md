*Cannabis* is the botanical name of the plant and the word the international treaties use. It is very old and it is not Latin in origin. The Romans had it from the Greeks, and the Greeks from the Scythians, nomads of the steppe. The English word *hemp* is the same word, borrowed far earlier by the ancestors of the Germanic peoples and changed by two thousand years of sound shifts.

## From the steppe to Linnaeus

| Stage | Form |
|---|---|
| Scythian (or Thracian) | The source word, not recorded directly |
| Persian | *kanab* |
| Greek | κάνναβις, *kánnabis* |
| Latin | *cannabis* |
| Botanical Latin | *Cannabis*, proposed in 1728 and fixed by Linnaeus in *Species Plantarum*, 1753 |

The Greeks learned of the plant's use by watching Scythian funerals. [Herodotus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Herodotus), about 440 BCE, describes the Scythians throwing hemp on red-hot stones in a felt tent and shouting for joy at the vapour (see [the Scythian vapour bath](/culture/traditions/the-scythian-vapour-bath/)). *Kánnabis* is the earliest recorded word for the plant as a drug.

Assyrian texts of the first millennium BCE have a word *qunnabu* for a source of oil, fibre and medicine, which belongs to the same family.

## Hemp is the same word

English *hemp*, Old English *hænep*, goes back to a Germanic form *hanapiz*. By the sound change known as [Grimm's law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grimm%27s_law), an original *k* became *h* in the Germanic languages. So *hanap-* points back to *kanab-*. The borrowing must have happened before that change, no later than about 500 BCE: the Germanic peoples had the word, and presumably the plant, long before they had any contact with Rome.

## Borrowed twice

The linguist [Elizabeth Wayland Barber](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elizabeth_Wayland_Barber) proposed that the word spread across Eurasia in two waves. People throughout the middle latitudes had grown hemp for fibre and food since about 5000 BCE, and had an old short name for it of the form *ken-* or *kan-*. The northern plant had little or no intoxicating power. When, some four thousand years later, the same peoples borrowed a new and longer word, *kannab-*, it must have been for something new: the drug-bearing variety, spreading from Iran in the early Iron Age together with the habit of using it.

Sanskrit preserves both layers: *śaṇa*, "a kind of hemp", and *bhanga*, "narcotic hemp", the ancestor of [bhang](/culture/language/bhang/).

## A Semitic source?

The anthropologist [Sula Benet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sula_Benet) argued that the word was Semitic, from Hebrew *qěnēh bośem*, the "aromatic reed" that Exodus lists among the ingredients of the holy anointing oil. Her theory was never widely accepted. Most scholars think *qěnēh bośem* is lemon grass or calamus, and that the word for hemp reached the Near East with Iranian traders. The Hebrew *qanbes* of the Mishnah, which does mean hemp, is a loan from the Greek.

## Two words, two meanings

In English the Latin word was at first only the botanist's term: the Oxford dictionary's first record of *cannabis* for the plant is from 1548. Its use for the parts "smoked, chewed, or drunk for their intoxicating or hallucinogenic properties" dates from 1848.

So the two forms of one word divided the plant between them. *Hemp* was rope, sailcloth and seed. *Cannabis* was medicine and then a drug. The law took over the split: the [1961 Single Convention](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Single_Convention_on_Narcotic_Drugs) controls "cannabis", and modern statutes carve out "hemp" below a set level of THC, as [Czechia](/history/czechia-hemp-one-percent-2022/) did in 2022.
