*Pot* is English slang for cannabis. It names the substance generally, rather than a particular variety, preparation or smoking implement. Unlike [joint](/culture/language/joint/) or [spliff](/culture/language/spliff/), it does not tell the listener how the cannabis has been prepared.

Its origin is less secure than its familiarity suggests. The often-repeated explanation connects it to Mexican Spanish *potiguaya*. Dictionaries present that connection cautiously; linguistic discussion has questioned whether the supposed Spanish source is adequately documented.

## Recorded in the 1930s

The [Online Etymology Dictionary](https://www.etymonline.com/word/pot) dates the cannabis sense to 1938 and calls the proposed shortening from *potiguaya* probable. This is a date for documented English usage, not proof that a particular speaker invented the word that year.

The ordinary English word for a container has a much older history. Its existence makes flowerpots, pipe bowls and teapots tempting explanations for the slang. A familiar object, however, is not evidence of an actual linguistic connection.

## A disputed borrowing

In a [LINGUIST List discussion](https://linguistlist.org/issues/16/2853/), linguist Max Wheeler investigated the Spanish explanation. He reported that respondents had not confirmed the use of *potiguaya*, *potaguaya* or *potación de guaya* in Spanish. The dictionary trail led to David W. Maurer's 1936 study of narcotics argot, where *potiguaya* was recorded as a term for crude marijuana leaves.

That supplies a written antecedent, but does not establish the proposed Spanish derivation. Wheeler also collected alternatives involving cannabis as “tea,” containers and pipe bowls. He concluded that the available evidence did not select a convincing winner. The frequently repeated story of cannabis steeped in wine should therefore be treated as an origin theory, not an established recipe behind the word.

## A word on a protest sign

The archival photograph of the [1965 demonstration](/history/us-ginsberg-cannabis-demonstration-1965/) shows Allen Ginsberg in snowy Greenwich Village, holding a placard declaring “Pot Is a Reality Kick.” Demonstrators outside the Women’s House of Detention on Greenwich Avenue demanded the release of people arrested for cannabis use or possession. Behind him, other participants’ signs urge people to smoke pot and repeal prohibition. The [Library of Congress record](https://www.loc.gov/pictures/item/99403586/) identifies the photograph in its New York World-Telegram and the Sun collection; the catalog dates the image to 10 January 1965. Related demonstrations occurred in December 1964, which can account for differing dates in reproductions.

Ginsberg became an early public advocate for cannabis legalisation. His participation moved the familiar word from private conversation onto a public demand, challenging the social stigma attached to users. The image documents the vocabulary of that movement, not the invention of the term. Pot’s recorded English use predates both this protest and the wider 1960s counterculture.

## Slang beside the statutory name

In the [United States](/country/us/), *pot* circulated alongside [marijuana](/culture/language/marijuana/) and [reefer](/culture/language/reefer/). Federal law chose *marihuana* for the [1937 tax act](/history/us-marihuana-tax-act-1937/); the [Controlled Substances Act of 1970](/history/us-controlled-substances-act-1970/) likewise regulated the plant under a formal legal definition.

Calling it pot did not create a different legal substance. The contrast is one of register: a short conversational name on one side, the vocabulary of statutes on the other. The surviving evidence supports that distinction more securely than any single account of where the slang began.
