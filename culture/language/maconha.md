*Maconha* is the Brazilian word for cannabis. It is African: from *ma'kaña* in Kimbundu, a language of Angola. The plant and its name came to Brazil with enslaved people, and for most of Brazilian history the laws against it were, in so many words, laws about them.

## The names

| Word | Origin | Note |
|---|---|---|
| Maconha | Kimbundu *ma'kaña* | The national word today |
| [Diamba](/culture/language/diamba/), liamba | Kimbundu | Still heard in the north-east; named in the decree of 1938 |
| Pango, *pito de pango* | — | The word of the first bans, for cannabis smoked in a pipe |
| Cânhamo | Spanish *cáñamo* | Hemp, the fibre plant |

The geographer Chris Duvall has pointed out that the earliest and most numerous cannabis users in the Americas were enslaved people from western Central Africa, and that their words for it survive wherever they were taken.

## Laws that named the slave

Cannabis is said to have been brought by the Portuguese, perhaps for fibre. But the people the Portuguese trafficked from Africa already knew the plant and smoked it.

| Year | Place | Measure |
|---|---|---|
| [1830](/history/brazil-rio-pango-ban-1830/) | Rio de Janeiro | The council banned *pito de pango*: a fine for the seller, up to three days in prison for any slave or other person using it |
| 1846 | Caxias | A similar directive |
| [1866, 1886](/history/brazil-sao-luis-directive-1886/) | São Luís | Sale, public display and smoking prohibited; slaves to be punished with four days in jail |
| 1870, 1876 | Santos, Campinas | Further municipal bans |

Whether any of these were enforced is unclear. Rio's is among the earliest cannabis laws anywhere.

## The revenge of the defeated

Slavery ended in 1888, and the association outlived it. In 1915 the psychiatrist [Rodrigues Dória](/history/brazil-doria-paper-1915/), a former governor of Sergipe, told a scientific congress in Washington that smoking maconha was a degenerate vice, the revenge of enslaved Africans on those who had enslaved them. His paper was the reference for Brazilian studies for decades.

The national ban followed in [1932](/history/brazil-federal-ban-1932/). The narcotics decree of [1938](/history/brazil-decree-law-891-1938/) named "maconha, diamba and liamba" and set five years for growing it. When the United Nations [surveyed Brazil in 1955](/history/brazil-un-survey-1955/), clandestine growing was centred on the north-east, along the São Francisco river, and smoking had spread from there to Rio and São Paulo. In the capital, cannabis accounted for twelve seizures for every one of any other narcotic.

Harry William Hutchinson’s 1975 chapter questions the evidence behind that reputation. He found that a 1958 government collection largely recycled older medical and police reports, while social research was scarce. His own work in Bahia did not substantiate claims that cannabis belonged generally to Afro-Brazilian religious ceremonies. The names *diamba*, *liamba* and *pango* document regional vocabulary; they do not establish a single custom shared by every community associated with them.

## A word you could be jailed for

Under the military dictatorship, maconha became linked with the counterculture and the left, which hardened official hostility. The offence of *apologia*, advocating a crime, was used against those who spoke up for it.

In 1997 the band Planet Hemp, whose songs were about maconha, was [arrested at a concert in Brasília](/history/brazil-planet-hemp-arrest-1997/) and held for five days. Judges banned the yearly [Marcha da Maconha](/culture/traditions/global-marijuana-march/) on the same ground. In June 2011 the Supreme Court ruled unanimously that marching for a change in the law is free expression and not incitement.

## Today

Since 2006 a user is no longer sent to prison, and in [2024 the Supreme Court](/history/brazil-supreme-court-2024/) ruled that possessing up to 40 grams or six female plants for one's own use is not a crime. It remains prohibited, and what is found is seized.

The old pattern has not gone. In Brazil, as an account of the marches notes, maconha was long stigmatised as the drug of the poor, the rural and the Afro-Brazilian. The word of the 1830 ordinances is now the one on the marchers' banners.
