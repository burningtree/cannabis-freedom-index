A *landrace* is a crop that has never been through a breeder's hands. It is a local population,
kept by farmers who save seed from their own fields year after year, until it has grown suited to
its place: its climate, its pests, its soil, and the uses its growers want from it. In cannabis
the word names the old populations of the regions that grew the plant for hashish, ganja or fibre
long before anyone sold seed in packets, and that are now, in many places, disappearing.

## A country breed

The word is German, *Landrasse*, literally "country breed". The first known reference to landraces
as a genetic resource was made at an agriculture and forestry congress in Vienna in 1890.
Kurt von Rümker defined the term in 1908; U. J. Mansholt described landraces in 1909 as more stable
and more resistant to hard conditions than bred cultivars, though less productive, and apt to change
when moved somewhere else. English took the word in the early 1930s, through a pig: the Danish
Landrace, a breed of lop-eared swine.

| Term | What it means |
|---|---|
| Landrace | A local population shaped by its place and its farmers, not by formal breeding |
| Cultivar | A variety bred to a standard and kept uniform |
| Farmers' variety | A local variety that has not been formally bred; it can include landraces |
| Autochthonous landrace | One grown in the same place for a long time |
| Allochthonous landrace | One introduced more recently |
| Accession | In seed banks, a sample recorded at one place and time |

## Mass selection

What makes a cannabis population a landrace, according to the conservation database
[Landrace.wiki](https://landrace.wiki/), is how its seed is kept. Farmers save seed in bulk from an
open-pollinated field rather than from a few chosen plants. The result is recognisably local, but
never uniform: it keeps some genetic diversity, gives a steady harvest in bad years and a moderate
one in good years, and usually carries a local name and a known use. In the Indian subcontinent,
for example, broad-leafed populations of the plant botanists call
[*Cannabis indica*](/culture/language/indica-and-sativa/) were grown for charas.

Some of the best-known commercial strains came from them: those sold as [*kush*](/culture/language/kush/)
were bred in California from the 1970s from landraces of the Hindu Kush, in Afghanistan and
Pakistan.

## Lost to hybrids

The same trade threatens them. Hybrids grown in a traditional region can cross with the local
plants or replace them outright, and once a population has been reduced to a handful of seeds, its
diversity goes quickly. Prohibition has done the rest: Landrace.wiki notes that eradication
campaigns hit the old growing regions again and again, and disrupted the passing on of growers'
knowledge. In Thailand, Cambodia and Laos it is unclear how much of the material described in a
survey of 1975 still survives.

Morocco is the clearest case. In the Rif the traditional [*kif*](/culture/language/kif/) plant was
largely replaced from the early 2000s by hybrids such as *khardala*, which researchers credited
with three to five times the resin. The old population was given a new name, *beldiya*: "local".

## Beldiya under licence

When Morocco [legalised growing for medicine and industry](/history/morocco-law-13-21-2021/) in
2021, the first legal crops were [grown from imported seed](/history/morocco-first-seeds-2023/).
From the beginning of 2024 the regulator also
[authorised beldiya](/history/morocco-beldiya-authorised-2024/), under a protocol with the food
safety office and after first results of a study characterising the local variety. By August 2024
it had given 106 cooperatives, with 1,816 farmers, permission to grow it on 1,916 hectares: a
landrace brought back as a licensed crop.

Landrace.wiki itself, published under an open licence, is an attempt to keep a record before
more is lost. In October 2026 its map showed 727 accessions across 15 growing regions, more than
a quarter of them rated a critical or high priority for conservation.
