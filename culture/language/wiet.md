*Wiet* is Dutch for herbal cannabis. The [Dutch government's terminology guide](https://www.rijksoverheid.nl/themas/familie-zorg-en-gezondheid/drugs/vraag-en-antwoord/experiment-gesloten-coffeeshopketen-wietexperiment/wat-is-het-verschil-tussen-cannabis-wiet-hennep-of-hasjiesj) distinguishes it from *hasj*, the resin product. The difference is useful in reading a coffeeshop menu, a news report or an account of the Netherlands' cannabis trade: not every product from the plant is wiet.

## An English loan in Dutch spelling

The [Dutch dictionary entry](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/wiet) derives the word from English *weed* in its cannabis sense. The spelling and pronunciation fit Dutch usage. The sources read for this entry do not establish a first speaker or a secure first-use date, so the borrowing should not be assigned a precise origin story.

Related compounds include *nederwiet*, *mediwiet*, *wiethandel*, *wietpas* and *wietsigaret*. They show how the borrowed word became productive in Dutch, appearing in vocabulary about origin, medicine, trade, access and smoking rather than remaining an isolated foreign expression.

## Product and plant

The government's distinction is between a plant and products obtained from it. *Hennep* and *cannabis* name the plant; *wiet* or *marihuana* refers to dried flower tops and leaves. *Hasj* or *hasjiesj* comes from resin associated with the flowers, compressed into a product of its own.

This also explains why *hennep* in Dutch legal writing should not automatically be translated as the narrow modern English category [industrial hemp](/culture/language/hemp/). Similar-looking words can have different ranges. The relevant statute's definition matters more than a retail label.

## Reading a menu

Romain’s photograph in the [Siberië menu archive](https://www.coffeeshopmenus.org/Siberie/Menus/Siberie.html), dated 16 July 2013, shows *WIET* and *HASJ* as separate categories. Under wiet, *buitenwiet* distinguishes outdoor-grown cannabis from *binnenwiet*, grown indoors. The hashish section includes *nederhasj*, a corresponding label for Dutch-produced resin. The photograph preserves the spelling, prices and categories used by one Amsterdam coffeeshop at that time.

*Nederwiet* means Dutch-grown herbal cannabis; it describes origin rather than a single variety. *Mediwiet* refers to cannabis used medicinally, while *wietpas* means “weed pass,” the access scheme associated with coffeeshop membership and restrictions on non-residents. These compounds answer different questions: where the plant grew, why it is used, or who may buy it. They should not all be read as names for products on a menu.

## A familiar name in an exceptional market

The [Netherlands](/country/nl/) separated cannabis from hard drugs in its [1976 toleration policy](/history/netherlands-toleration-1976/). Coffeeshops emerged during the 1970s. That policy is central to the [coffeeshop](/culture/traditions/the-coffeeshop/) as a social institution. The government nevertheless notes that the Opium Act itself uses *hennep* and *cannabis*, not *wiet* and *marihuana*.

In [2025](/history/netherlands-regulated-supply-only-2025/), the regulated-supply experiment entered its experimental phase in ten municipalities, with requirements for regulated cannabis supply and a longer transition for hashish. The reform concerns supply arrangements in specified places, not a new botanical meaning for the word. Wiet remains the everyday product name across discussions of both the tolerated market and its regulated experiment.

The *wietpas* vocabulary belongs to access restrictions, especially the [2012 introduction of membership and resident criteria](/history/netherlands-residents-only-2012/). It should not be mistaken for a nationwide declaration that cannabis was legal. A menu describes a retail offer; the toleration policy describes when authorities refrain from prosecuting otherwise prohibited sales.
