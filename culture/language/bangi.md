*Bangi* is the Swahili word for cannabis, and in the English of Kenya and Tanzania the plant is still called *bhang*. The word is Indian. It crossed the ocean with the trade of the Swahili coast, and on the way it changed its meaning: in India [bhang](/culture/language/bhang/) is a leaf that is eaten, and in East Africa it became a plant that is smoked.

## From Hindi to Swahili

Cannabis may have reached the Swahili coast by about the year 1000, presumably with traders who spoke Hindi, Persian or Arabic. The same word exists in all three worlds: *bhang* in Hindi, *bang* in Persian.

| Form | Where |
|---|---|
| Bangi | Swahili: [Tanzania](/country/tz/), [Kenya](/country/ke/), and beyond |
| Bhang, banghi | Kenyan and Tanzanian English, including official usage |
| Bangi | Juba, [South Sudan](/country/ss/), where the cannabis [seized in a raid of 2026](/history/south-sudan-juba-raids-2026/) was reported under that name |

From the coast the plant went inland. It may have been Swahili traders from Zanzibar who carried it to the Congo basin in the 1850s, where within a generation it became the centre of the [Bena Riamba](/culture/traditions/bena-riamba/) movement.

## A trade before the colonies

By the nineteenth century cannabis was part of the folk medicine of many East African peoples, and a long-distance trade had grown up around it. Cannabis grown by the Ngoni was carried to the port of Kilwa. Under German rule, small Nyamwezi traders took it from Unyamwezi, in the interior, to the coast and to Zanzibar. The few accounts from the period suggest that in East Africa it was mainly smoked.

## The colonial bans

As the trade grew, the European administrations moved against it, seeing the habit as an obstacle to their purposes.

| Year | Measure |
|---|---|
| [1908](/history/kenya-commissioner-asks-for-ban-1908/) | John Ainsworth, provincial commissioner at Kisumu, asked for a law, writing that cannabis had a most harmful effect on those who used it and caused crime |
| [1914](/history/kenya-ban-1914/) | The East Africa Protectorate banned it under the Opium Ordinance, in force from 1 January |
| [1935](/history/tanganyika-dangerous-drugs-ordinance-1935/) | Tanganyika's Dangerous Drugs Ordinance |

## Bangi today

Both countries still grow it widely. Kenya's crop is centred on Mount Kenya and the shore of Lake Victoria; Tanzania's on Tanga, Arusha, Kilimanjaro, Iringa and the lake zone. The United Nations counts both among the main producers of East Africa.

Older uses persist beside the trade. In rural Tanzania the plant treats earache, fever and malaria, and the leaves go into food. In Kisumu some traditional healers boil the buds and bathe children who have measles in the water.

| Country | Present law |
|---|---|
| Kenya | The Narcotic Drugs and Psychotropic Substances (Control) Act, 1994. An [amendment of 2022](/history/kenya-amendment-act-2022/) halved the maximum for personal use to five years and allowed a fine |
| Tanzania | The [Drug Control and Enforcement Act](/history/tanzania-drug-control-act-2015/): a fine or up to three years for small amounts, at least thirty years for growing |

In both countries the borrowed word is the everyday one. A petition by Kenyan Rastafari to be allowed it for worship was [dismissed by the High Court in 2026](/history/kenya-high-court-rastafari-petition-2026/).
