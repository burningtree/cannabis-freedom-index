*Cachimbo* is the Portuguese word for a smoking pipe. It does not specify cannabis: the Priberam dictionary's basic definition describes a tobacco bowl and a tube through which smoke is drawn. *Cachimbo de água* names a water pipe more specifically. Understanding that difference prevents the appearance of the word in an old document from becoming automatic evidence of cannabis use.

The word also carries a proposed African history. Chris S. Duvall connects it with pipe vocabulary from the lower Zambezi Valley, in and around present-day Mozambique. His account follows a technology and its name through Portuguese maritime networks into Brazil and other parts of the Atlantic world.

## A proposed Zambezi connection

Duvall points to regional words resembling Chichewa *kachimbo*, meaning a dry pipe. He suggests that Portuguese sailors learned the word, and perhaps the practice, through encounters in southeastern Africa. Portuguese travellers had penetrated inland along the Zambezi by the early sixteenth century.

That reconstruction is a linguistic and historical argument. It does not identify a recorded first speaker or an exact borrowing date. Duvall notes that some early Portuguese travellers left little written testimony, limiting what can be established about the encounter itself.

The distinction matters because a plausible route through maritime contact can easily harden into a confident origin story. The contemporary Priberam entry labels the etymology doubtful. Duvall's African derivation should therefore remain an attributed proposal, rather than a settled dictionary fact.

## Across the Atlantic

In Duvall's account, the Portuguese term circulated with sailors and entered other European languages. It also passed into languages of the Americas, including forms recorded in Brazil and in Mayan languages of Mexico and Guatemala. Some borrowings may have travelled through Spanish rather than directly from Portuguese.

Brazil is important to this story because Portuguese became a language through which African words could travel again. A word's presence in an Indigenous American language does not, by itself, make that language its original source. Establishing direction requires comparison with historical contacts and other forms of the word.

This differs from tracing a plant alone. Pipes, smoking practices, names and the substances put into a bowl could travel together or take different paths.

## A dry pipe and a water pipe

Duvall distinguishes the relatively small dry pipes associated with *cachimbo* from the [calabash water pipe](/culture/traditions/the-calabash-water-pipe/). A dry pipe carries smoke through a stem without a water chamber. A water pipe adds a container, which might be made from a gourd, horn or another material.

In the Vili vocabulary he discusses for northern Angola, *timba*, derived from *cachimbo*, named a dry pipe, while *nkondo* named a calabash water pipe. A recorded proverb assigned tobacco to the former and cannabis to the latter.

That local contrast helps read historical accounts carefully. It does not mean every Portuguese *cachimbo* was for tobacco or every African water pipe was for cannabis.

## The word and its contents

Portuguese speakers in Portugal, Mozambique, Angola and Brazil can use the same general noun without describing identical objects or habits. The modifier, setting and named substance provide the necessary context.

For cannabis history, the useful evidence is therefore more precise than a pipe-shaped object or the noun alone: an identified vessel, a contemporary description of its contents, or a source that explicitly connects its use with the plant.
