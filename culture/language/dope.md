*Dope* can mean cannabis, but it can also mean other drugs. The [Oxford Advanced Learner's Dictionary](https://www.oxfordlearnersdictionaries.com/us/definition/english/dope_1) notes that the cannabis meaning is usual in British English. An American source using the same word may be describing something else. Identifying the substance from the slang alone can therefore mislead. The dictionary also records drug use in sport and the unrelated expression for inside information. A headline, conversation or police account needs its surrounding details: the word’s presence alone does not establish what was taken.

## From sauce to a preparation

The [Online Etymology Dictionary](https://www.etymonline.com/word/dope) records the word in American English in 1807 for sauce, gravy or a thick liquid. It came from Dutch *doop*, a dipping sauce. By the late nineteenth century it could describe a mixture whose ingredients were unknown.

The narcotic sense is recorded by 1889 and is linked to preparations of opium used for smoking. Cannabis inherited a word already associated with other substances; it was not the plant that originally gave dope its drug meaning.

## A label for a person

Dope also became entangled with judgments about intelligence and behaviour. The meaning “foolish person” is recorded before the narcotic sense. Later usage could associate the insult with drug-induced stupor. The compound *dope fiend*, recorded by 1896 for a person with an opium habit, compressed a substance and a hostile description of its user into one phrase.

That history makes the word useful when reading older anti-drug writing. It may describe a drug, express contempt for a person, or do both. It does not reliably identify dependence, a diagnosis or a particular pattern of cannabis use.

## Dope Street and the tabloid scare

Duncan Webb’s “London’s Drug Fiends Exposed: Dope Street” appeared in *The People* on 20 June 1954, page 3. The clipping, reproduced in [Alex Brown’s account for *Points*](https://www.pointshistory.org/post/cannabis-in-the-1950s-british-tabloids), places the enormous word *dope* above an investigation of Archer Street and London’s jazz scene. Its text discusses marijuana, showing cannabis beneath a headline that could otherwise describe several drugs.

Brown connects such reporting to the anxieties surrounding post-war immigration and racially mixed jazz clubs. Tabloids associated cannabis with criminality, moral decline and the supposed corruption of young white women by Black men. These were the papers’ racial and sexual narratives, not neutral evidence about cannabis users or musicians.

The clipping makes the label’s work visible. A familiar street and musical community become a threatening drug landscape through the headline and the language around it. Reading the artifact means attending to that framing as well as identifying the substance. Its typography records the scare; it does not validate the allegations or make *dope* an exclusively cannabis word.

## A broad word beside precise names

For [Britain](/country/gb/) and the [United States](/country/us/), the same English word has overlapping but different everyday meanings. Even within one country, context matters. [Hashish](/culture/language/hashish/), [ganja](/culture/language/ganja/) and [hemp](/culture/language/hemp/) preserve distinctions that the broad label can erase.

American law's [1970 Controlled Substances Act](/history/us-controlled-substances-act-1970/) classified named substances, including marijuana, rather than creating one legal category called dope. When the word appears in a historical account, the next question is what that particular writer meant by it.
