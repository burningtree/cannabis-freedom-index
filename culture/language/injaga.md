*Injaga* is a name for cannabis recorded in Rwanda by anthropologist Helen Codere during research in 1959–60. Her chapter in *Cannabis and Culture* (1975) documents the word through comments on a photograph of a man holding a gourd water pipe.

The record is valuable for language and social history, but it requires careful reading. Codere’s original project concerned political attitudes during a period of upheaval. It was not designed as a national survey of cannabis consumption, and its respondents’ statements cannot simply be turned into prevalence figures.

## A plant and a pipe

In the chapter, *injaga* names cannabis. Codere separately calls the gourd water pipe *urumogi*. That distinction describes the vocabulary in this particular historical account; it should not be assumed to settle the meanings of either word in every later source or present-day conversation.

Respondents described plants concealed among tall crops, dried material and several ways of smoking. The photograph’s pipe acted as a recognisable clue for some viewers. Others did not recognise it or chose to discuss something else in the image.

Codere does not provide an etymology or establish when *injaga* first entered Kinyarwanda. Her evidence securely documents use of the term during her fieldwork, rather than the word’s invention or earliest possible appearance.

## What the questionnaire could establish

The study involved 252 respondents, including adults and children. A photograph of a Twa man with the pipe prompted comments about cannabis from 113 of them. Participants were invited to say what they wished about the pictures, rather than being asked a standard sequence of questions about their own drug use.

Most associated *injaga* smoking with Twa men. Some also mentioned Twa women, smokers from other communities and medicinal uses. Those qualifications are important: even the chapter’s own evidence does not support an absolute statement that only Twa people used cannabis.

The material is especially informative about how people identified a smoker and explained their opinions. It is much less secure as a measure of who consumed the plant or how frequently they did so.

## The social meaning attached to the name

The responses contain claims about strength, courage, irrationality and violence. Many are hostile. Some respondents presented themselves as witnesses; others repeated general beliefs about a socially marginalised community.

Codere herself notes that accounts of internal effects could be hearsay or repetitions of stereotypes. Her wider interpretation nevertheless uses strongly generalising social categories and language characteristic of its period. Neither the respondents’ accusations nor the author’s terminology should be adopted as neutral descriptions of Twa people.

The word consequently offers evidence for stigma as well as vocabulary. Cannabis was being discussed through existing social divisions, and favourable attitudes toward familiar alcoholic drinks could coexist with condemnation of *injaga*.

## Historical vocabulary and later law

This account predates Rwanda’s later [penal legislation](/history/rwanda-penal-law-2018/) and [medical-cannabis framework](/history/rwanda-medical-2021/). Those records concern legal categories and licensing; the photo-questionnaire concerns language, recognition and attitudes in 1959–60.

Keeping the two kinds of evidence distinct prevents a historical name from becoming a claim about present legal permission. It also preserves what makes *injaga* worth recording: a locally documented word whose social meaning extended beyond the botanical plant it named.
