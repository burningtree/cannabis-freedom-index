*Reefer* is American slang for a cannabis cigarette, or for cannabis itself. It belongs to the vocabulary of the jazz musicians of the 1920s and 1930s, along with *muggles*, *tea*, *viper* and *mezz*. It is remembered now partly because of one film. Made in 1936 to frighten parents, *Reefer Madness* became, forty years later, a joke that cannabis users told against the people who had banned it.

## Jazz slang

The words come from the jazz world between the wars, before cannabis was a federal crime.

| Word | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Reefer | A cannabis cigarette; also cannabis in general |
| Muggles | Cannabis. Louis Armstrong recorded a tune called "Muggles" in 1928 |
| Tea | Cannabis; a "tea pad" was a room where it was smoked |
| Viper | A smoker of it, as in the Stuff Smith song "If You're a Viper" |
| Mezz | Cannabis, after Mezz Mezzrow |

The last is a person. [Mezz Mezzrow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mezz_Mezzrow), born Milton Mesirow in Chicago, was a clarinettist and saxophonist who organised recording sessions for Sidney Bechet and Tommy Ladnier. He was at least as well known in the jazz world as a supplier of cannabis, so well known that his nickname became a word for it. He was called the Muggles King, and Louis Armstrong was one of his best customers; a letter Armstrong wrote from England in 1932 tells Mezzrow where and how to send it.

In 1940 Mezzrow was arrested with sixty joints at the entrance to a jazz club at the New York World's Fair. In jail he insisted that he was Black, and was moved to the Black section of the segregated prison. He told the story in his memoir *Really the Blues* in 1946, and spent the last twenty years of his life in France.

## The law arrives

American states had begun restricting cannabis before the jazz age, [Massachusetts first in 1911](/history/us-massachusetts-first-state-1911/), with the western states following from [El Paso's ordinance of 1915](/history/us-el-paso-ban-1915/). Federal prohibition came with the [Marihuana Tax Act](/history/us-marihuana-tax-act-1937/) of 1937, whose first convictions were [handed down in Denver](/history/us-first-tax-act-convictions-1937/) within a week. The officials preferred the Spanish word, [marihuana](/culture/language/marijuana/); the users went on saying reefer.

## Reefer Madness

The film began as *Tell Your Children*, produced in 1936 by George Hirliman and paid for by a church group as a warning to parents. It tells of high-school students drawn by dealers into smoking, and of the manslaughter, suicide and madness that follow; it is framed as a lecture by a school principal to a meeting of parents, and ends with the words TELL YOUR CHILDREN on the screen.

In 1938 the exploitation producer Dwain Esper bought it, added salacious shots, and released it under several titles, one of them *Reefer Madness*. It was one of a cluster of such films in the years of the Tax Act, beside Esper's own *Marihuana* and *Assassin of Youth*.

| Year | What happened to the film |
|---|---|
| 1936 | Made as *Tell Your Children*, for a church group |
| 1938–1940 | Recut and sold on the exploitation circuit under several titles |
| — | Its copyright notice was defective, so it fell into the public domain |
| 1972 | Keith Stroup, founder of NORML, found a print in the Library of Congress and bought a copy for 297 dollars |
| 1970s | Shown on college campuses to raise money for reform, and as a midnight movie |

The film found a new life as an unintended satire among advocates of reform, and a cult following within cannabis culture. Critics have called it one of the worst films ever made.

## The word now

*Reefer* is the word of the old songs and of the film. The official view of that time was answered within a decade: New York's [La Guardia report of 1944](/history/us-la-guardia-report-1944/) found that marijuana led neither to addiction nor to crime. It had no effect on the law.
