*Ols* (олс, traditional Mongolian ᠣᠯᠤᠰᠤ) refers to hemp and also to rope or cordage. The two meanings appear together in the [Mongolian Great Explanatory Dictionary](https://mongoltoli.mn/dictionary/detail/67735), which records the word in Cyrillic and traditional Mongolian script, with *olusu* as its transliteration of the latter.

This is a material vocabulary: the plant, its seed oil, the thread and the finished cord. Its meanings follow hemp through processing into useful goods. The entry does not establish the word’s earliest written occurrence or provide a secure account of its deeper etymology.

## A plant and the things made from it

The dictionary’s botanical meaning describes a tall herb whose seeds yield oil. It also records roots, leaves and flowers as ingredients in medicinal preparations. That lexical description documents a use associated with the word; it is not clinical evidence for the safety or effectiveness of a treatment.

Its second meaning moves from the living plant to cordage. This connection also appears in English *hemp*, where a plant name can identify a fibre and goods made from it. In both cases, the material gives its name to the object.

## The vocabulary of work

Several compounds make the plant’s practical associations explicit:

| Mongolian term | Meaning recorded in the dictionary |
|---|---|
| *олсны тос* | Oil extracted from hemp seeds |
| *олсон аргамж* | A long hemp rope used to tether horses or other livestock |
| *олсон нэхмэл* | Fabric woven from hemp thread |
| *олсон шаа* | Cloth woven from a cotton-and-hemp mixture |
| *олс татлага* | Rope or a binding cord |

The compounds preserve a connection between plant processing, woven material and the pastoral work of securing horses and other livestock. They name both useful materials and the objects made from them.

Lena Tidemand’s 1928 photograph in the [National Museum of Denmark](https://samlinger.natmus.dk/es/asset/354573) shows two women working with rope outdoors. The museum describes the rope as probably made from horsehair and suggests a wheel-based twisting method. It documents traditional Mongolian craftsmanship, rather than hemp processing specifically. The rope meaning of *ols* makes that craft relevant without establishing the photographed material as hemp.

## Strength in the twist

The dictionary records a proverb connecting the strength of twisted rope with mutual support. In the compact form:

> Олсны хүч эрчиндээ, олны хүч дэмэндээ байдаг.

Its meaning is: “A rope's strength lies in its twist; people's strength lies in helping one another.” The dictionary records a slightly longer form, repeating *байдаг* in both halves.

The comparison brings a useful plant, craftsmanship and social life into the same vocabulary. Separate strands form a strong cord; people support one another through cooperation. The image does not depend on intoxication. Rope-making supplies the practical observation through which an agricultural word becomes a metaphor for collective strength.

## An everyday word beside controlled-substance law

The [2002 control law](/history/mongolia-narcotics-law-2002/) defines narcotic drugs through international conventions and regulates activities involving controlled plants. The [Criminal Code](/history/mongolia-criminal-code-2015/) separately addresses unauthorised preparation, storage, acquisition, supply and cultivation.

Those legal classifications cannot be inferred from the dictionary’s rope meaning, and the ordinary word supplies no exemption for recreational cannabis. Equally, the existence of cannabis prohibition should not erase the recorded meanings involving seed oil, fibre and textiles.

In [Mongolia today](/country/mn/), the documented word therefore connects two different kinds of source: a dictionary preserving material and figurative meanings, and legislation determining which activities involving controlled substances are punishable. A single familiar word reaches from the plant to the tether and from the tether to an image of collective strength.
