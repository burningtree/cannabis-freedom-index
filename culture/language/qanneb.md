*Qanneb* is the Maltese word for hemp. For most of Malta's history it named a working plant, the fibre
in the rope, sailcloth and sacks of a seafaring island, and its seed, *qannebusa*, was bird food. Only in
the twentieth century did the word enter the criminal law, as *qanneb Indjan*, Indian hemp, the term the
Maltese text of the drugs ordinance used for cannabis until 2021.

The Maltese writer Martin Morana, who has traced the word's history, found no record of hemp being used
in Malta as a drug or a medicine before modern times.

## The word

*Qanneb* is the plural of *qanba*. Morana derives it from Arabic,
and ultimately from the Persian *kanab*, the same root as *cannabis*. The eighteenth-century Maltese
scholar Gianfrangesku Agius de Soldanis (1712–1770) entered it in his dictionary, *Damma*, glossing it
with the Italian *cannabo* and *barbella* and the Latin *cannabis*.

From the same root comes *qannebusa*, hemp seed. Maltese bird keepers still buy it, and some feed it to
greenfinches in the belief that it makes them sing more.

## Rope for the galleys

Under the Knights of the Order of St John, who ruled Malta from 1530 to 1798, hemp was used, in Morana's
word, profusely. Documents studied by the historians J. Muscat and Godfrey Wettinger show that the Order
bought it mainly through Venice, whose merchants sourced it from Ferrara, Bologna and Ancona and as far
away as Ukraine and Latvia. It rigged the Order's ships, and it was woven into cloth for clothing, tents
and awnings. One contractor, Vincenzo Gellel, set slaves to work on it and was paid three *tari* for
each *quintale* of uncarded hemp.

In 1841 the French consul in Malta, Dominique Miège,
[wrote](/history/malta-miege-hemp-rope-1841/) that hemp grew wild on the islands but was imported in
great quantities for rope, and put the output at up to a thousand tons a year. The trade clustered in
the harbour towns.

In 1830 rope makers were also leasing a long hall of the former Sacra Infermeria, the Order's hospital
in Valletta. Hemp sacks carried potatoes in and out of the islands, and caulkers still press hemp into
the seams of wooden boats before sealing them with tar.

## In speech

The plant left traces in the language. A proverb recorded by Erin Serracino Inglott, *fejn bidt mur kul
il-qanneb*, roughly "go and eat hemp wherever you were staying", tells someone to go back where they
belong. A
hemp poultice, *il-ġbara tal-qanneb*, was a grim euphemism for the hangman's noose. In 1838 the writer
George Percy Badger recorded a humorous verse about hemp seed in Maltese nougat.

## In the law

Malta's [Dangerous Drugs Ordinance](/history/malta-dangerous-drugs-1939/) of 1939 called the drug plant
Indian hemp, in Maltese *qanneb Indjan*. The hashish that Tunisia
[imported from Malta](/history/malta-hashish-to-tunisia-1895/) in the 1890s, according to the
geographer Chris Duvall, went by yet another name there, *shira*, from Syrian Arabic.

When Malta [legalised](/history/malta-legalisation-2021/) possession and home growing in 2021, the
reform minister, Owen Bonnici, said CBD products would be legalised by updating the legal definition of
Indian hemp. The definition was replaced by one for cannabis, *kannabis* in the Maltese text. It excludes
the seeds, and products of non-psychoactive cannabinoids with no more than 0.2 per cent THC. Police and prosecutors have
since maintained in court that CBD flower is itself cannabis.
