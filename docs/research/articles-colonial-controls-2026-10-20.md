# Articles: three early colonial cannabis controls

Added articles for three existing, highly ranked backlog entries:

- `nigeria-dangerous-drugs-ordinance-1927`: 382 words, distinguishing the
  treaty-driven ordinance from later policing campaigns and official claims
  about the absence of a drug problem.
- `kenya-ban-1914`: 409 words, distinguishing the Hague opium framework from
  a cannabis treaty obligation, and the 1908 request from the later restriction.
- `guyana-licensing-1861`: 414 words, describing licensed sales without
  expanding that evidence into a blanket possession or consumption ban.

## Sources actually examined

Used the local EPUB of *Cannabis: Global Histories*, edited by Lucas Richert
and James H. Mills (MIT Press, 2021). The title and copyright-page extraction
identifies paperback ISBN 9780262045209; no separate ebook ISBN is asserted.
Public citations identify chapter sections rather than an unverified mapping
from EPUB locations to print pages.

Read the relevant extracts and checked the central legal passages against the
original EPUB XHTML:

- Gernot Klantschnig, chapter 10, “Cannabis Use in West Africa Before 1960,”
  especially the 1927/1935 ordinances, international templates, official
  reporting and subsequent policing.
- Neil Carrier, chapter 14, “Quasilegality and Cannabis in Africa,” especially
  the 1914 ordinance, the Hague convention, Ainsworth's request and notes 16–18.
- Jamie Banks, chapter 3, introduction and “Dr. Grieve + Dr. Seccombe = ??,”
  especially the 1861 licensing discussion and notes 52–55. The interpretation
  of the ordinance as anticipatory is attributed through Banks to Hanoomansingh.

This was a targeted rereading, not a new claim to have read all three chapters
or their underlying archives. The existing research notes document earlier
whole-chapter reading of Klantschnig and Carrier.

The original Nigerian ordinance, Kenyan gazette notices, Guianese ordinance,
archival correspondence and Hanoomansingh dissertation were not independently
examined in this pass. The articles do not infer new offence details, penalties,
prevalence estimates or enforcement rates from those unexamined sources.
Colonial medical and criminality claims are attributed rather than adopted.

Web searches supplied additional leads. Google Books retrieval of the Kenyan
gazette and retrieval of Banks's later LSHTM-hosted article failed in the web
reader. Neither was added as an examined source. Searches about Soviet and
Thai controls were not used for these articles. No claims were taken solely
from search summaries.
