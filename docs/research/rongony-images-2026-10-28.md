fibre plant and its etymology, the regional names for the plant and the smoked tops, the
proverb tradition, the Merina ban stories, the 1915 and 1960 statutes, and the 1997 switch to

## The 1915 decree image

The PDF is an OCR'd scan of a bound legal collection. Page 8 (printed p. 13) has two columns;
the decree begins in the right-hand column. The page was rendered at 400 dpi with
`pdftoppm`, and the decree was cropped at x 4210–5640, y 460–1210 (1430 × 750). The crop
contains the date line, the full title, Article 1, and Article 2's penalties.

To keep *Rongony* legible on a phone, the published asset is a two-part image:

- top: the full crop, 1430 × 750;
- bottom: a 1.77× enlargement of the Article 1 line "chanvre à fumer dit Rongony sont
  interdits dans" (source crop 835 × 34 at x 4485, y 920), 1430 × 58;
- a 30-pixel light band and a hairline separate the two parts.

The caption says "Top: the decree as printed; bottom: a magnified detail of Article 1", so the
composite is not presented as a single uninterrupted page. No text was retouched, erased or
retypeset. At a 390-pixel display width the detail line remains readable; the full crop gives
the title and Article 1 in context.

The collection's own citation line under the title reads "(Journ. off., 15 oct. 1915.)". The
UN memorandum E/NL.1962/8 records the local promulgation by *arrêté* of 3 December 1915
(*J.O.M.* of 11 December 1915, p. 1222). The entry therefore gives the decree's date (9 October
1915) and does not assert a *Journal officiel* date that the sources describe differently.

## The GI-TOC photograph

The Risk Bulletin page carries five photographs. `ambanja.jpg` is a three-part composite of a
gendarmerie destruction operation, not the bottle. The bottle is `cannabis-oil-2.jpg`: three
improvised glass bottles fitted with long plastic tubes, standing on a wooden surface. The
page's caption is "A bottle used to inhale cannabis oil, Analabe Ambanja, February 2021.
Photo: Riana Raymonde Randrianarisoa." The adjacent photograph shows a resident demonstrating
the inhalation.

The published file is 810 × 1080, not the 1280 width advertised in its `srcset`. It carries a
visible moiré pattern, probably from photographing a screen; this is in the source file and was
not introduced here. The crop removes the upper background and lower foreground but keeps all
three bottles and the full tubes. The caption says "Bottles" rather than the source's singular
"A bottle" because three are visible.

The article uses the photograph to ground the word in a documented contemporary practice, not
to illustrate the market statistics. The GI-TOC text is cited for the production geography and
exports in one sentence; the photograph's caption carries the photographer and date.

## The proverb typesetting

The card is an editorial design, not a scanned document or a manuscript imitation. It uses the
site's own display face (Instrument Serif italic) and Inter, a deep green ground with a faint
woven grid, and the text:

> Manan-karena salasala, ka manao kitamby rongony.
>
> Neither rich nor poor: one wears a hemp wrap.

The Malagasy text and the French translation are from *Le Livre de la Sagesse Malgache*
(Veyrières and Méritens, 1967), no. 2439, as transcribed by Malagasy Word. Houlder's 1960
collection has the same proverb and explains that a hemp garment marked the middle condition,
between rich and poor; that note is the basis for the article's "middle condition" sentence.
The English on the card is a translation of the French, not a direct translation from Malagasy.
The card states the source and "Not a historical document" in its footer.

The other proverb quoted in the article, *Aza mantsim-bao miray, hoatry ny rongony*, is
translated from the French "N'attendez pas de sentir mauvais avant de vous unir, comme le
chanvre au rouissage", with the collector's note that it was said of accomplices. The article
uses it for the retting detail, not as a translation of a lost original.

## Historical and linguistic checks

- **Etymology.** The *Dictionnaire des noms malgaches de végétaux* (Boiteau, 1999) marks
  *rongony* as Merina and Betsileo and gives "(de rongo : ce que l'on tisse ; les fibres
  textiles)". The *rongo* entry gives cloth edging, a Tanala hairstyle, and a Sakalava sense of
  textile fibres (also a fibre-bearing tree). The entry offers no older written form, sound
  change or cognate. The article therefore calls it the best proposal found, not an established
  derivation.
- **Regional names.** *Angafo* is the Merina name for the upper leaves and inflorescences
  prepared for smoking, with the verb *manangafo*. *Jiajia* is Sakalava and Tankarana, especially
  young leaves and flower tops; the dictionary notes west-coast villages called Anjiajia and
  says hemp grown for fibre is called *rongony*. *Jamala* is Bara and Tanosy, and also
  Betsimisaraka and Taisaka; its general sense is impatience. *Maosy* is a Betsimisaraka familiar
  name for the tops; *katrinia* is leaves and seeds mixed with sugar and smoked. The article
  keeps these regional labels and does not present the names as interchangeable.
- **Merina ban.** The 1787 date is conventional and uncertain. The proverb collection's note on
  *Fon' ny mpifoka rongony* is a retrospective explanation published in 1967, not a contemporary
  record; it says the king made smoking a capital offence and that the law was soon abandoned.
  Duvall says Merina royalty had forbidden the plant drug by 1870 and cites Sibree (1870, 1880)
  and Baron (1878); his note 40 says the decree allowed fibre production. The botanical
  dictionary attributes the ban and a cultivation prohibition to Prime Minister
  Rainilaiarivony. The article gives these accounts separately and does not choose a single
  date or author.
- **1915 decree.** Read from the scan: title *Décret prohibant à Madagascar et dépendances la
  préparation, la vente et la consommation du chanvre à fumer dit Rongony*; Article 1 prohibits
  preparation, peddling, sale and consumption of *chanvre à fumer dit Rongony* throughout the
  colony; Article 2 sets 100–3,000 francs and six days to twelve months, or one of the two.
  The decree targets smoking hemp, not fibre as such.
- **1960 ordinance.** Read from the UN memorandum: Ordinance 60-073 of 28 July 1960,
  "relative à la répression du trafic de chanvre /cannabis/ dit rongony"; Article 1 bans
  cultivation, Article 2 bans preparation, transport, sale, possession and consumption, and
  Article 3 sets six months to five years and 10,000–1,000,000 francs, or one of the two.
  Article 5 repeals the 1915 decree and the 1958 deliberation.
- **1997 law.** Law 97-039, Article 8, bans cultivation of "la plante de cannabis"; Article 148
  repeals the 1960 ordinance and calls it "relative à la répression de la consommation du
  chanvre indien dit rongony", although the 1960 title was about *trafic*. The article notes
  both the switch to *cannabis* and the survival of *rongony* in the repeal clause.
- **RISD thesis.** The abstract and the RISD Grad Show statement were read. The thesis is a
  Master of Architecture project about hemp-clay building materials, not a history of the word.
  Its claim that hemp and marijuana are both called *rongony* and that this creates stigma is
  presented as the author's argument, alongside the dictionary evidence for regional
  distinctions. The entry does not treat the thesis as a historical authority.
