## Primary legal evidence and exact comparisons

Read the [NACD compilation, third edition, 2007](https://niph.org.kh/niph/uploads/library/pdf/LD208_Law_on_Drug_Control.pdf),
particularly English articles 90–98, printed pp. 48–51 (PDF pp. 137–140). Footnotes identify
which clauses changed through the law promulgated on **9 May 2005**. Visually inspected PDF
pp. 139–140. The event date is the promulgation date, not a separately certified Gazette
publication or commencement date. The compilation is not the unamended 1996 text.

Compared amended article 98 directly with the [original unofficial English translation](https://cdc.gov.kh/wp-content/uploads/2022/04/Law-on-Drug-Management_full-text_961209.pdf),
PDF pp. 47–48. Original: six days–one month, 100,000–1,000,000 riel, **or either penalty**.
Amended: seven days–one month, with a **possible additional** 25,000–100,000 riel fine.
The ordinary prison wording became stricter while the fine range fell. `other` reflects this
mixed replacement, with sentencing, traditional and treatment tags. Repeat offending or
health-professional status doubles the ordinary amended penalties. The very-small-quantity,
non-addictive ancestral village-use discretion **remained**; it was not abolished in 2005.
Articles 90–93 retain voluntary presentation before prosecution and treatment-related court
options; they are not described as wholly new reforms.

Downloaded/extracted the [2012 English law](https://www.sithi.org/medias/files/projects/sithi/law/Drug-Control-Law-2012-Final-English-Version.pdf).
Read articles 10–14, 38–45, 100–110, 124–125 and the cannabis entry in Table I. Visually checked
printed pp. 19 and 27. Cannabis, resin and oil are in Table I. Article 38: ordinary unlawful
cultivation **six months–two years**, or **two–five years for distribution, manufacture or
trade**; it also contains the limited habitual-consumption discretion. Article 40: ordinary
unlawful keeping, transport and trafficking **two–five years**, with higher penalties for
specified circumstances/quantities. No threshold amount is presented as permission to possess.
The English text has anomalies in some higher quantity tiers; these are not silently repaired
or used for tourist advice. The final wording on commercial cultivation was corrected against
the page image before the final build.

Article 45's English wording links the ordinary consumption offence to a person who has
already accepted compulsory treatment. Article 105 also addresses treatment advice before
prosecution, refusal and the prosecutor's powers. The earlier summary's simple claim that
users are offered treatment instead of up to six months was too broad. Revised public wording
describes the procedures without declaring an unconditional treatment-for-prison swap.

Articles 100–104 address state support, harm reduction, voluntary consent and withdrawal.
Articles 107–109 set medical conditions, decision-making and appeals for compulsory treatment;
article 102 gives six months–two years for treatment/rehabilitation in public centres. The
prosecutor's appeal route has a 72-hour decision period. The law's final date is **2 January
2012**; article 125 repeals the 1997 law and 2005 amendment, with transitional qualifications.
Licences under articles 10–14 are for research, clinical, scientific or industrial purposes.
This is not evidence of an operational cannabis patient programme or a public seed shop route.

## Criticism and enforcement evidence

Read [Kathleen Kingsbury's July 2011 warning](https://idpc.net/news/2011/07/cambodia-s-alarming-new-drug-law),
published by Open Society Foundations and reproduced by IDPC. It discusses a draft approved
by Prime Minister Hun Sen, proposed two-year compulsory treatment, the draft's broad addiction
definition and allegations of abuse in existing centres. The new `debate` event uses the
publication date **21 July 2011** and distinguishes the draft from the final January 2012 law.
The article's prediction that signature might follow within weeks is not treated as fact.

Downloaded and read Amnesty's actual [Substance abuses report](https://www.amnesty.org/en/wp-content/uploads/2021/05/ASA2322202020ENGLISH.pdf),
ASA 23/2220/2020: executive summary, methodology, campaign background, detention and
recommendations. The document's [catalogue](https://www.amnesty.org/fr/documents/asa23/2220/2020/en/)
is dated **12 May 2020**; the press release is 13 May. The new event uses the report date.

The campaign began in January 2017, initially for six months, and was repeatedly extended.
Its **55,770 arrests by March 2020 concern all drugs**, amid a regional methamphetamine
expansion. The report supplies no cannabis-only arrest count, and the public text says so.
Amnesty interviewed 51 people, including 34 people who used/formerly used drugs, six of whom
were minors at arrest/detention. It interviewed 20 people held in administrative centres and
documented 15 prosecution cases; these are research samples, not national prevalence counts.
The study recorded interviewees' claims, without determining each person's underlying guilt.
Allegations, the organisation's findings and the law's safeguards retain their attribution.

The 2020 event is `debate`: a published investigation and reform demand, not an enacted
liberalisation. The 2017 entry retains `tightening` because the controlled category explicitly
includes a crackdown, with enforcement, treatment and police-powers tags. The new report
article explains methods and recommendations; the campaign article explains its onset,
continuation and the two routes into detention.
