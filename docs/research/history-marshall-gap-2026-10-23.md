## The 1989 marijuana appeal

The judiciary’s **Marshall Islands Law Reports, volume 1**, was downloaded (`court1.pdf/.txt`). **Republic v Sakaio**, Supreme Court criminal appeal 88-01, **18 October 1989**, occupies printed pp. **182–191**. The full relevant judgment was read, including the facts, missing trial record, police/court advice duties, informed guilty plea, waiver of counsel, requested withdrawal and disposition. It is the primary source, not the headnote alone.

George Sakaio pleaded guilty to marijuana possession under P.L. 1987-11 without a lawyer and immediately received two years. He asked to withdraw the plea and was refused. At a hearing five days later, the Public Defender was refused permission to represent him because he had already been convicted. The Supreme Court reversed and remanded for further action. It found denial of legal assistance and insufficient assurance that the plea/waiver were understood. It did not acquit him, grant a cannabis exemption or change the substantive penalty ladder. Quantity and conviction history are not supplied in this opinion, so the two years are not described as the standard punishment for a first small amount. The event is case-specific enforcement under existing constitutional rights, tagged court/sentencing/police powers, rather than a new score step.

## Signed 2020 and 2025 amendments

The official parliament index and public-law library supplied the original scans. **P.L. 2020-12** was recovered through the parliament’s 2020 download link (`amend2020.pdf`). Its preamble, definitions, possession/control wording, marijuana penalty scale, seizure provisions, minor-supply rule and effective-date/certification clauses were reviewed with local Vision OCR (`amend2020-ocr.txt`). The final certificate was visually checked: parliamentary passage **21 May 2020**, certification **4 June 2020**. The operative clause makes certification the commencement date.

The amendment renames the law, expressly covers methamphetamine, MDMA and synthetic cathinones, and adds cash to drug-transport/import/export seizure powers. It republishes the separate marijuana and concentrate ladders and the penalties for supplying minors. Its preamble refers to changing proof burdens, but the public text does not turn that statement into a broader unsupported description of the operative owner-recovery procedure. It states the positive cash addition and the one-month recovery procedure. The event is `tightening`, with an explicit unscored reason: this property rule is not evidence of a whole-point change to ordinary adult possession/use/supply scores. It does not claim this was the first forfeiture authority.

**P.L. 2025-37** was downloaded from the parliament’s amending-act folder (`amend2025.pdf`). All three pages were read through OCR, with the certificate visually checked. This caught an OCR error that rendered the day as 1: the signed date is **6 October 2025**, after passage **19 September 2025**. Section 103 commences the Act on certification. The existing year-only history event is corrected to **2025-10-06**. The existing reporting-duty article is rewritten around the recovered original, including the definition addition omitted from its earlier account.

The amendment does two things: it expressly adds marijuana and marijuana concentrate to the prohibited-drug definition, and creates section 912A’s reporting duty. The latter requires people who witness activities involving drugs to report to national or local police, protects good-faith reporters against criminal/civil penalties, guarantees confidentiality of the information and sets a $1,000 fine for non-compliance. The public text follows that wording; it does not invent a reasonable-suspicion standard, reporting deadline or witness immunity beyond good faith.

The explicit definition addition matters because the 2020 revision’s new prohibited-drug list did not expressly name cannabis even though marijuana definitions, the older deeming clause, penalty bands and minor-supply rules remained. No binding decision establishing a legal cannabis interval was recovered. No decriminalisation interval is invented from that drafting issue, and the 2025 addition is not called the first cannabis prohibition. The reporting duty concerns witnesses; it and the definition clarification have a documented unscored reason.
