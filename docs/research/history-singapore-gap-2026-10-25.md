# Singapore: closing the 1870–1951 history gap, 25 October 2026

Singapore's timeline jumped from the 1870 Excise Ordinance to a 1951 Dangerous Drugs Ordinance
entry whose only source was a Library of Congress survey. This pass recovered the missing 1927
law, corrected the 1973 and 1990 entries, and wrote explanatory articles for the 1927, 1973 and
1990 changes. The 1951 ordinance itself remains unread; no article was written for it.

## Sources actually read

- **Straits Settlements Government Gazette, vol. 62, no. 51** (2 June 1927), pp. 992 and
  1027–1049, through the National Library Board's BookSG flipbook. Page 992 carries the Objects
  and Reasons for the Liquors Revenue Bill; pp. 1027–1049 carry Ordinance No. 7 of 1927, the
  Deleterious Drugs Ordinance, with the First and Second Schedules. The ordinance was passed on
  16 May, assented to on 2 June and published on 10 June 1927.
- **Singapore Statutes Online, Misuse of Drugs Act 1973, legislative history.** Part 1, items
  11–18 give the 1926 Revised Edition of the Deleterious Drugs Ordinance, the 1927 Ordinance,
  its 1928 and 1935 amendments, the 1936 Revised Edition, and the 1951 Dangerous Drugs
  Ordinance. The 1951 ordinance repealed the 1936 Revised Edition and commenced on 1 March 1951.
  Part 4, item 32 gives the 1973 Act's commencement as 7 July 1973. Item 48 gives the 1989
  amendment's commencement as 15 February 1990.
- **Misuse of Drugs Bill 1972 (Bill No. 46/1972)**, clauses 3, 6, 8, 15, 21–22 and 28, the
  Second Schedule, and the Explanatory Statement. Read from the Singapore Statutes Online bill
  supplement; first reading 22 November 1972.
- **Drugs (Prevention of Misuse) Bill 1969 (Bill No. 7/1969)**, the Schedule and Explanatory
  Statement. The schedule covers amphetamine and its derivatives, lysergamide, mescaline and
  related substances, and does not list cannabis.
- **Misuse of Drugs (Amendment) Act 1989 (No. 38 of 1989)** and its **Bill (No. 39/1989)**,
  sections 17, 22, 31, 33 and 37, the Second Schedule and the Explanatory Statement.
- **MHA Home Team Journal**, January 2020 special edition, pp. 59–61 and Table 1. Used for the
  1975 morphine and diamorphine thresholds, the 1988 arrest figure, the Minister's reasons for
  the 1989 amendment, and the cannabis trafficking data. Table 1 dates the 1990 commencement to
  15 January, which conflicts with the Act and was not followed.
- **Library of Congress, Cannabis laws in Singapore** (1970 survey), section 6 of the Dangerous
  Drugs Ordinance, 1951. Used only for the 1951 possession penalty in the 1973 article and entry.
- **Los Angeles Times**, 11 February 1990, "Law Extending Death Penalty in Drug Cases to Take
  Effect This Week in Singapore" (Reuters). It says the law would come into force on the coming
  Thursday, 15 February, and corroborates the Act's commencement.

## Database changes

- Added `singapore-deleterious-drugs-ordinance-1927`, dated `1927-06`, category `tightening`.
  Section 22(a) made cultivating, buying, selling or possessing the Cannabis sativa plant an
  offence; section 22(b) did the same for the resin and preparations based on it. The First
  Schedule defined Indian hemp as the dried flowering or fruiting tops from which the resin had
  not been extracted, and a proviso kept Indian hemp and galenical preparations of it outside
  section 22. Indian hemp therefore became a licensed "deleterious drug": possession required a
  licence or a doctor's or veterinary surgeon's directions under section 17, dealing required a
  licence under section 21, manufacture a licence under section 26, and chemists supplied it on
  prescription under section 25. Import and export were vested in the Principal Civil Medical
  Officer under section 5. The general penalty under section 23 was up to $10,000, three years or
  both; self-administration under section 20 carried up to $5,000.
- The Objects and Reasons explain why the change came in 1927: the bhang provisions were removed
  from the liquor code because the 1925 Geneva International Opium Convention had made them
  inadequate, and restrictions on Indian hemp and its derivatives moved into the Deleterious
  Drugs Bill.
- Date precision is a month, not a day. The Statutes Online history gives 2 June 1927 as the
  commencement, while the gazette's bracketed date is 10 June 1927. The ordinance was passed on
  16 May and assented on 2 June. June is the only date all three accounts support.
- The 1927 step is `{possession 1, cultivation 1, enforcement 2, sharing 1, consumption 1,
  access 0}` before and `{possession 1, cultivation 0, enforcement 2, sharing 0, consumption 1,
  access 0}` after. The plant ban and the end of open dealing are the changes a user would feel;
  possession and consumption were already restricted by the 1870 excise system, and the new
  licensing route did not create a patient access channel. The 1973 entry was re-based to the
  same vector so the chain stays continuous.
- Revised `singapore-misuse-of-drugs-1973`. The Act replaced the 1951 ordinance and the 1969
  Act, put cannabis in Class B, set possession or consumption at up to 10 years and $20,000,
  trafficking at 3–20 years, $5,000–$40,000 and 2–10 strokes, trafficking to a person under 18
  at up to 30 years, $50,000 and 15 strokes, and growing at 3–20 years and $5,000–$40,000. The
  trafficking presumption was 15 g of cannabis or cannabis resin. The 1969 Act did not cover
  cannabis; that exclusion is now stated in both the entry and the article.
- Revised `singapore-cannabis-death-penalty-1990` and dated it 15 February 1990. The 1989
  amendment set death for trafficking more than 500 g of cannabis or 200 g of resin; 330–500 g
  of cannabis or 130–200 g of resin carried 20–30 years and 15 strokes; the trafficking
  presumption was 15 g of cannabis or 10 g of resin. It also covered importing and exporting,
  not only selling or distributing. It is marked `unscored`: Singapore was already at zero on
  every segment, so the death penalty deepened an existing prohibition rather than moving a
  score that had room to fall.
- Merged the 1927 ordinance and its Objects and Reasons into one source record. The site builds
  reference lists by URL, and the two records pointed at the same NLB page; keeping them apart
  produced a citation link to a reference that the page never created. The merged record gives
  both locators.

## Articles

- `articles/singapore-deleterious-drugs-ordinance-1927.md` covers the move from licensed bhang
  shops to medical and pharmacy licences, the plant and resin ban, the Indian hemp proviso, who
  could still handle hemp, and the repeal in 1951. It links the 1870 entry and the country page.
- `articles/singapore-misuse-of-drugs-1973.md` covers the two earlier laws, the 1969 Act's
  exclusion of cannabis, Class B, the penalty schedule, the trafficking presumption, and the new
  arrest, search and urine-testing powers. Its opening now cites the Statutes Online history for
  commencement and repeal.
- `articles/singapore-cannabis-death-penalty-1990.md` covers the 1975 heroin and morphine
  threshold, the 1988 arrest figures and the Minister's reasons, the cannabis thresholds, the
  wider trafficking definition, the other drugs in the same schedule, the urine-testing change
  and the later 1993 and 2013 amendments.

## Remaining gaps and blocked leads

- The 1951 Dangerous Drugs Ordinance is still known only through the Library of Congress survey
  quoting section 6. The Ordinance itself was not retrieved, so the existing 1951 entry remains
  a context entry with its original source and no article. Its date could be tightened to
  1 March 1951 once the text or commencement notice is read.
- The 1928 and 1935 amendments were not read. The Statutes Online history gives their
  commencement dates (21 July 1928 and 1 April 1936), but their text was not needed for the 1927
  entry.
- The 1969 Act's operation and the 1971 creation of the Central Narcotics Bureau are covered
  from secondary sources already in the database; no new primary text was read for them in this
  pass.
- The MHA table's 15 January 1990 date was treated as an error. The Act's commencement notice,
  the Statutes Online history and contemporary reporting all give 15 February. The discrepancy
  is recorded in the entry's source locator rather than silently ignored.
