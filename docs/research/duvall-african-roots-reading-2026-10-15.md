## Findings applied

- Rewrote `culture/traditions/bena-riamba.md`: removed the unsupported 1850s
  Zanzibar arrival narrative, the treatment of lubuku as the country's name, and
  the translation of moyo as hemp. Duvall reconstructs a local trading encounter
  around 1864 through later sources; that is not the first arrival of cannabis
  in the Congo Basin. Lubuku is the society of friendship; moyo is oath/life.
- Qualified “Sons of Hemp”. Note 21 distinguishes bena/bana and competing
  translations; Duvall's “people of the riamba” is a reasoned preference, not
  an uncontested word-for-word translation.
- Added `culture/people/sangula-meta.md`, with her religious authority, the
  disagreement over kinship, and the interests of observers. No birth/death dates
  invented. Note 35 qualifies the probable-sister account with Dias de Carvalho's
  conflicting wife account. Reports of coercion are attributed rather than
  treated as Sangula's own account.
- Rewrote `articles/dr-congo-bena-riamba-1881.md` and refined the existing history
  event's title/description/source. Kept its stable ID, 1881 date and contextual
  status. It records an encounter, not the movement's founding. No scores changed.
- Corrected the previous account's reference to three seated “men”: figure 1.4
  identifies Chingenge, Sangula and Mukenge. The existing *Am Kongo* hero image
  stays in place; the separate portrait adds information on Sangula.

## Interpretation and next research

Duvall challenges romantic cannabis histories through source comparison. His
reconstruction remains a secondary interpretation: colonial German, Portuguese,
British and Belgian accounts had conflicting allegiances and ethnographic limits.
Do not substitute one absolute narrative for another, infer drug effects from
political outcomes, or present cannabis as a cause of a peaceful or violent society.
