# History database: important gaps and research priorities

Audit date: **16 October 2026**. This is a database audit and research plan, not a claim to have exhaustively checked the world's cannabis history. No history entries, scores or country profiles were changed during this audit.

## Main finding

The largest remaining gap is the reconstruction of **how regulation actually developed and affected people**. We have accumulated substantial detail about early medicines and prohibitions, but still compress entire national transitions into single score changes. More isolated discoveries will not resolve that imbalance by themselves.

The main treaties, several influential commissions, major modern legalization events, the 1964 THC discovery and early endocannabinoid discoveries already have entries. They should not be presented as missing. Regional or scientific details may be folded into a broader existing record rather than needing a new event: CB1 cloning in 1990 and CB2 in 1993, for example, are already mentioned in the 1988 receptor entry.

## Priority 1: reconcile the historical score timelines

These are more consequential than another small pharmacy notice because they affect the historical charts.

- **United States:** `us-state-bans-1913` explicitly says it stands for the whole state-by-state process through the following decades. The national score changes from 98 to 22 at 1913. The database needs a documented aggregation method and dated stages, rather than placing the whole transition in that year.
- **Australia:** `australia-ban-1926` describes a federal import prohibition and state restrictions continuing through 1959. Nevertheless, the national score changes from 98 to 11 at 1926. Import control, state possession offences and nationwide coverage need separate treatment.
- **Ceylon/Sri Lanka:** the scored chain stays at 49 until the 1905 ordinance, while the database now records the 1878 bhang/ganja prohibition with exceptions and its 1899 consolidation. The new evidence and old score allocation have not been reconciled. The 1905 event itself remains unchecked.
- **India, Pakistan and Bangladesh:** reconstruct the licensed excise systems and transitions to later prohibitions, including provincial variation and legal inheritance. India has 45 events but one scored change; Pakistan has 10 and one. An entry such as India's 1930 Dangerous Drugs Act already exists, so the task is integration and original-law verification, not rediscovery.

The chain diagnostic flags seven countries starting below the unregulated baseline: Bangladesh, India, Morocco, Nepal, Pakistan, Tunisia and South Africa. These are **review flags, not seven proven errors**: some correctly reflect documented licensed systems. Passing the diagnostic also does not validate Australia's compressed chronology.

## Priority 2: replace assumed first restrictions with actual legal histories

There are **48 placeholders**, including **18 in Africa**. Their interpolation changes the charts even though their first instruments remain unidentified.

High-value starting points are Tanzania/Tanganyika, Zimbabwe/Southern Rhodesia, Namibia/South West Africa, Cameroon, Ethiopia, Somalia and Cabo Verde. Several already have later domestic instruments in the database. Establish which earlier law applied, its territorial reach, whether cannabis was expressly scheduled, and what conduct it restricted. Later statutes are anchors, not proof of a first prohibition.

Ethiopia's placeholder, for example, applies a generic inference about colonial governments without identifying an Ethiopian instrument. That is evidence for a research task, not a verified Ethiopian ban date. The same distinction should govern every estimate.

## Priority 3: correct geographic and jurisdictional blind spots

Pre-1900 coverage is concentrated: India has **21** events and Egypt **14**, together 35 of the 130 early entries. China, Japan, Indonesia, Pakistan, Bangladesh and South Korea have **zero pre-1900 entries attributed to their present-day profiles**. Some relevant history may sit under predecessor jurisdictions; zero does not prove that nothing is covered anywhere or that cannabis was unregulated.

Research should follow legal systems across borders: British Indian excise laws into Pakistan and Bangladesh; Dutch East Indies ordinances into Indonesia; Ottoman and mandate law; French, Portuguese, German and British African regulations into successor states. Present-day countries are useful navigation, but do not themselves define the boundaries of an old law.

China's first half of the twentieth century and the early PRC period, Japanese controls before the 1948 Act, and Soviet/republic-level distinctions also deserve targeted audits. These are **coverage leads**, not newly verified prohibition dates. Latin American early coverage outside Mexico and Brazil is another gap to investigate.

## Priority 4: connect legal changes to consequences

There are already enforcement, patient activism, Rastafari, cultural and scientific entries; these subjects are not absent wholesale. The next improvement is a comparative set of evidence on arrests, imprisonment, sentencing disparities, crop destruction, growers' livelihoods, practical patient access and relief from old convictions. A seizure announcement alone says little about the wider impact of a policy.

For each major ban or reform, aim for a small linked sequence: the legal decision, when and where implementation began, and a credible later assessment. Separate all-drug figures from cannabis-specific figures. Separate statutory medical permission from medicine actually reaching patients, and adult legalization from legal retail opening.

## Priority 5: strengthen foundational records before expanding obscure detail

The article backlog identifies missing full accounts for the Soviet 1934 measure, Nigeria 1927, Siam 1935, Kenya 1914, Australia 1926 and the U.S. state-ban transition. All already have database events. Several merit an original-law review before writing an authoritative article, particularly where a large score movement is attached to a short account.

Source priority should follow consequence: a weak source supporting a whole country's first prohibition matters more than a weak source supporting an incidental cultural anecdote. Different source domains are useful, but retrieving the original instrument is more valuable than adding several sites that repeat one another.

## Recommended next work order

1. Reconstruct the U.S., Australian and Ceylon score transitions, with an explicit aggregation method for federal systems.
2. Audit colonial excise inheritance across India, Pakistan and Bangladesh.
3. Replace a first batch of African placeholders using territorial gazettes, schedules and consolidations.
4. Add the seven concrete milestone candidates above, avoiding duplicates and distinguishing policy recommendations from law.
5. Write full articles for the highest-impact foundational entries once their legal scope and dates are established.

For future data design, consider explicit fields for **historical jurisdiction, legal conduct, cannabis form, date type and evidence confidence**. Those would make an import prohibition, a prescription rule and a personal-possession ban easier to distinguish consistently. This audit does not implement a schema change or automatically rescore any country.
