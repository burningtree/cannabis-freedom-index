## Evidence boundaries and further leads

Consumer claims that flower is cleaner or less addictive are social beliefs,
not established chemical/clinical conclusions. The word does not guarantee
potency, a particular strain or botanical difference. Some interviewees'
classification of hashish as an opiate is explicitly identified as mistaken.
No assertion about the first-ever use of gol in Persian is made.

The chapter's accounts of Tehran's bams, Qalat and Hormoz may support later
entries on social spaces. They describe particular settings in the 2010s,
not present legal exemptions or safe travel destinations. Observed tolerance
and the author's concept of quasi-legality must be distinguished from law.
Media reports and users' claims of non-enforcement need the same care.

Early historical passages need additional checking before reuse:

- The chapter conflates “Ibn Rushd (Razhes)” with dates 854–930. Those names
  identify different scholars; the 2018 paper correctly names al-Razi/Rhazes.
- Identification of ancient mang/bang with cannabis is uncertain. Do not
  convert a textual hypothesis into chemical identification of a ritual drink.
- The Rudabeh/Rostam childbirth story needs examination of the primary text
  before identifying a preparation as cannabis or describing its medical use.
- The Bibi Shahr Banu passage's religious chronology and the claimed survival
  of a cannabis-stew ritual need independent verification. No new ritual
  entry created from this account alone.
- Violence/gateway claims in press reports belong to the history of rhetoric,
  not findings that cannabis caused the crimes described.
- Personal reports of substituting cannabis for heroin or methamphetamine do
  not establish treatment efficacy. No medical recommendations added.
