## 137: New Zealand's permitted hemp plants

Opened the full [enacted SL 2026/85](https://www.legislation.govt.nz/secondary-legislation/pco-drafted/2026/85/en/latest/)
in the in-app browser after web/terminal requests returned empty responses or 403.
Read commencement, definition, regulations 14A–14B and 22A–22B and transitional provisions.
The instrument begins 28 May 2026. Regulation 14A permits cultivation, possession,
procurement, sale and supply for enumerated purposes and legally entitled recipients;
14A(2) preserves import/export licensing and cultivation notifications. Police notification
lasts a year; MPI notice precedes cultivation and is no more than a year old. Old licence
holders have transitional timing. Non-cultivation activities do not themselves require
notification. The event can now be checked.

Opened the Ministry's full [Hemp guidance](https://www.health.govt.nz/regulation-legislation/hemp)
in the same browser. It expressly excludes high-THC cultivar clones, tissue, seedlings
and seeds from hemp, even though immature material may itself contain little THC. The
legal threshold is no more than 1% dry weight. This is not blanket permission for cuttings
of any cannabis. Domestic permissions and import/export licensing remain distinct.

Added `conditional` to both planting vocabularies rather than mislabelling this revoked
licence regime as licensed or declaring all clones illegal. Applied it to seeds and clones,
including trade; map colours, table ordering and automatic schema/legend propagation were
checked. The six scored New Zealand dimensions are unchanged.

## 138: Cambodia's unsupported “fines typical” implication

Read the existing local NACD 2012 translation, including articles 27–28, 38, 40, 45,
100–110 and the cannabis schedule. Article 40 prescribes a basic two-to-five-year prison
term for unlawful keeping, with a possible additional fine. Article 28 disapplies the
Criminal Code's article 97 sentencing provision and prevents replacement of the drug law's
principal penalties with alternative/additional penalties under the Code. The actual
article 28 page was rendered and visually inspected. This is stronger evidence than
inferring a custodial outcome merely from an arrest statistic.

Read Amnesty's [2023 CESCR submission](https://www.amnesty.org/en/wp-content/uploads/2023/01/ASA2363562023ENGLISH.pdf),
ASA 23/6356/2023, printed pp. 4–5, and the existing 2020 report's methodology/context.
The issue calls the 2023 document *Substance Abuses*; that is the title of the underlying
2020 report, not this 2023 submission. The evidence concerns drugs generally, not a
cannabis-only sentence distribution. Article 48 in the law covers Table III; cannabis's
Table I possession provision is article 40. Use that precise provision.

Opened the government's [2025 enforcement figures, published March 2026](https://akp.gov.kh/post/detail/364183).
They distinguish cannabis plants/weight seized but do not give a cannabis-only sentencing
or fine-versus-prison breakdown. Searches for personal cannabis dispositions found travel
warnings and cultivation cases, not a representative sentencing dataset. None establishes
that fines are typical for small cannabis possession. Neither the all-drug detention
figures nor occasional cannabis cases are used to manufacture such a rate.

Decision: enforcement 2's empirical “fines typical” claim is unsupported. Move Cambodia
from 2 to 1 on the documented custodial penalty basis, not on an invented frequency. The
custodial band's label now describes custodial penalties, and its generic answer says
prison/compulsory detention can occur instead of claiming users are “routinely jailed”.
Reviewed the score-1 profiles' enforcement accounts; several already expressly state that
frequency is unknown or include administrative custody, so the narrower claim is appropriate.
The quick answer for Cambodia remains the explicit prison warning, and the profile states
the scope of the all-drug evidence.

The 2012 law is the scored tightening event, with the previous six-score vector (including
