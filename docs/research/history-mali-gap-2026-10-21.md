## 2001: use the full official gazette

The [official gazette of 20 October 2002](https://sgg-mali.ml/JO/2002/mali-jo-2002-29.pdf)
reproduces the complete drug law signed 18 July 2001 (printed pp. 1122–1139). Downloaded,
extracted, read relevant complete passages and visually inspected PDF pp. 12 and 18,
printed pp. 1132 and 1138. The earlier retained UNODC copy is an image scan with little
extractable text; this explains the profile's previous claim that personal-use penalties
were unavailable. That claim is now removed.

Article 138 explicitly covers small quantities possessed, purchased OR cultivated for
personal consumption. High-risk plants/substances, including cannabis oil: three months
to one year AND 25,000–100,000 francs OR either alone. Cannabis derivatives other than
oil: two–six months AND 15,000–75,000 francs OR either alone. No numeric gram or plant
threshold. Do not substitute flower versus concentrates for the statute's terminology.
The following waiver language refers to the last, lower-risk tier and contains age and
recidivism conditions; no general cannabis waiver was asserted.

Articles 94–95 require prison AND a fine for ordinary high-risk drug cultivation and
international trafficking: five–ten years, with different fine ranges. Article 96 local
trafficking and Article 98 direct supply for another's consumption do allow either prison
or a fine alone. Direct supply: six months–three years and 20,000–200,000 francs, or either
alone. Article 9 requires landholders to destroy cannabis plants growing on their land.
Article 137 prohibits use outside prescription and addresses confiscation; no standalone
sentence for bare use was invented by copying a trafficking penalty.

Article 112's optional ten-to-twenty-year territorial exclusion for foreigners and other
additional penalties apply to the specified Articles 94–102, not automatically Article 138.
The profile now states this scope and cites the actual law. It also replaces unsupported
Wikipedia-based statements about production/products with the documented hemp history.

Date remains the verified signature, 18 July 2001, as in the existing event. Parliament
adopted it on 29 June. The retained gazette issue is dated October 2002; no separate
operational commencement date is asserted. Article 144 expressly repeals the 1983 law.
Article 138 has odd cross-references (95 and 99 despite the explicit cultivation wording);
the article's express personal-cultivation rule is quoted in substance without silently
repairing its statutory numbering.

## 1983 and the score chain

[UNODC text of Law 83-14](https://www.unodc.org/cld/uploads/res/document/mli/loi-83-14_html/mali-loi_stupefiants.pdf)
was downloaded and read in full, including visual inspection of its complete PDF p. 3.
Article 8 allows five–ten years of forced labour AND a fine OR either alone for illicit
use. Article 3 provides life at forced labour AND a fine for illegal narcotic production,
manufacture, import or export. Article 9 concerns defendants accused of attempted illicit
use, medical assessment, possible compulsory detoxification and possible withholding of
punishment. Refusal invokes the use penalties. It is not a universal voluntary diversion
scheme. Article 13 permits day/night searches of manufacturing, processing or storage
premises. The description is rewritten around these consequences with proper tags.

The [reproduced 1988 schedule decree](https://www.scribd.com/document/779745678/Decret-88-199-Liste-stupefiants-JO)
was actually read, including both pages' relevant text and signature. It explicitly lists
cannabis and resin under the 1983 law and is signed 12 July 1988. It is not claimed that
the 1983 law itself names cannabis or that 1988 was its first restriction. The 1983 event's
pre-existing `before` is preserved. The new 2001 `before` is:

`{possession: 1, cultivation: 0, enforcement: 0, sharing: 1, consumption: 1, access: 0}`.

This gives the earlier long-sentence regime cultivation 0 (no lesser personal-grow tier)
and enforcement 0 (long forced-labour sentences for users), matching `segments.yaml`.
The 2001 personal-grow tier restores cultivation 1 and its shorter personal penalties
restore enforcement 1, leading to the unchanged current profile. Possession and the other
segments stay unchanged: the law alone does not show that fines are typical in practice,
so it does not justify a higher current score. Rounded chain: 1916 98→14, 1983 14→5,
2001 5→9. The early colonial step remains inherited and is not independently reconstructed
here. Category corrected from tightening to easing; obsolete `unscored` reason removed.

The 1972 special-procedure entry becomes `other`, tagged enforcement: its own retained
note says penalties were unchanged. No original 1972 ordinance was recovered; the 1983
law separately cites a July 1973 special-procedure ordinance. Do not conflate those dates.

## 1997 controlled medicines and 2013 investigations

Read the [original 1997 gazette](https://sgg-mali.ml/JO/1997/mali-jo-1997-19.pdf), decree
97-230, printed pp. 787–789; complete PDF pp. 12–13 visually inspected. It uses the
1988 narcotics list, including cannabis/resin, and prescribes special forms, a maximum
seven-day treatment supply, a fresh prescription for continuation, locked storage,
stock records and import certificates. The date is signature, 8 August 1997, distinct
from the issue's 15 October publication. Category `other`, tags trade, retail, science.
General controlled-medicine rules are not evidence of a functioning cannabis programme,
actual patient dispensing or a new cannabis exception.

Read the [complete 2013 ordinance](https://sgg-mali.ml/JO/2013/mali-jo-2013-40.pdf),
printed pp. 1565–1566, and visually checked both pages (PDF pp. 5–6). It modifies Articles
118, 141 and 142 and inserts 141-1, giving the central drug office an operational and
criminal-investigation role. Controlled deliveries and incitement to sell retain prompt
notification to judicial authorities. The original 2001 Article 118 already allowed an
office director to authorise operations; do not invent a new removal of prior judicial
approval. Other services must inform the office immediately. International trafficking
cases can be transferred to the office at the specialised prosecutor's request through
the competent prosecutor-general. No user penalty amended. Category `other`, tags
police-powers and trafficking. Signature 2 September 2013, publication 4 October.

## 2017 authorisation and 2020 withdrawal

Read the [2019 report of the licence terms](https://www.maliweb.net/societe/culture-du-cannabis-industriel-au-mali-les-raisons-a-retenir-2847302.html)
and the [2020 account of OCS director Adama Tounkara's press conference](https://www.maliweb.net/societe/culture-du-chanvre-industriel-au-mali-les-precisions-du-directeur-general-de-locs-2875623.html)
in full, excluding reader comments. The latter is a first-hand account of an official's
21 May statement, published 25 May. He confirms permission dated 28 February 2017 and
withdrawal on 25 February 2020, explaining that producers sought to change the agreed
terms. Events are checked against the read accounts; the underlying signed decisions
and production contract remain unrecovered, and that limitation is retained here.

The 2019 account identifies African Seeds, initial seed exports for medical purposes,
a 5 March 2018 oil amendment and a reported 0.2% THC ceiling. This ceiling is a reported
project condition, not a verified general national hemp definition. Approved seed,
restrictions on resowing, inspections and harvest supervision are attributed to the
reported conditions. No harvest, export volume, operating farm, domestic patient supply
or consumer access was inferred from an authorisation. The director's account attributes
the cancellation to proposed term changes; another report cites irregularities, which was
not substituted as the verified reason. No accusations from reader comments were used.

Both events use category `hemp` and tags hemp, seeds and trade. Medical export purposes
do not establish actual patient access, so neither event changes access or other scores.
The article follows the authorisation through its cancellation and public explanation.
