# Papua New Guinea: closing the 1970 possession gap, 26 October 2026

The 1970 and 2022 Papua New Guinea entries were added in an earlier pass but left unchecked. This pass read the
UN trusteeship report, the 1952 Dangerous Drugs Ordinance, the consolidated old Act, the 1973
*Henderson* judgment, the short-lived 2021 amendment, the 2022 Controlled Substance Act and the
commencement gazette. It checked the 1970 and 2022 entries, added the 2021 amendment as an
unscored context entry, corrected the 1970 locator from Chapter 9 to Chapter 8, added the 1952
ordinance as a source, and replaced the profile's dead Parliament link with an archived copy. No
scores moved.

## The documents

### The 1970 trusteeship report

**United Nations, T/1733, 23 May 1972.** The report of the Commonwealth of Australia on the
administration of Papua New Guinea covers 1 July 1970 to 30 June 1971. The drug passage is in
Chapter 8, "Narcotic Drugs", printed p. 182, PDF p. 211. Chapter 9, "Drugs", begins on printed
p. 183 and is a short general chapter; it does not contain the possession passage. The entry and
article use the Chapter 8 locator.

The report says:

> Narcotic drugs are neither manufactured or produced in, nor exported from Papua New Guinea.
> Importation is controlled by the Dangerous Drugs Ordinance 1952-1970.

It then describes the gap:

> It was indicated that, as the result of a recent prosecution, a loophole had been discovered in
> the Papua New Guinea legislation which enabled persons to possess or smoke locally grown
> marihuana. This anomaly was corrected when the Dangerous Drugs (Possession) Bill was adopted by
> the Papua New Guinea House of Assembly in 1970. This Bill amended the principal Ordinance.

The report also records an officer's evidence to the Australian Senate Select Committee on Drug
Trafficking and Drug Abuse: "isolated instances of trafficking in narcotic drugs and a very small
incidence of drug abuse." The prosecution, the person prosecuted and the exact date are not given.

### The 1952 ordinance

**United Nations, E/NL.1956/73–75, 6 September 1956.** The document communicates the Dangerous
Drugs Ordinance 1952 (No. 21 of 1953) under the 1931 Narcotics Convention. The ordinance was
reserved on 4 November 1952 and the Governor-General's assent was notified on 23 April 1953. The
PacLII index gives its commencement as 25 August 1955. It is on pp. 1–3 of the UN document;
the regulations follow on pp. 4–5.

The definition of "dangerous drugs" includes, at paragraph (f):

> galenical preparations (such as extract and tincture) of Indian Hemp and all preparations made
> from such galenical preparations.

"Indian hemp" is defined as the dried flowering or fruiting tops of *Cannabis sativa* from which
the resin has not been extracted. The definition therefore covered hemp extracts and tinctures, not
the raw plant material that people smoked.

Section 9 allowed imports only for medicinal purposes, under licence. Section 12(1) created
possession offences only for a "prohibited import": possession on board a ship, importation, or
possession of a prohibited import brought in contrary to the ordinance or a licence. Its penalty
was three months to two years. Section 12(2) applied the section to raw opium, prepared opium and
all dangerous drugs whose importation was prohibited. Section 14 punished exporting or making a
dangerous drug with a fine of £50 or three months. Raw, locally grown cannabis was not a listed
dangerous drug, and local possession fell outside section 12. That is the loophole the 1970 Act
closed.

### The consolidated old Act

**Dangerous Drugs Act (Chapter 228), consolidated to No 23 of 1990.** The vLex text is in vigour
from 25 August 1955. Section 3(1) provides:

> A person who knowingly—
> (a) cultivates a plant from which a dangerous drug can be made; or
> (b) makes a dangerous drug; or
> (c) exports a dangerous drug; or
> (d) is in possession of or conveys a dangerous drug or a plant or part of a plant from which a
> dangerous drug can be made,
> is guilty of an offence unless he is authorized to do so by or under some other Act.
> Penalty: Imprisonment for a term of not less than three months and not exceeding two years.

The consolidation note says section 3(1) was amended by the Dangerous Drugs (Amendment) Act 1990
(No. 23 of 1990) and that "it is unclear whether this Act has commenced." The text shows both the
amended opening ("A person who knowingly—") and the original ("A person who—"). The 1990 amendment
was to add "knowingly" to all four paragraphs. The consolidation is the fullest statement of the
old regime; the 1970 Act's own text was not recovered.

### Henderson v Blackwell

**[1973] PNGLR 223, 5 April 1973, Frost SPJ.** Henderson was convicted in the District Court at
Boroka (as the judgment spells it) under section 7(1)(d) of the Dangerous Drugs (Possession) Act
1970 and sentenced to three months, the minimum. The judgment quotes the provision:

> By s7(1)(d) of the Dangerous Drugs (Possession) Act 1970, inter alia a person shall not knowingly
> be in possession of a dangerous drug and shall be liable to a penalty of "Imprisonment for not
> less than three months or more than two years."

The Supreme Court held that the minimum did not exclude section 138(1)(b) of the District Courts
Act (conditional release) or section 206(2) (a fine in lieu of imprisonment). It allowed the appeal
and remitted the case for further hearing. This is the only source found that gives the 1970 Act's
own section number and quoted penalty.

### The 2021 amendment

**Dangerous Drugs (Amendment) Act 2021, No. 20 of 2021.** The three-page scan is an archived copy
of the Parliament PDF. The Act is "MADE by the National Parliament to come into operation upon
certification by the Speaker." Its arrangement of sections lists only the repeal and replacement
of section 3 of the principal Act. The replacement repeats the four paragraphs of the old section
3(1), with "knowingly," and sets a penalty of a fine not exceeding K1,000,000 or imprisonment for
a term not exceeding 40 years, or both; offences are punishable on summary conviction. The
certifying officers record that the Act was made on 25 November 2021 and certified on 13 January
2022.

This was a genuine but very short-lived change. The Controlled Substance Act commenced on 31 March
2022, eleven weeks later, and replaced the old Act. The 2021 entry is therefore `unscored`: scoring
it would show a flat 40-year maximum for simple possession and then a 2022 "easing" when the new
tiers are in fact a tightening against the long-standing three-month-to-two-year regime.

### The 2022 Act and the commencement gazette

**Controlled Substance Act 2021, No. 26 of 2021.** The archived Parliament scan and OCR were read.
Schedule I lists Cannabis, Cannabis resin, Cannabis Oil and Cannabis Sativa Plants. Section 34
declares a Schedule I controlled plant strictly prohibited and not to be cultivated. Section 58
makes Schedule I substances strictly prohibited. Section 59 creates the cultivation offence.
Section 63 creates the possession offence, section 64 deals with implements, section 65 with
facilitating use, and section 68 with trafficking.

Schedule VII, Part A, sets the quantity and penalty table for Schedule I and Schedule II
substances:

| Quantity | Penalty |
| --- | --- |
| 10 g or less | Up to 2 years, a K15,000 fine, or both |
| More than 10 g and less than 2 kg | Up to 15 years, a K25,000 fine, or both |
| More than 2 kg | 16 years to life, a K26,000–K2,000,000 fine, or both |

For plants, the table gives fewer than five plants up to two years; more than five but fewer than
20 plants up to 15 years; and more than 20 plants 16 years to life. The table leaves the exact
five- and twenty-plant cases unclear.

**National Gazette No. G198, 22 March 2022.** The Governor-General's notice, dated 21 March 2022,
fixes 31 March 2022 as the date the Controlled Substance Act 2021 and the Lawyers (Amendment) Act
2021 come into operation. The notice is on PDF p. 55, printed pp. 197–198. This is the primary
source for the 2022 entry's date; the Parliament PDF itself does not give a commencement date.

### The index

**PacLII, Index to the Laws of Papua New Guinea, April 2019**, archived copy. The relevant entries

- Dangerous Drugs Act 1962, 19/1962, 24.05.1962, Chapter 228.
- Dangerous Drugs (Extension of Definition) Act 1968, 39/1968, 12.09.1968, Chapter 228.
- Dangerous Drugs (Possession) Act 1970, 82/1970, no chapter number or commencement date.
- Dangerous Drugs (Amendment of Section 9) Act 1973, 34/1973, Chapter 228.

The 1970 Act's year-only date in the index is why the entry keeps `date: "1970"` and the article
says "in 1970" rather than giving a day.

## Why the scores did not move

The chain was already `1970 100 → 11`, `2022-03-31 11 → 7`; this pass verified both steps rather
than changing them.

- **1970: 100 → 11.** The 1952 ordinance did not cover raw locally grown cannabis, and its
  possession offence was limited to prohibited imports. The 1970 Act created the general possession
  offence, with three months to two years. The consolidated old Act also covers cultivation,
  making, export and conveyance on the same penalty. Cultivation is scored at 1 from 1970 on the
  strength of section 3(1)(a) of the consolidation. The original 1970 Act's text was not recovered,
  so that attribution is not independently confirmed for 1970; the entry says "the old Act, as
  later consolidated" to keep the distinction.
- **2022: 11 → 7.** The new Act moved possession from 1 to 0 and enforcement from 2 to 1;
  cultivation stayed at 1. The Schedule VII tiers are harsher than the old three-month-to-two-year
  range: up to two years for 10 g or less, up to 15 years for more than 10 g but less than 2 kg,
  and 16 years to life for more than 2 kg. The 2021 amendment is unscored, so the 2022 entry's
  `before` remains the long-standing 11.
- The 1927, 1973, 2025 and 2026 PNG entries remain open in `scores:triage`; they were outside this
  pass.

## Remaining gaps

- **The 1970 Act's own text was not recovered.** The Act number and adoption year come from the
  PacLII index and T/1733. Section 7(1)(d) is known only from *Henderson*. The consolidated
  Chapter 228 text is the fullest statement of the old regime, but it is a later consolidation;
  the 1970 Act may have covered fewer activities than the consolidated section 3(1). The article
  and entry both attribute cultivation, making, export and conveyance to the old Act "as later
  consolidated," not to the 1970 Act's own text.
- **The 1968 Extension of Definition Act was not read.** The index gives 39/1968, 12.09.1968,
  Chapter 228. Its title suggests it extended the definition of dangerous drugs, possibly to raw
  cannabis, before the 1970 possession Act. If it did, the timeline may need an earlier definition
  entry. It was not added because the text was not read; the 1970 Act remains the first entry in
  the sources read that clearly creates a general possession offence.
- **The 1990 amendment's commencement is unclear.** The consolidation says so. The 1990 Act would
  add "knowingly" to section 3(1); the article mentions this but does not treat it as a scored
  change.
- **PacLII is behind Cloudflare.** The current consolidated index and the Act PDFs return 403 to
  automated requests from this environment; the archived April 2019 index supplied the Act numbers. The 2022 Act's current
  consolidated version was not read; the archived Act 26/2021 scan and the gazette were used.
- **The original Parliament PDFs for Acts 26/2021 and 20/2021 return 404.** Archived copies are
  used in the entry, article and profile. The profile's `controlled` source now points to the 2023
  capture.
- **Section 63's simple-possession route is awkward.** Section 63(1) covers controlled substances in
  Schedules II, III or IV; section 63(3) covers a Schedule I substance over two kilograms inside a
  school zone. The entry and article therefore describe what Schedule VII provides rather than
  asserting a clean section 63 charge for a small amount of Schedule I cannabis. A current
  consolidated Act or a court decision would settle how the simple-possession tier is charged.
- **The 2022 plant-count tiers have gaps.** The table says fewer than five plants, more than five,
  and more than 20; it does not state the penalty at exactly five or exactly 20. The entry follows
  the table's wording.
- **The 1952 ordinance's commencement date comes from the index.** The UN document gives the
  reservation (4 November 1952) and assent notification (23 April 1953), not the commencement. The
  PacLII index gives 25 August 1955. The article does not give a commencement date.
- **The old Act's penalties were not always uniform.** The 1952 ordinance's section 14 punished
  making or exporting with £50 or three months, while the consolidated section 3(1) uses three
  months to two years for all four paragraphs. The change may date from 1970 or a later amendment;
  the consolidated text is the only source for the unified scale.
