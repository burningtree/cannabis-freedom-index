## Added context events

- **June 1906: Middelburg growers petition.** The Cape's 1905 poison listing reserved sales to
  chemists. A petition from 170 growers and supporters challenged the resulting buying power and
  demanded evidence for medical claims of harm. The 1907 repeal promise and Council motion are
  reported as such; neither is treated as proof that repeal took effect. Category `debate`,
  tags `campaign`, `retail`, `colonial`.
- **2 July 1909: Hlabisa proposal.** A Zululand magistrate sought a circular prohibiting smoking
  by boys below puberty and all women. Chiefs and other magistrates held differing views about
  smoking, age and patriarchal authority. This is a proposal, not an enacted national ban.
  Category `debate`, tags `youth`, `traditional`, `colonial`.
- **August 1912: Paarl farmers seek restrictions.** The association petitioned the justice
  minister, enclosing an account of a fifteen-year-old purchasing cannabis from a chemist.
  Allegations about labour and threats to white farm women are attributed to campaigners.
  The request sought agricultural and sales controls; it did not itself change the law.
  Category `debate`, tags `campaign`, `colonial`, `race`, `youth`.

The 1903 ordinance criminalised giving, selling, bartering, exchanging or otherwise supplying,
and accepting, purchasing, exchanging or otherwise receiving cannabis. No unsupported exact
enactment day or penalty maximum was added. The chapter discusses neighbouring Transvaal
requests alongside the ordinance, but its correspondence includes earlier dates: prose avoids
claiming that the 1903 ordinance caused a request dated 1902.

The 1908 and 1911 survey replies came from officials and mine management, not a representative
survey of miners. Their tolerance is explained through labour recruitment and control; it is
not recast as a modern rights campaign. Accounts that smoke-induced coughing cleared mine dust
are explicitly presented as contemporary beliefs, not clinical findings. Violence, insanity and
sexual-threat claims are attributed to the officials and campaigners who made them.

Two notes in the chapter print **2011** for material the body and surrounding dated correspondence
place in **1911**. No exact newspaper day was inferred from the inconsistent note. The follow-up
entry remains dated only to 1911.

The 1916 bill was prepared in response to the Hague drug-control arrangements. The prose does
not claim that the 1912 convention required cannabis prohibition. It distinguished cannabis
from Leonotis plants also called dagga and covered plant parts, including seeds, plus smoking
pipes/materials. Proposed maximum penalties were £100 and three months. Its intended January
1917 start is described as a draft intention: the bill was never presented to parliament.

The 1916 `advertising` tag covers paraphernalia under the repository's existing definition; it
does not assert that this bill imposed a cannabis advertising ban. Other touched tags cover
colonial administration, race, military origins, retail, traditional use, campaigning, youth,
sentencing and seeds as supported by the episode.
