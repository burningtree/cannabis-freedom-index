# Malaysia: the 1891–1952 gap, no earlier hemp ordinance text opened

23 October 2026. Longest post-1850 hole: North Borneo bhang licensing in 1891
(`malaysia-north-borneo-bhang-licensing-1891`) to the peninsula Dangerous Drugs
Ordinance in force on 1 November 1952 (`malaya-dangerous-drugs-1952`), 61 years.
No new entry.

## The 1952 ordinance was read and is already the closing event

Wikisource text of the Dangerous Drugs Ordinance, 1952, as passed (Legislative
Council, 3 July 1952). Indian hemp is the dried flowering or fruiting tops of
the female Cannabis sativa from which the resin has not been extracted.
Import, export and possession need the High Commissioner’s authorisation, on
a fine of up to 10,000 dollars or up to five years, or both. The High
Commissioner in Council may regulate cultivation, production, possession, sale
and distribution. Resin of Indian hemp is treated with prepared opium. Extract
and tincture of Indian hemp sit in Part III of the First Schedule with the
other dangerous drugs. Section 50 and the Third Schedule repeal, by title, the
Straits Settlements Deleterious Drugs Ordinance (Cap. 190), the Federated Malay
States Deleterious Drugs Enactment (Cap. 183), and the deleterious-drugs
enactments of Johore, Kedah, Kelantan, Trengganu and Perlis, plus chandu laws.
The schedule does not say what those repealed laws did about hemp. The existing
1952 entry was not rewritten. Its “200 g trafficking presumption” is a later
amendment; the 1952 text does not contain it. The CommonLII reprint of Act 234
as revised to 2006 uses “cannabis” and applies the prepared-opium ban to the
plant itself. That is the amended Act, not the 1952 original.

## Leads that were not statutes naming hemp

Singapore Statutes Online legislative history of the Misuse of Drugs Act 1973
lists the Deleterious Drugs Ordinance from Ordinance XIV of 1907 through the
1936 revised edition, Chapter 190 (1 September 1936), and Ordinance 7 of 1951,
which repealed Chapter 190. The table gives readings and dates. It does not
list the drugs.

A Singapore Medical Journal pharmacy history says the 1907 ordinance dealt with
opium, morphine, cocaine and their derivatives. It does not name hemp.

The Straits Settlements Blue Book for 1930 records a gazette notification
adding to the First Schedule of the Deleterious Drugs Ordinance, 1927. The
Blue Book does not say what was added.

A Singapore Free Press item of 23 May 1933, as returned by the newspaper site,
begins “possession of 480 oz. of Indian hemp, as a deleterious drug” and a
sentence of nine months plus a fine. The article body on that fetch is a
truncated OCR line. It was not used.

A 1935 “Dangerous Drugs Ordinance” claim on an encyclopaedia page was not
opened as a statute. The United Kingdom Coca Leaves and Indian Hemp
Regulations, 1928, are a UK statutory instrument.

The texts of Cap. 190, of the 1927, 1928 and 1935 Straits amendments, and of
the state deleterious-drugs enactments were not found in a form that could be
read.
