## Original rules and amendments must be separated

Read the Iraqi Legislation Database's actual text of the 1933 cultivation
law, articles 1–6, including publication details. The law was signed on
1 May but took effect on publication, **8 May 1933**, issue 1248. It prohibits
cannabis cultivation, orders uprooting at the offender's expense, imposes a
first fine up to ten dinars and a repeat maximum of three months or twenty
dinars or both, and requires reporting crops. The earlier entry's description
of cultivating both hemp and hashish was replaced by the actual plant ban.
The original vector is retained without revalidation: a cultivation-only law
cannot by itself substantiate the existing simultaneous fall of all segments
to zero. That is an explicit score-reconstruction gap.

Read the 1938 law's articles 1–9 in the same database. They expressly include
Indian hemp, resin and preparations, with manufacture/import/export permits,
authorised possession and record keeping. The database paginates the law;
no claim is made to have read its later articles. Kept the existing 1938 entry
and its UN source unchanged rather than deriving a commencement date from
publication metadata without reading the operative commencement clause.

Obtained the **original 1965 text** from Dorar's transcription of the official
gazette, retained as `law1965.html` and `law1965.txt`. Read articles 1–17,
the reasons and the cannabis schedule items. Signature is **8 April 1965**;
article 16 commences it on publication, **24 May**, issue 1117. The cultivation
ban expressly excepted industrial fibre-and-seed crops with agriculture
ministry permission and direct supervision. Possession followed authorised
professional/institutional or prescription channels. Article 14's original
general penalty is **100–1,000 dinars OR six months–five years OR both**.
The official consolidated database's first page no longer contains the crop
exception because it incorporates the later repeal. That consolidated version
must not be backdated into 1965. The 1979 amendment independently identifies
its deletion as article 2(2). No claim is made that a practical patient supply
service or industrial cannabis industry operated under the permission.

Read the full **1967 Law 4** in both the official database and Dorar's matching
transcription. Signed 24 January, published and effective **6 February 1967**,
issue 1368. New article 14 distinguishes personal possession/growing
(one–five years AND 100–500 dinars) from trafficking, giving away drugs and
commercial growing (temporary hard labour AND 500–2,000 dinars); relevant
officials face lifelong hard labour. Hosting use and encouraging minors are
also expressly covered. No conversion of historical hard-labour terms to
modern sentence ranges was guessed.

Read the full **1968 Law 196** in the official database. Signed 16 December,
effective **30 December**, issue 1673 p. 901. Principal trafficking offences
carry lifelong hard labour and a 3,000–10,000 dinar fine. Death/lifelong hard
labour alternatives apply to the precise repeat-supply group, trafficking-group
leaders and specified officials, not to every person with cannabis. Personal
possession/growing rises to three–ten years plus 500–1,000 dinars. The law's
own reasons state that earlier penalties had failed to deter smuggling.
Cannabis plants and their seeds are explicitly included in commercial movement.

Read the full **1970 Law 160**, issue 1905 p. 1, effective **9 August 1970**
on publication. The special armed-forces clause covers members, employees
and people working with or for the forces, with life or at least ten years
and 1,000–2,000 dinars; death is possible when the offence happens while
confronting the enemy. Kept the description close to the insertion and its
express stated purpose of deterring military drug smuggling/use. Did not infer
that all civilian possession was now a capital offence. The text has inconsistent
internal drafting/signature dates; operative publication resolves the event date.

Read the full **1979 Law 144**, issue 2741 p. 480, effective **19 November**
on publication. Article 2 repeals the industrial-crop exception; article 7
raises the personal-use maximum to fifteen years, retains special military
penalties, changes other sanctions and permits six months of treatment instead
for dependence arising from a medical condition. Earlier release requires
medical evidence. This is a mixed reform, category `other`, with hemp,
treatment and sentencing tags. It must not be presented as general voluntary
treatment immunity or as a purely liberalising change. The explanatory reasons
explicitly discuss treatment/rehabilitation and the amended treaty.

Read the full **2002 Law 38**, issue 3948 p. 470, signed 7 September and
effective **16 September** on publication. Its replacement of the introductory
penalty for article 14(I)(b) broadens death/life alternatives and requires
movable/immovable property confiscation. The covered list comes from the
1968 amendment. Kept that source alongside the short 2002 text so readers
can identify why growing cannabis for trade was included.

## The 2003 suspension and conditional return in 2004

Recovered CPA Order 7 from the archived CPA government collection at the
University of North Texas. Downloaded the actual three-page PDF and text;
visually checked PDF pp. 2–3. Section 3(1) suspends capital punishment generally;
section 6 commences on signature. The header says **9 June**, while the signed
page visibly reads **6/10/03**, matching the archive index's 10 June and the
later Iraqi order's own reference. Used **10 June 2003**, not the header date.
The law supplies the general suspension; its application to drug penalties
follows from that general scope and the preceding drug law. No drug offence
was made lawful by this change.

Read all of **Order 3 of 2004** in the official Iraqi database. Paragraph 2
restores capital punishment for the specified article 14 trafficking groups
**when intended to finance or assist article 190 offences**. Paragraph 9
commences on issue, **8 August 2004**, despite later publication on 1 September,
issue 3987 p. 6. Paragraph 7 commutes death sentences already final before
commencement. Read Amnesty's explanation on printed p. 10 (PDF p. 12) and
English legal reproduction in annex 1, printed pp. 43–45 (PDF pp. 45–47).
Its explanation identifies the article 190 link as violent overthrow of the
government. The event retains that condition; several general summaries
simply say drug trafficking, which would conceal a substantive limitation.
No inference was made about subsequent judicial practice or today's law.

## Scores, categories and further gaps

All existing `before` vectors are preserved without claiming their historical
validity. The baseline has an all-zero interval from the 1933 step to the
2017 step. New harsher penalties cannot decrease it further; the temporary
suspension, conditional treatment and hemp permission also need joint
reconstruction rather than isolated arbitrary numbers. Explicit `unscored`
reasons record the open work. The 1965 consolidation is `other`, the 1967
change `tightening`, and capital-punishment changes `death`. The 1979 law is
`other` for its opposing changes. Tags were selected from the controlled list,
including hemp/seeds, trafficking, sentencing, military, treatment, youth and
eradication where the event actually addresses them.

The 1938 law's remaining articles, the 1955 amendment, the older score chain,
post-2004 sentencing practice and Kurdish legal differences remain follow-up
work. Neither article nor this batch claims comprehensive national coverage.
