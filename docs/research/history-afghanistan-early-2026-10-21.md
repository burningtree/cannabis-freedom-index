## 1970: a hotel raid predates the supposed first ban

Read James Bradford's chapter in *Cannabis: Global Histories*, printed
pp. 194–196 and notes 41–51, from the retained chapter text
`tmp/global-histories/chapter_8.xhtml.txt`. It records a **16 May 1970**
raid on Kabul's Shak-Foladi Hotel: thirteen foreign nationals arrested,
100 grams of opium and 200 grams of hashish seized. These are the raid's
aggregate amounts, not an asserted amount per person. Bradford identifies
foreign involvement as what concerned American officials and records
a US narcotics agent being stationed in Kabul later in 1970.

Added an enforcement event and a 350–500-word article, tagged visitors
and enforcement. It gives no unverified sentence or court outcome. The
article places the incident alongside actual 1971 diplomatic discussion
and the later arrest campaign. The primary raid report cited through
Bradford's book has not been separately recovered; the source is plainly
identified as the historian's account.

## 1971: use the meeting date, and attribute the prohibition claim

Read the complete State Department FRUS document 350, including its
heading, participants and paragraphs 2–8:
https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1969-76ve07/d350.
Secretary William Rogers and foreign minister Musa Shafiq met in New York
on **13 October 1971**. The telegram was sent on 14 October. The new event
uses the meeting date.

Paragraphs 5–6 record discussion of young foreign visitors, weak enforcement,
and possible displacement of production after action in Turkey. Shafiq
said hashish and opium cultivation were prohibited under Islamic law but
not always controlled, especially in remote areas. Ambassador Malikyar
raised the need to replace poor poppy farmers' cash income. Earlier parts
of the same record describe drought, debt rescheduling and Afghanistan's
concern about Soviet financial influence. No aid-for-ban bargain is inferred.

Added a debate event, trafficking tag, and article. This records a political
commitment and discussion of existing restrictions, not a new operative
law. The article also uses FRUS document 372, read in full, for Shafiq's
late-1972 parliamentary programme. Any identification of the ambassador
beyond the source's 'Malikyar' was excluded.

The existing event records new anti-smuggling units and arrests, not a

## Recast 1973 as renewed prohibition, with the original order still missing

Read Bjelica and Foschini's 7 January 2019 Afghanistan Analysts Network
history, especially the subsection on cultivation from the 1970s onward.
It supports the retrospective claim of royal prohibition in 1973, but does
not supply the original order. Bradford and the contemporaneous 1971
record establish that cannabis restrictions and enforcement predated it.

Replaced the bare Wikipedia-based first-ban presentation with renewed
prohibition under American pressure, category tightening, retaining the
existing eradication tag and stable ID. Removed the unsupported implication
that this was the first law or that a specific US grant has been verified.
The $47-million story is repeated in later sources, but the underlying
grant or appropriation was not recovered and is not published as fact here.
Set `checked: false` until the original 1973 legal action is verified.
No article was built on the unresolved order.

## 1997: a reported Taliban radio ban fills part of the later gap

Located UN report **A/52/493**, dated 16 October 1997. Read the complete
indexed paragraph 130 on printed p. 33. It reports Radio Shariah announcing
in Kabul on **20 August** that production, sale and taking of heroin and
hashish were banned. It also records the Taliban representative in Islamabad
explaining the health/Islamic rationale and discussing alternative crops,
tools, seeds and fertilisers for poppy farmers.

Added a tightening entry, religion tag, explicitly unchecked and unscored.
The reported broadcast does not establish a nationwide legal change across
all competing authorities during civil war. Direct UN downloads produced
an empty file; Refworld's catalogue and viewer identified the same PDF,
but the PDF download returned 403. These failures were not treated as
having read the scan. The original radio broadcast/order and precise
scope of enforcement remain unrecovered, so no article yet.
