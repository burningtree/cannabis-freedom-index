## The 1901 law has exceptions, and the 1906 additions are marked

Read Chris S. Duvall, *The African Roots of Marijuana* (2019), printed
pp. 188–189 and notes 38–39 on pp. 315–316, from the retained book text
`tmp/book-intake/duvall.txt`. Duvall identifies 1901 contraband controls
and a 1907 hashish ordinance. The existing 1924 first-ban claim cannot
stand alongside that evidence.

Recovered the actual official revised ordinance collection from Internet
Archive, item `ordinancespromu00sudagoog`. Downloaded its PDF (222 pages),
OCR text and scan metadata. Visually inspected PDF 74–75, printed 62–63.
The collection was published in 1907, covering ordinances from 1899–1905
and showing subsequent revision annotations. This edition, not the
1901 original gazette itself, is the recovered primary text.

Contraband Goods Ordinance 1901, No. 13, was promulgated in Sudan
Gazette 31 on **1 January 1901**. Table item 6 expressly lists cannabis
indica, Indian hemp/hasheesh. It permits consignments to qualified
medical practitioners, licensed poison sellers and people specially
permitted by the governor-general. Section 1 provides forfeiture, possible
transport confiscation, and up to £E500 OR seven years OR both. Section 3
treats possession as evidence of assisting introduction/growth/manufacture,
subject to the holder showing lawful receipt without knowledge of
contraband status. Do not flatten this into prohibition without exceptions.

The text expressly marks italic wording as additions by Revision Ordinance
1906, Gazette 92 of **1 April 1906**. Those additions extend section 1 to
goods made/manufactured locally and the people involved in making them.
The manufacture extension must not be silently backdated to 1901.
Duvall's compressed statement of a full ban is less precise than the
recovered statute and was not used to erase its exceptions.

Added separate 1901 and 1906 entries, with an article on the import rule
and its development. Categories tightening, tags colonial/trade/sentencing
as relevant. The article's possession presumption comes directly from
section 3; it is not a claim that all consumption was expressly prohibited
by this ordinance. Do not confuse the separate tobacco proclamations on
printed pp. 64–65 with cannabis cultivation rules.

## The 1989 change was an amendment, signed on 27 July

Read the complete two-page indexed UN legislative reproduction,
E/NL.1990/12, communicated by Sudan. The source tool returns page 1
(title, commencement, replaced article 4(1)–(2)) and page 2 (remaining
article 4, replaced articles 7 and 8(2), signature). Direct PDF downloads
were empty, and docs.un.org returned HTML. Those response files are
retained; no scan verification is claimed for this source.

The text identifies **Amendment 1 of 1989** to the 1924 ordinance, not a
wholly new principal narcotics act. Commencement is on signature,
**27 July 1989**. Article 4(1)/(4)(a) permits death OR ten to twenty years
for its listed supply/cultivation/import/export/transport conduct.
Smoking, ordinary possession and smoking/preparation equipment receive
three to seven years under article 4(2)–(3)/(4)(b). Article 7 reaches
owners/managers/guards/responsible people of public cafés/restaurants who
commit, abet or permit a contravention, with death OR ten to twenty years.
It also permits licence withdrawal and closure up to a year.

Downloaded and read Amnesty International's 1995 report, printed p. 69,
to verify the coup context and cases. Its broad description of a new act
is refined by the actual legislative title. It reports Bane's September
1989 death sentence, Hamed's February 1990 hashish sentence and Gaballa's
December 1989 heroin execution. The article keeps those offences distinct.

Revised the existing event and article, preserving its stable ID and
death category. Added tags sentencing, trafficking, military and advertising;
the last tag follows the controlled vocabulary's inclusion of pipes and
paraphernalia, not a claim about commercial advertisements in this law.
