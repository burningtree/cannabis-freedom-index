## Licensed sales in 1939 were limited to Indians

UN E/CN.7/W.43, printed p. 99, reproduces Burma's 1946 report. Read the
complete indexed Burma paragraph, which expressly says the 1939 ganja
rules legalised sale **to Indians only**. Those rules remained in force
in 1946, but the government made no sales that year and issued no
cultivation licences to supply its ganja trade. The paragraph also
identifies illicit cultivation in the Pegu Yoma hills, 216 hemp-drug
prosecutions and 208 successful prosecutions. These are case counts,
not an asserted count of prisoners or exclusively trafficking cases.

The actual UN PDF returned 403; the 1939 rules themselves have not been
recovered. Corrected the overbroad legalisation description and title,
replaced the Wikipedia source with the precise primary-report locator,
and set `checked: false` pending full source verification. Tags colonial,
race and retail replace the unsupported tax tag. Added a separate 1946
enforcement entry, also unchecked. No claim of permanent shop closure,
repeal of the rules or a new 1946 ban. No article padded from this short
report. The earlier nationwide score vector is preserved but the actual
racially restricted scope needs a future historical-score audit.

## Original 1993 law: possession, growing, sale and registration differ

Downloaded the Myanmar Financial Intelligence Unit's actual MLIS English
text, ten pages, dated **27 January 1993**, retained as `law-1993.pdf`
and layout text. Read definitions, objectives, sections 9, 15–16, 19–20,
26(f), 28–31. Visually checked PDF 6 and 8, covering registration,
possession/growing and the production/sale/death penalty group.

Section 16: five to ten years plus an optional fine for unauthorised
possession, cultivation, transport/transfer and other specified conduct.
Section 26(f): **75 grams or more** of cannabis or cannabis essence,
combined where applicable, is deemed held/transported/transferred for sale.
Section 19: at least ten years to an unlimited period for possession for
sale and its other offences. Section 20: fifteen years to an unlimited
period OR death for production, distribution, sale and import/export.
Do not describe a quantity below 75 grams as legal or exempt from prison.

Section 15 imposes three to five years for failing to register for treatment
or comply with treatment directions. Sections 28–31 provide authorised
research/medical exceptions, preserve compatible earlier subordinate rules,
and repeal the 1974 law. Those general exceptions do not prove an operating
cannabis patient programme. Removed the old seed claim: silence in this
text is not enough for a statement about all seed regulation.

Rewrote the existing event, corrected its full date, replaced its source
and added a 410-word article. Category other: a replacement law with
several offence groups; no assumption that every penalty was newly raised.
Tags sentencing, thresholds, trafficking and treatment. Death is permitted,
but the source alone does not establish when it was first introduced or
changed relative to the amended predecessor, so no new death event.

## 2018 removed one prison offence but retained coercive treatment

Downloaded the actual eight-page MLIS amendment, Law 6/2018 dated
**14 February 2018**, retained as `amendment-2018.pdf` and text. Read the
complete amendment, especially sections 10, 18–20 and 25(b). Visually
checked PDF 4 (treatment/bond/six-month process) and 8 (100-gram threshold).

The old registration offence is deleted. Replacement section 9 allows
police referral for assessment and treatment, a court-ordered treatment
bond, and **six months** in a health/rehabilitation centre after refusal
to execute the bond or breach of a condition. New section 15 prescribes
**240–360 hours** of supervised unpaid community service at two hours
per day for breaches of centre instructions/discipline. Do not equate
abolition of registration imprisonment with fully voluntary care.

Section 25(b) substitutes **100 grams** for the former 75-gram cannabis
sale presumption. It leaves the ordinary possession offence intact.
Rewrote the existing easing event and added a 400-word article. Tags
thresholds, treatment, sentencing and police-powers. Added an explicit
unscored reason: this limited reform retains very severe possession
punishment and compulsory treatment; no whole-point score change assigned.
All earlier `before` vectors and current country scores are unchanged.

## The separate policy proposed voluntary treatment

Downloaded and read the Central Committee for Drug Abuse Control's
**20 February 2018** National Drug Control Policy from UNODC, retained
as `policy-2018.pdf` and text. Read development/consultation history,
printed pp. 9–10, treatment pp. 18–19 and human-rights pp. 24–25.
Visually checked PDF 23, printed p. 19, expressly proposing transformation
from compulsory to voluntary treatment. The policy also suggests treating
users as patients, harm reduction, proportionate responses and considering
repeal of drug-related death sentences.

Added a separate debate entry, tags treatment and sentencing. Policy
recommendations do not change the operative drug offences. The 2018
Transnational Institute commentary was read for context and identifies
this as the first national policy, but the actual amendment controls the
account of compulsory treatment and the actual policy controls its goals.

## Remaining larger gaps

Recovered the Library of Congress's two-page November 1994 report by Mya
Saw Shin, `loc-1974-law-report.pdf` and text. It describes the **1974 law
as amended in 1983 and 1988**, with five-to-ten-year and heavier/capital
offence groups. Do not backdate that consolidated account's full penalties
to 1974 or treat it as the exact content of the 1983/1988 amendments.
Those original amendment texts remain missing.

The 1974 original is catalogued in UN E/NL.1974/17; implementing rules
in E/NL.1975/48. The latter is dated 20 February 1974 despite its UN
circulation in 1976. The existing 1974 entry still needs comparison with
the original, especially its claim of a general re-ban after a limited
1939 Indian-sales scheme. Rules in 1995 and amendments announced in
2023/2024 are further leads, not yet researched changes. No present-law
claim follows from an official announcement alone.
